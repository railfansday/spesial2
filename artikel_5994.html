Boston-Bound British Airways 787-10 Declares Emergency Over Second Oil Leak In 10 Days

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

This article was updated on Thursday, November 26, 2026, to include a statement from British Airways and the aircraft's current status. It was originally published on Wednesday, November 25, 2026.

A British Airways Boeing 787-10 Dreamliner declared an emergency over the North Atlantic on Tuesday night after reporting an engine oil leak. The aircraft (G-ZBLC) — powered by two Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 engines — was operating flight BA239 from London Heathrow Airport (LHR) when it squawked 7700 more than four hours into the flight.

Despite a recommendation by the airline's operations team to divert to Iceland, the pilots managed to continue to Boston Logan International Airport (BOS), where the plane landed safely. The incident comes just ten days after the same aircraft shut down an engine over the Mediterranean and diverted to Athens. Tuesday's flight to Boston was the airframe's first flight back in service since the Athens diversion.

Flight tracking data shows that BA239 departed Heathrow just after 7:30 PM local time on Tuesday evening, and was over four hours into its roughly eight-hour journey to Boston when the pilots squawked 7700, indicating a general emergency. The Boeing 787-10 was cruising at around 33,000 feet and traveling at 378 knots when it declared the emergency.

Declared an emergency after an engine oil leak. Landed safely in Boston.

Air traffic control recordings reveal that the pilots stated the problem was due to an engine oil leak. The pilots told Gander Air Traffic Control in Canada that the jet was losing oil when the engines were running at high power, and once they lowered the thrust, the oil leak stopped getting worse. The crew then descended to 28,000 feet and kept the engines at lower power for the remainder of the flight.

As reported by X account Flightdrama, ACARS messages during the emergency show that British Airways' maintenance had recommended a diversion to Keflavik (KEF) in Iceland. The messages also indicated that the same jet had previously experienced an oil leak issue, in reference to a diversion incident just ten days prior to Tuesday night's incident.

BA239 ultimately landed safely in Boston at 10:55 PM EST, around an hour after its scheduled 9:50 PM arrival. Flying lower and slower than planned (at under 400 knots, compared with a typical cruise speed close to 500 knots) added time to the journey. It remains to be seen whether authorities will open an investigation into the emergency.

The aircraft involved, G-ZBLC, experienced an engine shutdown emergency on November 14, while it was operating BA105 from Heathrow to Dubai International Airport (DXB). The widebody was roughly three hours into the flight when the crew shut down the right-hand engine and diverted to Athens (ATH).

According to The Aviation Herald, a passenger on the flight reported that the pilots announced the cause of the diversion as an oil leak, although this has not been confirmed by the airline. However, ACARS messages during Tuesday night's incident cited a previous oil leak during a diversion, suggesting this did happen.

Unlike on BA105, Tuesday's pilots did not shut down an engine, instead managing the problem by reducing thrust. Tracking data shows the aircraft remained on the ground in Athens for six days, until it was repositioned back to Heathrow on November 20. It then stayed in London for another four days before re-entering service on Tuesday with the flight to Boston.

The 787 and A350 are becoming even more competitive with one another.

G-ZBLC first flew in September 2020 but was not delivered to British Airways until December 2022, after being caught up in Boeing's prolonged pause in 787 deliveries over production quality issues. It operated its first revenue flight on December 23, 2022, to Denver International Airport (DEN).

BA's 787-10s can seat up to 256 passengers across four cabins and operate long-haul routes to destinations including Boston, Newark, Seattle, Nairobi, Lagos, and Dubai. With a total of 44 Dreamliners in its fleet, the carrier is one of the few airlines globally to operate all three variants, namely the 787-8, 787-9, and 787-10.

Another of the airline's 787-10s was involved in a mishap this month after getting stuck in the mud at Heathrow ahead of a flight to Lagos. The jet was taxiing towards the runway when it failed to complete a left turn and ran onto the grass verge, with its nose landing gear sinking into the ground.

The aircraft remains out of service in Boston since Tuesday's incident. The aircraft was scheduled to return to Heathrow as BA238 at 7:30 AM on Wednesday, less than nine hours after arriving, but the flight was canceled. British Airways has also commented on the incident. In a statement to Simple Flying, a spokesperson for the airline said:

"The aircraft arrived safely in Boston and customers disembarked as normal. Safety is always our highest priority."

The airline said the flight crew identified a "minor technical issue" and followed established procedures. It also apologized to passengers for their late arrival in Boston. However, the carrier did not offer any details on what caused the oil leak, or whether the problem was linked to the one that forced the Athens diversion ten days earlier.
