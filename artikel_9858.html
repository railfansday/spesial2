Is AI a bubble? Yes and no, and the difference matters to you

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Everyone is asking whether AI is a bubble. Investors ask it about stocks. Founders ask it about funding. Students ask it about careers. They are asking three different questions with the same word, and that is why the debate goes in circles.

The numbers are enormous. Analysts' estimates for what the largest cloud companies will spend on capital expenditure in 2026, mostly on AI data centres and chips, range from roughly $660 billion to $800 billion, depending on the tracker (Futurum Group estimate via banthebots.org, AL Capital Advisory). Critics point to rising debt, to companies funding each other's demand, and to revenues that have not yet caught up with the spending (Man Group).

Even people building AI have said it out loud. In August 2025, OpenAI's chief executive said investors as a whole were "overexcited" about AI. And in the same month, an MIT report on enterprise generative AI pilots found that most of them were not producing measurable returns yet.

So: could AI stock prices and private valuations fall hard? Yes. Very possibly. That is what bubbles look like from the inside.

Here history is useful. Between March 2000 and October 2002, the Nasdaq lost about three quarters of its value. Thousands of internet companies died.

The fibre laid during the bubble was used for the next twenty years. The survivors of the crash became the largest companies on Earth. A bubble is often how a society overpays for infrastructure it will need anyway.

I see the same pattern every day in my own work. I use AI to draft, to review code, to translate between Arabic and English, to explain errors. Some of that value is real and permanent, and it does not disappear if a stock falls 50%.

So: will the technology vanish? No. But a lot of what is being sold as "AI" will.

This is the question that actually matters to most readers, and it is the one nobody asks.

And your plan is not a bubble if it rests on things that survive a crash: understanding real users, owning a hard problem, data you are allowed to use, and skills like design, judgement and communication.

When I built Rafu, a stock and credit book for small shops, I deliberately made it work with no internet and no cloud AI service. Not because AI is bad, but because a shop owner's records should not depend on anyone's funding round.
