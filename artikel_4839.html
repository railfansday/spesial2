Why We Can’t Just Stop Feeling Guilty

It’s something I come across in the consulting room all the time. A client has come to therapy to stop doing something she knows is not serving her. “I need to stop taking her calls”; “I need to stop always picking up after him”; “I need to stop neglecting myself.” So what gets in the way? This is a question I ask a lot. And more often than not, the answer is guilt: “I just feel too guilty when I don’t do it.” And after some probing, the story often goes something like this: If I don’t do what my guilt tells me to do, I’m a bad person, a bad mum, a bad friend, a bad daughter. I’m not who I want to be. If I got rid of my guilt, I wouldn’t know who I am anymore. I’m always the one who takes care of others and fixes things. What if I let go of my guilt and I become lazy and selfish?

I’ve changed my approach to guilt in therapy, and it’s still evolving, but It’s at this point that I used to fall back onto my trusted therapy tools. If this is a belief that is not serving my client, the next logical step is to find a way to challenge it. The problem is that most of my clients know that logically, rationally, they need to stop taking care of everyone else. They know that they need to set boundaries. They even know that they are not, in fact, selfish or lazy because they have mountains of evidence to the contrary.

So why can you not just stop feeling guilty? The question is, in many ways, as nonsensical as asking why we cannot stop feeling anxious or sad or angry. Yet somehow, we have constructed this idea in popular psychology that guilt is something inconvenient we need to let go of. What if we start by trying to understand why you might feel guilty in the first place? Just as anxiety is an emotion that we can never seem to get rid of, it might be that feeling guilty serves a function in our lives that we need to understand. Only then can we find ways to accept that this uncomfortable feeling is unavoidable but does not need to define who we are.

Guilt in itself is what researchers have called a “prosocial” emotion—that is to say, guilt will often motivate us to repair relationships when we have done something wrong. In its purest form, guilt supports cooperation in our social groups. But, just like anxiety can spiral out of control, overinflated guilt that stops us from looking after ourselves has moved far beyond its prosocial utility.

What makes guilt spiral out of control? Researchers suggest that what my clients might be suffering from is “omnipotent responsibility guilt.” The origins of this particular type of guilt feeling can be traced back to our earliest relationships. If a child grows up in a relationship with a parent who is unpredictable or whose feelings are uncontained, the child can learn that it is safer to take responsibility for the adult’s feelings and try to fix them. Children are in a precariously vulnerable state—dependent on their caregiver for safety, regardless of how emotionally safe that caregiver might be.

To keep that caregiver safe in the child’s mind, it can be easier to construct an internal model in which the responsibility for the parent’s happiness is with the child, and the child is to blame if the parent is unhappy. The child continuously fears and predicts relational rupture, and that prediction model grows into adulthood, where it becomes the foundation of an identity in which everyone else’s feelings are more important than our own, and we feel selfish for looking after ourselves.

So, what my therapy tools did not take into consideration, then, is that guilt can be linked to a deep sense of relational danger. Because the story, in fact, goes something like this: If I don’t do what my guilt tells me to do, I’m a bad person, a bad mum, a bad friend, a bad daughter. But if it’s my fault, at least that means I can make it better. And that feels a lot safer than the alternative. Because if I’m not the one who has done something wrong, that means the ones who were meant to keep me safe might not be safe after all. And then the whole world stops being safe.

It can be difficult to make the leap from our present feelings of guilt to our earliest experiences. But the intensity with which many of my clients hold on to their guilt suggests that it fulfills a deep function that feels like survival. By the time we are adults, a tendency to accept responsibility for others’ feelings may have become fiercely intertwined with who we believe we are. We protect ourselves by telling ourselves we are lazy or selfish when we veer off course because, for many of my clients, guilt has become the thing that keeps their relationships, and with that their sense of self, alive.

Tracing this back opens the doors to new therapy tools that can slowly address the impasse so many of my clients can find themselves in:

The reason we cannot just stop feeling guilty is because our feelings are there for a reason. We may not objectively have done anything wrong, but our guilt feelings might still serve a function, even an outdated one that nevertheless keeps us feeling safe. Remember that it is OK to set boundaries, and it is also OK to find it hard. Be kind to yourself and don’t embark on this by yourself. We are social beings who learn safety in relationship, and we need each other’s company to do that.
