Vegas is the center of the sports world right now. Why is everyone so mad about it?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Las Vegas often finds itself at the center of the sports universe, but the last two weeks of November have become among the most vital on Southern Nevada's civic calendar with the presence of F1 and college basketball's Players Era Festival both being held on the Strip.

On Saturday night, Red Bull's Max Verstappen won his third Las Vegas Grand Prix in four years, claiming a race that's gotten consensus rave reviews and is being hailed as one of the sport's all-time classics. Verstappen, ever the showman, followed it up with a hilarious tirade against rival George Russell, who dared to engage in a war of words with motorsport's pettiest man.

Exactly 48 hours before lights went out at the Grand Prix, Houston and Kansas tipped off in the championship game of the Players Era 8, the first of two college hoops "festivals" occurring before and during the patented "Feast Week". Houston held on for a 86-76 win, and it was an especially meaningful result because it was the culmination of a career defining week for Cougars point guard and Las Vegas native Dedan Thomas Jr., who became the city's most popular hooper in a generation during his time at Liberty and the two years he spent at UNLV.

The Players Era 16 is set to tip-off on Tuesday, featuring twice as many teams as the Players Era 8 did (yes, that's what the numbers mean). This week's bracket doesn't feature the at-the-time top ranked team in Florida and the top-ranked prospect in Kansas' Tyran Stokes like last week's did, but it's still an exceptionally strong field with contenders like Gonzaga, Louisville, St. John's and Texas Tech.

Like most things that have to do with the City of Sin, however, both the Grand Prix and the hoops tournament have taken their fair share of criticism in the short time they've been present. We could talk for years about the saga F1 officials have put Las Vegas locals through since 2023, from the lost profits blocked businesses suffer during construction and race weekend, to the Los Angeles-esque traffic on the Strip when the streets are shut down. Among F1's global fanbase and even its drivers, the race is viewed as much as a PR event as it is a motorsports contest, although the quality of racing has removed all doubt that it's ultimately a net positive.

The Players Era discourse has occurred strictly on a national scale, as the tournament has been getting pelted with proverbial tomatoes because it's seen by fans as yet another relic of what feels more and more like the twilight of collegiate sports. The Maui Invitational and other mainstays of Feast Week have been stripped of relevance because most of the country's elite teams head to Las Vegas during these few weeks to compete for NIL money in front of mostly empty arenas.

The tie that binds these two events together, aside from their shared time in public consciousness, is the fact that both events have taken what is probably an unfair reputational hit because the general public opinion of Las Vegas among sports fans is probably as low as it's ever been.

Football and baseball die-hards have it out for Vegas because they took two beloved teams right from under Oakland's nose. Hockey fans have it out for Vegas because Golden Knights fans have been tabbed as the league's black sheep fanbase since the team's inception and the franchise has been riddled with controversy off-the-ice. Wrestling fans have it out Vegas because we took WrestleMania 41 out of Minneapolis' hands. Soccer fans are about to have it out for Vegas if we move the Whitecaps out of their native Vancouver. I'm sure I could list more if I dedicated myself to it.

Taken together, the general stance is largely the same. People see that Vegas is essentially buying its way to becoming a sports mecca, regardless of what it costs morally and reputationally. Society views Vegas almost as a caricature of American consumer capitalism, and in today's political and economic climate that's a distinctly bad label to have.

These opinions were noticeable for me when covering the Grand Prix as a national reporter the last few years, a role in which I had to divorce myself from the local opinions and civic inconvenience to cover the on-track action. With that came seeking out opinions from national and international fans, who were still scoffing at the spectacle of it all and were nowhere near considering it an essential stop, much less one worth taking other fan-favorite tracks off the schedule for. It's similar to how college hoops fans view Players Era in relation to the traditional Feast Week events.

At the end of the day, the only way the hearts and minds of fans will change is if Vegas hosts moments that last longer than the fickleness of public opinion. Fans across the world have a newfound appreciation for the spectacle that is the Las Vegas Grand Prix after seeing the thrilling racing it could produce on Saturday. Dedan Thomas Jr.'s heroic hometown run was another moment that registered on a national scale, but it probably would have been appreciated more if it was a different event. Part of it is luck, but part of it is also creating hype, fostering it and building an atmosphere people want to join instead of deride.

The good news is, I believe we're well on the way. And it's just part of our natural evolution as a sports city. Not only have we been the new kid in school, but we're the really attractive and rich new kid that immediately started hitting on everyone's girlfriend and did so with reasonable success. It makes sense we haven't gotten invited to many parties yet, but people will realize we can probably be chill if they give us a chance. These two weeks are a start.
