The 8 Best Cordless Leaf Blowers for Cleanups, Yardwork, and Other Small Jobs

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

We all know leaf blowers are a lawn care essential in the fall, but they're handy the rest of the year, too.

As the end of the year is lurking around the corner, many of us are trying to manage our fall leaves, a job that is made much easier with a leaf blower. These versatile tools, in addition to moving fall foliage, can do much more, including cleaning grass clippings after you mow, unclogging your gutters, clearing sawdust out of the garage, or blowing away other debris.

The world of leaf blowers has changed dramatically in recent years. Cordless, battery-powered blowers, once looked down on for power, have closed the performance gap with those mighty gas-powered models. High-end cordless blowers now offer airspeed and volume comparable to gas, minus the maintenance and fumes. That, combined with a growing number of legal limitations on gas-powered lawn tools, as well as annoyance with gas leaf blower noise in particular, means cordless leaf blowers are becoming the overwhelming favorite for homeowners looking to balance power, price, and ease of use.

Evaluations of these blowers were done in several batches, allowing our reviewers, Test Editor Brad Ford and contributor Dan Stout, to focus on specific models while also seeing how they compared head-to-head.

Stout tested his blowers, including the Husqvarna, Kress, Bosch, Ego, DeWalt, and Craftsman, by running them through basic tasks—clearing leaves from lawns and flower beds and moving grass clippings off driveways. He used them in a variety of weather conditions, clearing freshly cut grass on sunny days, but also immediately after heavy rains when he wanted to remove tree debris that clung to every wet surface. He also used the blowers to dislodge older, decaying leaves in overgrown areas, as well as blow sawdust out of a garage workshop.

His tests grew more difficult and involved climbing on a roof to clean out gutters, clearing ponded water, moving gravel across concrete, and clearing caked-on mud off a concrete pad. He also tested the blowers’ control by clearing a paved walkway that ran between two mulched beds, and tracking whether the mulch was disturbed.

To measure continuous runtime, he set the machines to maximum standard power and ran them until the battery died. By default, he did not use any ‘turbo’ or ‘overdrive’ for this test. Any blower runtimes tested using turbo are noted in the specs. When considering runtime, keep in mind that you'll likely be using the blower in short spurts, so 20 minutes spent clearing leaves might only consume 12 minutes of actual runtime.

In addition to using them himself, Stout asked several volunteers to use the blowers so that he could understand how body type and experience level impacted the user experience.

Ford tested using diagnostic tools such as the MGL Avionics Stratomaster Vega airspeed indicator, along with a custom-built testing apparatus to gauge airspeed. He blew leaves and hay off of grass, comparing the results to a small gas-engine leaf blower. He measured continuous runtime with the machines set to maximum power, and used a sawdust erosion test created by the Popular Mechanics test team. For this test, a large area of pavement was covered with a thin layer of sawdust, then the leaf blowers were set horizontally over the pavement and turned on, leaving an impression of the shape of the airstream.

Any leaf blowers not tested are included because of their strong specs in areas such as airspeed and cubic feet per minute. Brands that we’ve used and come to trust for power tools were prioritized. All models had their sound levels measured at both the operator’s position and at 75 feet away (to simulate the noise levels your neighbors might encounter). Our combined efforts have resulted in this compilation of the best cordless leaf blowers available to buy now.

Ryobi’s 40V HP Whisper Series Leaf Blower had to perform on multiple levels to come out on top. It recorded some of the highest numbers of any handheld blower for airspeed and cubic feet per minute in our testing.

Those numbers helped the 40V HP Whisper Series blow last year’s leaves out of dense weeds and brush along the property borders of our test area. With an air volume of 587 cubic feet per minute, we were able to stand in one place to blow a wall of leaves up to 25 feet away.

The combination of high airspeed and volume also made the blower great at clearing debris off hard surfaces like patios, driveways, or the garage floor. While it is plenty strong to use on gutters, its size makes it difficult to handle on a ladder. We recommend using a blower of this size on a roof with a gentle slope for gutter cleaning.

The 40V HP Whisper Series blower is well balanced, making it generally easy to use, although some wrist fatigue may set in on longer blowing sessions.

Even with its smaller battery, the Echo DPB-2500 has a runtime more than 25 percent longer than that of the next best handheld blower we tested—at a discount of $30 or more. With lower airspeed and volume numbers than some of the more expensive models on this list, the blower is still quite powerful, able to move most leaves and debris, though you may need more time to work with heavy layers of wet leaves.

A test at full consistent speed gave this blower a runtime of 23 minutes, but in real-world testing, Reviews Editor David Dritsas used it for just over an hour while he was cleaning off a patio and clearing some grass clippings after a session of mowing a backyard.

He also liked the comfort of the DPB-2500. Besides being lightweight at 9.5 pounds (even with the battery), there’s also a rubberized grip around the handle that didn’t leave his hand feeling numb the way some vibrating power tools can make it feel after long periods.

One feature he thought was lacking was a locking lever for the variable-speed control. While this worked well in locking the trigger in place, Dritsas found changing speeds on the fly was awkward because he had to adjust the switch and the trigger at the same time. Regardless, he really liked this leaf blower, especially for its long runtime.

Echo’s DPB-5800T backpack blower is a pro-level powerhouse, boasting an airspeed of 148 mph and an air volume of 608 cfm. It houses two 5-amp-hour batteries, giving it a runtime of 24 minutes at max power—and if that’s not enough, there is room for a third battery. Of course, you’ll rarely be blowing leaves at full power the entire time—we were able to work for nearly an hour before exhausting the batteries.

Test Editor Bradley Ford also tested this blower and found that the combination of airspeed and volume, plus a long adjustable tube, makes it quite effective at clearing heavy leaf cover. It can roll a layer of leaves back and blow them up to 30 feet in some conditions and clear 10-foot strips with each pass. And it effortlessly blew grass clippings and debris from a driveway.

Make no mistake, this is a pro-level machine, basically the less expensive version of Echo’s DPB-5800TD2 backpack blower, whose only difference is that it comes with two 8-amp-hour batteries instead of three. It’s well-built, with a rigid backpack frame and a wide, stable base, which makes the blower easy to stand up on a table to put it on. And though we’d like to see a padded hip strap, the shoulder and chest straps do a decent job of keeping everything in place and make the job of clearing the leaves comfortable.

Ryobi’s 40V HP Brushless Whisper Series Backpack has almost identical performance to its handheld model we tested—our top pick in this roundup. The one difference? Runtime. With two batteries, the backpack version of this blower ran nearly 10 minutes longer. So, like the handheld version, the Whisper Series Backpack worked well blowing leaves from the lawn, brush, and bushes. Likewise, it easily blows debris from hard surfaces like driveways.

There are two differences to note in practical use. First, this backpack version is better suited for longer leaf-blowing sessions in larger areas. Carrying the weight—even though it is light—on your back takes a lot of strain off your wrist and arm. Second, the backpack can make maneuvering in tighter spaces more difficult because it’s bulkier, hanging off your back.

Deputy Editor Colin Aylesworth also tested this leaf blower and noted that it can take time to get used to a backpack blower, noting the limited range of motion of the blower nozzle compared with a handheld blower. When blowing grass from the driveway, he found himself backing up or sliding sideways in order to keep air moving where he needed it to go. That can feel a bit awkward, but is typical of backpack blowers.

At the end of the day, this Ryobi backpack blower is well suited to homeowners with large properties. The variable-speed trigger allows you to adjust the airspeed to low levels to gently clean off areas with loose stones, but it also has plenty of power and runtime for removing a lot of leaves from wide-open spaces.

Bosch’s Profactor 18V Blower is ideal for smaller jobs. With less airspeed and volume than some of the larger models we tested, it’s not effective at moving large loads of leaves, but it will help clear light leaf cover from smaller yards. Plus, its small size and lower weight make it more manageable to handle.

Our reviewers found it easy to use on the porch, reaching behind furniture to clear out leaves that had settled in hard-to-reach places. It was also useful in blowing leaves off hanging plants and out of decorative planters. And, if you need to blow debris off the garage floor, the Profactor is a handy way to get the job done quickly.

Just don’t expect too much runtime. During a high-speed test, it only lasted 13 minutes, and during a medium-speed test, it only lasted 25 minutes. That’s half the time you’d find with more powerful leaf blowers. However, if you already have some 18-volt Bosch power tools, you could extend that runtime with those same batteries.

One great feature this blower offers is an independent throttle. Instead of controlling the airspeed with the trigger, there’s a separate dial placed where your thumb rests as you hold the blower. That means you can lock the blower into the on position, but change the speed quickly and easily as you go—no more worrying about the pressure you put on the trigger. It’s a small feature, but one that makes this small blower stand out in the crowd.

The lightest and most compact model we tested, Milwaukee’s M18 Handheld Blower, can certainly blow leaves, but it really shines for cleanup tasks around the house.

Senior Reviews Editor Jamie Scorcher tried it for smaller jobs at her home, like blowing debris off the patio and cleaning up after cutting fallen tree limbs. Its smaller size makes it easier to hold and manipulate in small spaces, or from a ladder, where we used it to blow debris out of the gutters.

Scorcher was impressed with how light the model is—only 4.9 pounds with the battery—and how quiet it ran, only 62 decibels, which is roughly as loud as a normal conversation. But it was also powerful enough to get her tasks completed. In our tests, we found a real-world air volume of 376 cfm and an airspeed of 87 mph. Runtime is a fairly decent 21 minutes, which got me through most of the job without a recharge. She found the runtime was around 21 minutes on a single charge.

Work was made easier, too, by Milwaukee’s lock-on button, which locks the trigger into an always-on position. This allows you to maintain the machine at full throttle without having to continuously hold the trigger.

While this Milwaukee blower kit is $329, if you’re already on their M18 tool platform, you can get it for $150 as a tool only, with no battery. We found it perfect for quick cleanups and a handy tool for light indoor jobs like cleaning out the garage or even a quick hack for removing dirt from the trunk of your car.

Test Editor Paul Smith still loves his gas-powered leaf blower, but he was pleasantly surprised by the performance of the Makita GBU04 Backpack Leaf Blower after he ran it through testing. He noted that it feels relatively light and maneuverable on your back while producing a serious blast of air. Makita rates the output at 675 cfm with airspeed up to 215 mph. At full throttle, Smith felt enough kick that he had to clamp down on both the handle and trigger for a firmer grip.

It’s not hard to regulate that power, though. You can drop the speed at the touch of a button.

The GBU04 has two speed modes with a variable-speed trigger, so you can back off when full power is unnecessary, like when you don’t want to blow mulch from a garden bed that you are clearing leaves from.

This blower runs on two 4-amp-hour, 40-volt XGT batteries, but the blower taps their power sequentially, using just one and then switching over to the other when the first runs low. That could present some performance issues. Some owners have reported that the machine’s blowing power starts to wane as the active battery’s charge drops. That could present a problem if you are working on a large yard and using high power a lot. As the first battery hits low levels, it could cause a drop in power before switching over to the second.

That potential issue aside, the GBU04 is a highly capable, quiet, and lightweight machine that’s well-suited for someone with just enough yard to justify wearing a backpack blower. There’s ample power to move a decent pile of leaves, but it’s easy to attune when you’re working in more sensitive areas. If you already own Makita 40-volt tools, you save money by purchasing it as a tool-only.

With a large 7.5 Ah battery, the Husqvarna 350iB delivered close to 15 minutes of runtime at max power—and up to 30 minutes at lower settings. Unlike other models we tested, it comes with two tips: one with a smaller opening, which yielded a higher airspeed—146 mph—and more precision, and a larger one that provided greater air volume at 675 cfm.

The higher CFM with the large tip worked well, moving more leaves farther. This helped make the 350iB one of the more effective handheld machines where blowing leaves in the yard is concerned. We confirmed this in our practical testing, moving heavier leaf cover comparably to some of the backpack blowers tested. The 350iB was equally exceptional at freeing leaves from tangled weeds and brush.

Using the smaller tip, we were able to blow leaves with more precision, getting closer to flowerbeds or mulch with minimal disturbance. It also worked well at attacking cobwebs along the eaves and beneath gutters. And, speaking of gutters, the 350iB is well-suited for clearing them, but using it from a ladder is awkward.

Leaf blowers, as you might expect, drain batteries quickly. Depending on the size of your yard and the amount of leaf cover, it’s likely you'll run out of charge before the job’s done. To double your runtime, pick up a second battery and invest in one with a higher amp-hour rating. A 5.0 Ah battery will typically power a blower twice as long as a 2.5 Ah battery.

If you have more than a couple of acres to tackle, consider a backpack-style blower. Backpacks distribute the weight of stronger, heavier batteries across your body, and many of these designs allow for storing a spare battery for easy swap-out.

Leaf blower performance is described in terms of airspeed (mph) and volume (cubic feet per minute, or cfm). Airspeed is a measure of how much force the blower applies while cfm describes how much air it moves. A higher cfm is needed for moving lots of small, easy-to-move items (dry leaves over a driveway) while higher mph is required to move larger or heavy items (wet leaves across tall grass).

It’s worth noting the advertised airspeed and cfm of a leaf blower are the maximum output in a best-case scenario. But, as we all know, real-world situations are far from ideal. That is precisely why we put these leaf blowers through a testing process to determine which ones perform, regardless of their advertised specs.

As a rule of thumb, any blower with at least 110-mph airspeed and 300 cfm should be suitable for light lawn cleanup. Blowers with 120 mph airspeed and cfm from 400 to 650 are able to handle most homeowners’ needs. This range is powerful enough for a blower to move leaves and debris easily while still keeping its weight down and cost low. Anything over 150 mph or 650 cfm is appropriate for heavier tasks like moving heavier, wet leaves, blowing debris from second-story rafters, or clearing gutters.

The speed setting on a leaf blower determines the air volume and velocity it produces. The more powerful the blower, the more important variable speed becomes. Otherwise, it’s too easy to blow away mulch or topple plants when you try to clear a sidewalk. Variable speed may be set with a trigger or a dial, depending on the blower.

Using lower settings preserves battery life and allows for more precision. Go with low speed when blowing leaves off flowerbeds or clearing dust from a garage. Move up to a higher speed when blowing wet leaves, clearing debris, or for a quick sweep of larger areas. Some models have turbo boost modes, helpful for stuck-on debris like wet leaves, or when trying to clear areas just out of reach.

For electric models, very little. The most important maintenance is making sure that the intakes are clear. I find a leaf or clump of grass sticking to the intakes when I’m using a leaf blower for any length of time. Simply turn off the blower, pull away the obstruction, and you’re back in business.

Gas powered models have the added issue of engine maintenance. Depending on the model, you can expect to deal with oil, spark plugs, and filters. You can take it to a lawncare center for a maintenance session, or tackle it as a DIY project. If you’re comfortable performing maintenance on a lawn mower or other small engine, you can upkeep a gas-powered leaf blower with no problem.

There are really just two things that will wear out on a cordless blower: the blower motor itself and the battery. It will depend on usage, but you should get at least three years of use before seeing any degradation battery capacity. The motor itself should last 10 years or so. If working life is a concern, be sure to check the blower warranty for details.
