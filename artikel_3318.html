'SilverFin's Full-Cast Audiobook Is an Immersive Look at the Boy Who Would Be Bond | Review

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

There’s officially a new James Bond on the big screen in the shape of Robert Pattinson, which means there’s a whole new 007 to spend the next few years speculating about. There’s also a new Bond to listen to right now, and he’s still a schoolboy. The fun of Big Finish’s SilverFin is hearing how much of the iconic spy is already there at thirteen, although it's fair to say the production is in absolutely no rush to introduce us to him.

In fact, it spends nearly ten minutes with two children, Alfie and Jeannie, on a fishing trip that will initially have you thinking, "where's the Bond boy?" However, Joe Kraemer’s score and Benji Clifford’s sound design are so striking that they give the loch just enough time to go from peaceful to ominous. Then there's an attack, and then something attacks, the new theme kicks in, and suddenly it sounds like Bond.

Isaac Rouse (The Crown) plays James in the full-cast audio adaptation of SilverFin, the first novel in Charlie Higson’s Young Bond series, produced by Big Finish and Ian Fleming Publications. Higson takes Bond back to the 1930s, when he’s a new boy at Eton. Instead of global annihilation, his biggest problem here is a bully with a rich father. Imagine. Then James goes to Scotland for the holidays, the children’s disappearance becomes his first real mystery, and it's not the bully that's the big problem; it's the rich daddy.

The remainder of the cast includes Charlotte McBurney as Wilder Lawless, Charlie Higson as Michael Merriot, Tim McInnerny as Max Bond, Miranda Raison as Charmian Bond, Miriam Margolyes as Cleek MacSawney, William Hope as Lord Randolph Hellebore, Christopher Nathan as George Hellebore, Paterson Joseph as Dr. Perseus Friend, Harry Marcus as Red Kelly, Lucy Fleming as Madame Ouspenskaya, Timothy Bentinck as Sergeant White, and Penelope Rawlins as Alfie Kelly.

Spanning 8 hours across 16 episodes, the adaptation has been helmed by writer and director Barnaby Edwards, and he wisely makes the decision to give James time to settle into both worlds. After all, there's an entire school sporting competition to get through, and at one point Uncle Max explains how a Rolls-Royce engine works in frankly ridiculous detail. The scene has every right to be boring, and yet, it’s fascinating, partly because Max takes such pleasure in explaining it and partly because James perks up once he realizes how many little explosions it takes to make a car go. That’s SilverFin at its best. It gives its characters time to enjoy one another’s company, which makes it hurt all the more when things go horribly wrong.

Now, Rouse is a hard sell as a 13-year-old boy, and even more so when the story flashes back to an even younger James. Yet, by the end of the Hellebore Cup, you cannot imagine anyone else doing the role. The things that pop out instantly are the charm, that basically obnoxious and undeserved confidence he has at that age, and the way he's still figuring out how far a witty line can get him.

Rishi Manuel’s Pritpal Nandra gives the Eton episodes their warmth. He explains the school to James and the listener in one go, in a moment that actually feels very "Basil Exposition," if you want to feel extra Bond-meta. Their teasing feels like two boys actually becoming friends, and there’s room for another episode of that before the Hellebore Cup takes over. After two episodes of George Hellebore being an absolute Malfoy of a boy, hearing James overtake him is wildly satisfying. Then George has to face his father, and suddenly third place feels like the worst thing that could have happened to him.

Perhaps the heart of the Scottish scenes is when James and his Uncle Max go fishing, something you wouldn't think could be that compelling in an audio drama — yet, it is. McInnerny is just great here, and he brings that same easy charm and warmth to the scene where Max tells James about working as a spy during the war, as well as how he got the limp he came home with. Miranda Raison’s Aunt Charmian makes it easy to see why coming back to this family matters to James, especially after the surprisingly affecting flashbacks to his parents.

Red Kelly’s return is a delight, and Wilder Lawless makes such an immediate impression that it’s a shame she takes more than three hours to turn up. And then there’s Meatpacker Moran, a name that deserves to go down in history, whom Stuart Milligan makes unexpectedly good company. The first half’s finale spends most of its time following him, James, and Red around the outside of Castle Hellebore. Clifford’s sound design makes the grounds easy to picture, so there’s time to appreciate the sheer scale of the place and how much trouble the boys will be in once they go inside.

William Hope makes Lord Randolph Hellebore vile from the moment he opens his mouth, and Miriam Margolyes makes his gamekeeper frightening enough to carry a scene on her own. Even in that company, Paterson Joseph is something else as Doctor Perseus Friend. He talks to George about eugenics and mass death like they've just caught up for coffee in Cafe Nervosa in Seattle. Later, he manages to make the simple prospect of watching someone eat sound deeply sinister. A plan to create super-soldiers using eels sounds gloriously daft on paper, but Joseph makes it disturbing by playing Friend as a man who’s found an excuse to do exactly what he wants to do.

The laboratory episodes spend a long time explaining what Hellebore is doing, and by the later chapters a few of Kraemer’s cues have become a little too familiar. “The Future of Warfare” still lands hard enough to make a break between episodes feel welcome. Edwards gives away very little about what happened to Alfie and Jeannie. Those first ten minutes on the loch do a lot of the work. By the time James and George start smashing up the laboratory in “Hellfire,” it’s a punch-the-air moment. George’s change of heart happens quickly, but Christopher Nathan has been letting the fear slip through his arrogance since Eton.

And then there’s Uncle Max. The series gives plenty of reason to suspect he’ll die, and his letter is still an absolute gut punch. He’s reminding James how to start the car one moment and telling him how much those last few days together meant to him the next. Then Charmian says, “How lucky we are,” even as she’s grieving him. It’s a lovely way to end their time in Scotland.

SilverFin is an enjoyable, impressively atmospheric start for Big Finish’s Young Bond, but its eight-hour runtime gives the uneven pacing plenty of room to show. The school adventures and quieter family scenes build an appealing world around James, but lengthy laboratory explanations slow the story just when it should be gathering momentum. The irony is that, in reviewing this audio adaptation, the strongest praise goes to the performances, direction, and sound design, while the biggest frustrations lie with a story that’s been around for two decades and is showing its creaks. However, Isaac Rouse’s charm, a strong supporting cast, and Benji Clifford’s vivid soundscape do a lot of the heavy lifting, while the emotional finale delivers a proper gut punch, rounding off a promising debut for Big Finish.

Superb performances and atmospheric audio production make SilverFin an engaging Young Bond debut, though the decades-old story shows its weaknesses through uneven pacing and lengthy exposition.
