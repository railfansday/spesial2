12 places to watch the winter solstice, from Stonehenge to Newgrange

Where to watch the winter solstice, from Stonehenge's sunset axis to Newgrange's lit chamber, across 12 places in Europe, Asia, Africa and North America with access notes

The shortest day of the year has shaped architecture for millennia. Neolithic communities in Ireland, Britain and continental Europe set stones, passages and earthworks to catch the sun at the turn of winter. Some of that building happened more than 5,000 years ago. Several of those alignments still work. On the right morning or evening, light still falls where the builders aimed it.

The December solstice falls on Dec. 21 or 22 in most years. It marks the shortest day and longest night in the Northern Hemisphere. On that date, the sun stands directly over the Tropic of Capricorn, its farthest point south. North of the equator, the noon sun sits at its lowest height of the year. In the Southern Hemisphere, the same date brings the longest day.

The solstice is also a single instant, not a whole day. Its exact time shifts from year to year and depends on the viewer's time zone. That is why the calendar date can differ between countries.

This guide to where to watch the winter solstice covers 12 places across Europe, Asia, Africa and North America. They are a selection, not a ranking. They are also not interchangeable. Some frame the rising sun. Others face the setting sun. One is a former imperial ritual site rather than a viewing point. Two sit far enough north that the sun does not clear the horizon at all in midwinter.

Each entry explains what a visitor can and cannot see. Where a site has a managing body, the entry names it. Some chambers admit only a small number of people on solstice mornings. Some outdoor sites open for organized gatherings in some years and not in others. Weather also decides the outcome. A clouded sky over a passage tomb in Ireland or Scotland means no beam of light, whatever the date.

Dates, times and access rules change. Before planning a trip, confirm the year's solstice timing and each site's arrangements directly with the organization that runs it. Sunrise and sunset times also vary with latitude. Check local times for each location as well.

Stonehenge, the prehistoric stone monument in Wiltshire, England, is a sunset site at the winter solstice. Its main axis lines up with the point on the horizon where the sun goes down at midwinter.

The same axis also points to the midsummer sunrise. One line through the monument therefore connects two events six months apart. In June, the event on the axis is dawn. In December, it comes at the end of the afternoon.

The two ends of the line face opposite directions. At midsummer, the sun rises in the northeast along the axis. At midwinter, it sets in the southwest at the far end of the same line. A visitor in December looks the opposite way from a visitor in June.

To follow the midwinter alignment, an observer stands on the northeast side of the stones and looks southwest along the axis. From there, the setting sun drops toward the horizon in line with the monument rather than off to one side.

The sarsen circle is the most recognizable part of the site. It is the ring of large upright stones topped in places by horizontal lintels. It dates to roughly 2500 BC. Sarsen is a hard sandstone. The name refers to the stone itself rather than to the shape of the monument.

Because the alignment involves a sunset, the sky to the southwest has to be clear for the effect to show. Cloud on the horizon can hide the sun before it reaches the line of the stones. Overcast skies are frequent in southern England in winter. A trip planned around the date carries no guarantee of a visible sunset.

English Heritage looks after Stonehenge. The organization has run managed open access for solstice gatherings at the monument. Those arrangements are not fixed from one year to the next. Anyone planning to attend should check English Heritage for the current year's dates, timings and conditions of entry.

At Newgrange, a passage tomb in County Meath, Ireland, the rising sun at the winter solstice travels down the tomb's passage and lights its inner chamber. The illumination lasts about 17 minutes.

Newgrange is part of Brú na Bóinne, a complex of Neolithic monuments in County Meath. The tomb dates to around 3200 BC. That places it in the farming era before metalworking reached Ireland. Its builders aimed the structure at a sunrise that still reaches the chamber each December.

The light gets in through a roof box. This is an opening set above the tomb's entrance, separate from the doorway itself. Around the solstice, the morning sun shines through that opening rather than through the door. The beam then follows the passage inward until it reaches the chamber. The design lets light enter even when the doorway is blocked. That the beam still arrives shows how precisely the passage and roof box were set.

This makes Newgrange different from sites where builders marked the sun's position with stones on the horizon. Here, the alignment is built into the tomb itself. The solstice event happens inside the monument rather than in the open landscape around it. Viewers do not watch the sun rise over a skyline. They watch a beam of light move across stone in a dark interior.

The event is brief. The beam withdraws from the chamber as the sun climbs higher, and the passage returns to darkness.

Places inside the chamber on solstice mornings are allocated through an annual lottery. A visitor without a lottery place cannot watch the light enter the chamber during the solstice event.

Application arrangements and visiting details can change from year to year. Check the current process with the Office of Public Works or the Brú na Bóinne visitor centre before planning a trip.

Dowth is the sunset site of Brú na Bóinne in County Meath, Ireland. Around the winter solstice, the setting sun lights the southern chamber of this Neolithic passage tomb.

That role makes Dowth the evening counterpart to Newgrange, its neighbor in the same complex. Newgrange is tied to the solstice sunrise. Dowth is tied to the last light of the same short midwinter days.

A passage tomb is a mound of earth and stone raised over an internal chamber. A passage runs from the edge of the mound into that chamber. Dowth has more than one chamber. The southern chamber is the one aligned with the midwinter sunset.

The alignment follows from where the sun sets in midwinter. In the Northern Hemisphere, the sun sets at its most southerly point on the horizon around the December solstice. For the rest of the year, it sets farther north than that point.

A chamber oriented toward the midwinter sunset receives direct evening light only when the sun is setting near that southern limit. That is why the effect at Dowth is linked to the solstice rather than to any other time of year.

Near the solstice, the sunset point barely shifts from one evening to the next. The word solstice comes from the Latin for the sun standing still, which describes that pause. As a result, the light reaches the chamber over a run of evenings rather than on a single date.

Brú na Bóinne lies in a bend of the River Boyne. Dowth and Newgrange both sit within that bend, a short distance apart. A solstice trip to the Boyne valley can take in both tombs. Newgrange marks the morning, and Dowth marks the late afternoon.

Viewing arrangements at Dowth around the solstice should be confirmed with the Office of Public Works or the Brú na Bóinne visitor centre before traveling.

Maeshowe is a Neolithic chambered cairn in Orkney, Scotland, built about 5,000 years ago. Around the winter solstice, the setting sun shines down its entrance passage and into the tomb.

That makes Maeshowe a sunset site, not a sunrise one. The light it frames arrives at the close of the day. Visitors should plan around local sunset times rather than dawn.

A chambered cairn is a stone-built tomb covered by a mound. The structure is closed except for its entrance passage. At Maeshowe, that passage faces the point on the horizon where the sun sets near the year's shortest day.

In midwinter, the sun sets farther south along the horizon than at any other time of year. A passage pointed at that spot lines up with the setting sun only in the period around the solstice. For the rest of the year, the sun goes down out of line with the entrance.

The cairn also holds a much later layer of history. Viking runic carvings from the 12th century are cut into the stone of its interior. They were added thousands of years after the Neolithic builders finished the tomb.

The carvings show that Norse visitors entered the chamber long after its original use. The interior therefore carries two separate records. One is the Neolithic design that frames the setting sun. The other is the Norse presence in Orkney during the 12th century.

Historic Environment Scotland manages Maeshowe. Opening hours and access around the solstice can change from year to year. Check current arrangements with Historic Environment Scotland before traveling to Orkney.

The Ring of Brodgar is a Neolithic stone circle in Orkney, Scotland, measuring about 104 meters across. No solstice alignment has been established for it. A December visitor comes for the monument itself, not for a sunrise or sunset framed by stone.

That makes it a companion stop rather than a solstice destination. Travelers $TRV who reach Orkney in midwinter can pair it with Maeshowe, a separate Orkney site covered in its own entry. The two monuments show different sides of the same Neolithic landscape.

A diameter of about 104 meters is roughly 341 feet. That scale is the main thing the site offers. The monument is an open ring of standing stones, with no roof, chamber or passage. There is no interior space for a beam of light to enter.

The open design changes how a visit works. A visitor does not wait at a fixed point for a timed moment. Instead, it is possible to walk around the circle and take in its layout from any side. The visit does not hinge on a particular hour of a particular day.

Claims linking the circle to the midwinter sun should be treated with caution. No alignment between its stones and the solstice sun has been demonstrated. A stop here is a look at large-scale Neolithic construction, not a solstice observation.

The Goseck Circle in Saxony-Anhalt, Germany, is a circular enclosure dating to about 4900 BC. Its gates mark the points where the sun rises and sets at the winter solstice.

That date makes the enclosure nearly 7,000 years old. It predates the passage tombs and stone circles of Ireland and Britain by more than a millennium. Goseck was built as an enclosure, not as a ring of standing stones or a chambered tomb. Its design relied on a ring and the openings in it rather than on heavy stone.

That difference shapes how the site reads to a visitor. At a stone circle, the alignment is built into the stones themselves. At Goseck, the alignment sits in the gaps. The gates do the work, and the sky is what they frame.

What stands on the site today is a modern reconstruction. Visitors walk through a rebuilt version of the ring, not the original structure. The rebuilt gates give the solstice alignments a physical form that people can stand beside. They also make the layout easier to read than a bare outline in a field would be.

At Germany's latitude, the midwinter sun rises in the southeast and sets in the southwest. Goseck's gates face both of those points. A visitor can look out through one gate in the morning and through the other in the late afternoon.

That pairing means a single enclosure frames both ends of the shortest day. Many solstice monuments are tied to one moment, either sunrise or sunset. A solstice visit to Goseck can run from sunrise to sunset instead.

The reconstruction also sets limits on what a visitor sees. The view through the gates shows where the builders placed their openings. It does not show the original materials those builders used.

The Mnajdra temples in Malta are a Neolithic complex where the rising sun lights particular stones at the solstices and the equinoxes. The temples date to roughly the fourth millennium BC.

That makes Mnajdra a sunrise site. Around the December solstice, the moment to watch comes early in the morning, when the first light reaches the stones. There is no equivalent sunset event.

The effect is not limited to midwinter. Sunrise lights particular stones at both solstices and at both equinoxes. The complex is therefore linked to four turning points in the solar year rather than one.

The reason different stones catch the light lies in how sunrise moves along the horizon. At the equinoxes, the sun rises close to due east. In the Northern Hemisphere, the December solstice sunrise sits at its farthest point south of east. The June solstice sunrise sits at its farthest point north of east. Between the solstices, the sunrise point drifts gradually from one extreme to the other.

A doorway or passage facing that horizon admits light at a different angle on each of those dates. That shifting angle is how one stone structure can register several points in the year.

That range affects trip planning. Most other places in this guide are tied to a single December sunrise or sunset. At Mnajdra, a visitor who cannot travel in December still has other solar dates connected to the same structure.

On those dates, a visitor can watch early light reach the interior of a prehistoric stone temple. Which stone is lit on which date is not confirmed, so this guide does not name them.

Visitors hoping to see a particular stone catch the light should check with Heritage Malta, which manages the site, before they travel. Opening hours, early-morning access and any arrangements for solstice or equinox visits should be confirmed the same way.

The main axis of the Amun-Re precinct at Karnak Temple in Luxor, Egypt, is commonly described as aligned to the winter solstice sunrise. The solstice link here comes from the orientation of a temple, not from a lit chamber or a framed sunset.

Karnak stands on the east bank of the Nile in Luxor, the modern city on the site of ancient Thebes. Amun-Re was the chief god of Thebes. His precinct is the largest of the complex's enclosures.

Construction at Karnak continued for roughly 2,000 years. Pharaohs added pylons, courts and shrines from the Middle Kingdom into the Ptolemaic period. The result is a series of gateways set one behind another along the main axis.

The Great Hypostyle Hall sits on that same line. Its 134 sandstone columns were raised mainly under Seti I and Ramesses II in the 13th century BC.

The precinct also contains a sacred lake. This rectangular, stone-lined pool was used by priests for ritual purification.

The complex has a second axis running south from the Amun-Re precinct toward the precinct of Mut. The solstice description applies to the main east-west line, not to this southern route. Farther south, an avenue lined with sphinx statues connects Karnak to Luxor Temple.

Orientation also shapes where to look. In the Northern Hemisphere, the winter solstice sun rises south of due east. A sunrise alignment for this date points toward the southeastern horizon, not straight east.

The western end of the main axis opens toward the river, where the temple's main entrance stands. Visitors following the axis toward its eastern end face away from the Nile.

Karnak forms part of Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, which UNESCO inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1979.

Egypt's Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities manages the temple. Check opening hours, dawn access and the year's solstice timing with the ministry before planning a sunrise visit.

The Circular Mound Altar at Beijing's Temple of Heaven is where China's emperors historically performed rites for the winter solstice. Visitors come for the history of that ceremony. The site is not a sunrise viewing spot.

The Temple of Heaven complex dates to the Ming dynasty and was built around 1420. It sits inside a large walled park in the southern part of central Beijing. UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List in 1998.

The altar is an open, three-tiered circular terrace of white marble. It has no roof and no enclosed chamber. Its paving stones are laid in rings. The number of stones in each ring is a multiple of nine. Nine was a number associated with the emperor.

The altar's purpose was ceremonial. In imperial China, the emperor held the title Son of Heaven. At the solstice, he led a sacrifice to Heaven at the altar on behalf of the realm. Before the rite, he fasted at the Hall of Abstinence inside the complex.

The round Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests, with its triple-eaved roof of blue tiles, served a separate ceremony. Emperors prayed there for good harvests, apart from the winter solstice sacrifice at the altar. The imperial rites no longer take place.

The Dongzhi festival keeps the solstice in Chinese life today. Dongzhi is one of the 24 solar terms of the traditional Chinese calendar. It begins at the December solstice. Many families mark it with a shared meal.

Food customs differ by region. In northern China, dumplings are a common Dongzhi dish. In southern China, many households eat tangyuan, glutinous rice balls served in a sweet soup. Chinese communities in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore also observe the festival.

A visit to the Temple of Heaven around the solstice pairs a historic ritual site with a seasonal tradition still observed in the surrounding city. Local residents use the park grounds for morning exercise, including tai chi. Opening hours and access to the altar can change. Check with the park's management before going.

Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site in Illinois, U.S., has a reconstructed circle of wooden posts known as Woodhenge. The posts are used to mark the sun's position.

The ring at the site today is a reconstruction, not an original structure. It shows how a circle of upright posts can track the sun through the year. An observer can note where the sun appears on the horizon in relation to each post.

That method depends on a basic pattern in the sky. The sun's rising point is not fixed. It moves along the eastern horizon over the course of the year. A ring of fixed posts gives a viewer steady reference points for following that movement from one season to the next.

The markers here are timber posts set in a ring. There are no standing stones, no roofed chamber and no passage for light to travel down. A visitor watches the sun's position against the posts instead. The result is an open-air view of the sunrise, not a beam of light inside a tomb.

The specific relationship between the circle and the December sunrise is unverified. No particular post can be named as marking the winter solstice sunrise. The circle is a general sun-tracking structure, not a confirmed winter solstice marker.

At the solstice, a visitor can see the reconstructed post circle and the sunrise over the site. A visitor should not expect a documented winter alignment to play out on the day.

The site has held solstice sunrise events. A past event does not guarantee a future one. Anyone planning a December visit should check with Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site about current plans and start times.

Tromsø, in northern Norway, has no winter solstice sunrise at all. The city sits inside its polar night, which runs from roughly Nov. 27 to Jan. 15. The sun stays below the horizon for that entire stretch.

That sets Tromsø apart from sites built to frame a sunrise or sunset beam. What a visitor sees here is the solstice as a season of darkness and brief midday twilight, not a single moment of light.

The polar night happens because of the tilt of Earth's axis. Around the December solstice, the Northern Hemisphere leans farthest away from the sun. Places north of the Arctic Circle then go at least one full day without the sun clearing the horizon. The farther north a place lies, the longer that period lasts. Tromsø's run of roughly seven weeks reflects its position well above the circle.

Darkness here does not mean total blackness at midday. Near noon, the sun passes only a short distance below the horizon. Its light scatters through the atmosphere and produces a few hours of twilight. The sky can take on deep blue tones, with pink or orange along the southern horizon where the hidden sun sits.

Norwegians have a word for this season: mørketid, which translates as "the dark time." The midday blue light is the main daytime sight a visitor can expect during it.

The long nights also open the door to the northern lights. Tromsø lies beneath the auroral oval, the ring-shaped zone around the geomagnetic pole where auroras appear most often. With the sky dark for most of the day, there are more hours in which a display could be seen. Whether one appears depends on solar activity. Whether it can be seen depends on clear skies. Coastal cloud and snow can hide the sky for days at a time.

Visitors planning a solstice trip should confirm the current year's solstice date and local twilight times with an official astronomical source. The sun will not appear above the horizon until the polar night ends in mid-January.

Utqiaġvik, Alaska, passes the winter solstice in polar night, with no sunrise at all. The sun sets in mid-November and stays below the horizon until late January.

That makes the city a different kind of solstice destination from the stone monuments built around a single sunrise or sunset. Here the marker is the sun's absence. The shortest day of the year arrives without any direct sunlight.

Utqiaġvik is the northernmost city in the U.S. It sits on Alaska's Arctic coast, well north of the Arctic Circle, close to Point Barrow. The Chukchi and Beaufort seas meet at that point. The city is the seat of the North Slope Borough.

The city was called Barrow until 2016. Residents voted that year to adopt Utqiaġvik, the community's name in Iñupiaq. Older maps and guides may still use the former name. Most residents are Iñupiat, and whaling remains central to local life and food.

Around the solstice, a visitor sees twilight rather than daylight. For part of the midday, the sky along the southern horizon lightens to a dim blue glow. That glow is civil twilight, when the sun sits only a few degrees below the horizon. The sun itself never clears the horizon.

The long darkness also gives clear nights a chance at the northern lights. Any sighting depends on solar activity and cloud cover. No single night can be counted on.

No road links Utqiaġvik to the rest of Alaska. Travelers $TRV arrive by air, with commercial flights connecting the city to Anchorage and Fairbanks. Winter weather can delay those flights. December temperatures are routinely well below freezing, and wind adds to the cold.

Around the June solstice, the pattern reverses. The sun stays above the horizon for weeks without setting.

The exact dates when the sun sets for the season and returns vary slightly from year to year. Check the current year's sunset, sunrise and solstice timing with an official source before planning a trip.
