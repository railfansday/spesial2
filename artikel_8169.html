Day 34: The Decision to Walk as a Family

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

The premature grief of leaving the experience of the Camino lingered from the night before. Those emotions also surfaced in my dreams. So in the way I processed best, I began walking in the dark.

There were so many emotions for so many aspects of my life.

And even though the emotions were hard, I was thankful to be feeling them. Typically, tears didn’t come easy to me. So I was grateful that I could express anything at all.

I stopped at the crest of a hill because of the giant dog sitting outside the bar. The affectionate beast was the only reason I decided to stop and eat breakfast.

Another tortilla. Another cafe con leche.

Just as I hoped, my brothers arrived soon after and their presence helped shed some of the weight.

A pilgrim we knew came up and told us that someone stole his wallet while he was sleeping in the albergue last night. As a necessary precaution, I asked my one brother, who always forgot his things, if he had his. He did not.

Turns out, he left it at the restaurant where he ate breakfast, 8 kilometers away. The three of us called many places to sort out the problem and eventually coordinated a taxi to take him back to the location to look.

The American and the Argentinian tried to be the calm in the Italian storm, reassuring him that the Camino provides and that his wallet would be there. He didn’t believe us.

Then, fifteen minutes after he left in the taxi, he sent us a picture of his wallet.

He eventually caught up to me, cheerful and relieved. I took the opportunity to share something I’d been feeling.

What I wanted, more than anything, was to spend my birthday with my brothers. If we arrived in Santiago on my birthday, it’d be a dream come true, but I also knew we would have to do more kilometers each day, and I didn’t want to make them change their plans for me.

I told this to him. And he said that even though he started the Camino alone, he wanted to end it all together, just like me.

It was set then. Only one more brother to talk to.

As we walked, we found a tree carving of a Neanderthal. On the sign, it was written in three languages: Spanish, English, and Galician. Like the Basque Country, Galicia has its own language, though the two have very different linguistic roots.

My Italian brother and I arrived at the albergue the three of us reserved together in the morning, Pensión García en Triacastela.

We chose this place simply because it was €2 more than staying in the giant albergue with everyone else. And it was worth it.

We grabbed a late lunch at the restaurant below. A local dressed in neon construction clothes approached our table with a bucket. At first I was confused, but then he dropped a handful of roasted chestnuts on our table. With a big smile, he walked away.

They were still warm, and I knew they were picked from the forest nearby because of the multitude of nuts I’d walked past.

Our Argentinian brother arrived shortly after, along with several other pilgrims we knew.

One from Brazil, whom I met at the halfway mark, began telling us about the legend Jesús—the owner of the albergue we stayed at in Villafranca del Bierzo. The Brazilian told us that Jesús knows Paulo Coelho so well that Coelho actually began writing The Pilgrimage in the building where I slept. This was pivotal because that was the book that brought me to the Camino.

I struggled at that albergue because I wanted to have a meaningful conversation with the legend, but felt my Spanish wasn’t good enough. I blurted this out. The Brazilian reassured me and responded,

“I’m proud of you. I want to be like you.”

Many people on the Camino didn’t attempt to speak the language, but truthfully, most days I spoke more Spanish than English. I could feel myself improving, and the reflections from the people around me helped validate that feeling.

My Italian brother and I presented our idea to our Argentinian brother about getting to Santiago for my birthday. He agreed! Suddenly, all the grief I’d been feeling about leaving them went out the window.

On a walk to the small town supermarket, my Argentinian brother told me that he had an idea for him and me. In that conversation, he also said,

Both brothers had now said this to me one-on-one at some point during the Camino. The dynamic that was formed with these guys surpassed what I could’ve ever imagined for my walk.

Because we weren’t staying in the main albergue in town, there were only five of us in the whole place. The bunkhouse connected to the kitchen with sliding doors, and in that eating area we had the rare treasure of a TV with YouTube. Someone put on an American classic music playlist, and we all danced and had the most beautiful time.

The boys and I would now begin our journey to Santiago for real.

We each set our alarms for an early start.
