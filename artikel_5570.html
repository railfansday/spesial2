3 NOAA winter outlooks show a warmer North, wetter South and drought shifts

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

NOAA's winter outlook favors a warmer North and wetter South. See what 3 outlook areas mean for snow, floods and drought.

Americans getting ready for winter face a season whose weather depends on ocean temperatures thousands of miles away in the tropical Pacific. That El Niño pattern is already in place and is likely to shape the cold months ahead. Forecasters at the Climate Prediction Center announced its arrival in early June 2026, according to USDA Climate Hubs. The Post and Courier reports that an El Niño or La Niña pattern typically lasts nine to 12 months. If this one runs a typical course, a pattern that began in early summer would still be shaping the weather through much of the coming winter. People can plan for the general character of this season now instead of waiting to see what it brings.

This El Niño will probably be powerful, but nobody can promise that. The odds that the event reaches very strong levels during the three months starting in November 2026 stand at 63%, according to USDA Climate Hubs. The chance that it falls short is real, and every seasonal outlook built on it carries some uncertainty as a result. Its effects will also differ from one part of the country to another. Weather that one region welcomes can cause trouble in another. Even within a single place, relief from one effect can come with a new risk. Each outlook is one side of a trade-off, and none of them is a verdict on the season.

NOAA set out to predict what El Niño would do to America's winter temperatures, rain and drought, from the snow-dependent Northwest to the dry Carolinas. The three outlooks make sense only when they are read together. Temperature determines whether winter precipitation falls as snow or as rain. How much water falls, and how quickly, determines whether a drought eases or gives way to flooding. A forecast of milder weather or more rain is only part of the story. Readers who compare the outlooks can see where the odds lean and what each of those shifts is likely to cost.

This winter's temperature outlook divides the country between a warmer North and a cooler South, and the split covers the core of the season. NOAA forecasts temperatures for December 2026 through February 2027, the months that define winter rather than its edges. In a typical El Niño winter, the northern U.S. turns warmer and drier while the southern U.S. turns cooler, according to USDA Climate Hubs. For people across the northern tier, that sounds like a milder season to get through. The warmth comes in the same months when snow is supposed to pile up in the mountains, however, so a warm forecast tells only half of what the season will bring.

In the Northwest, a mild winter carries a real cost, because the region relies on mountain snow to store its water. Idaho, Oregon and Washington usually have warmer, drier falls and winters during an El Niño, according to USDA Climate Hubs. Less precipitation falls in those states, and warmer air turns more of what does fall into rain instead of snow. A thinner snowpack means less meltwater in spring and summer, when farmers and towns depend on it most. A pleasant winter can therefore leave the region short of water and more prone to fire months later.

Not all of the coming warmth may be El Niño's doing. Long-term warming lies underneath any single season, and how the two interact isn't fully understood. That makes a warm northern winter harder to dismiss as a one-time event tied only to this El Niño.

For a northern reader, the useful question is not how mild the winter will feel but how much snow it will leave behind, and answering it takes the temperature and precipitation outlooks together.

The South is likely to get a wetter, stormier winter, and the Carolinas are where people will most likely see the change. Ken Graham, director of NOAA's National Weather Service, says that storms are more likely across the South this winter because a very strong El Niño will probably push the jet stream south and east over the contiguous U.S. In the Carolinas, that shift means rain. Courtney Maskell, lead meteorologist at the National Weather Service Charleston, expects more rain than normal to be the most noticeable change in the region this winter, according to the Post and Courier. The forecast reverses the region's experience in La Niña winters, which are drier there. A wet winter in the Carolinas therefore reflects the opposite phase of the same Pacific pattern rather than ordinary weather. Even so, the outlook raises the odds of rain over the season and does not promise a wet week at any particular time. It gives Southerners a reason to prepare for rain but not a guarantee of it.

A wetter winter is not automatically a better one. The same rainfall total can fall as steady, soaking rain spread over weeks or as a few heavy storms, and those two patterns have very different results for rivers and low-lying neighborhoods. The forecaster who expects more rain in the Carolinas sees that risk in her own outlook. "That comes with the caveat of if it all comes at one time, then we risk some river flooding or localized flooding," Maskell told the Post and Courier. Her warning moves the question from how much rain will fall to how quickly it falls. A season of heavy downpours could cause damage even as it pushes the rainfall total higher.

People across the South should plan for flooding downpours as well as a damp winter, because the rain they want may come in bursts.

For communities already short of water, the question this winter is whether El Niño ends their drought or only changes the kind of trouble they face. The drought outlook covers the stretch from Oct. 15, 2026, through January 2027, according to NOAA. That window opens in mid-autumn and closes partway through winter, during months that El Niño is likely to influence. The forecast describes the fall and early winter rather than next spring. It gives drought-hit towns and farms a guide to the coming months, when the season's rain and snow will begin to reach their dry land.

The better odds of rain and snow carry a risk that belongs to dry ground in particular. Ken Graham, director of NOAA's National Weather Service, named both sides of that trade in a single warning. "While higher chances of rain and snow may bring relief to drought-afflicted areas, too much precipitation on dry soil can fuel destructive flash floods and mudslides," Graham said. The dryness that makes rain so welcome in these places is the same condition that can make it destructive. In a drought, the danger comes from the parched ground as well as from the storms overhead. Residents of the driest areas have reason to hope for this winter's water and to prepare for the damage it could cause.

A wet winter is not the same as a drought-ending one, and what counts is whether the water comes in a form dry land can use.
