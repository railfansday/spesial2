Australia’s national medical research body advises conditional use of puberty blockers for transgender children

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

New National Health and Medical Research Council guidelines at odds with public hospital bans in Queensland and NT

Get our breaking news email, free app or daily news podcast

The federal health minister, Mark Butler, says he’s confident Queensland and Northern Territory will consider the advice of Australia’s “pre-eminent authority” over their bans on puberty blockers for transgender children.

The federal government last year commissioned the commonwealth-funded National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) to develop the advice in response to bans in public hospitals imposed by the two governments.

The NHMRC’s interim national clinical practice guidelines released on Monday advise the use of puberty suppressants for gender incongruence in children in certain circumstances.

Butler said the bans were a matter for the Queensland and the Northern Territory governments and the commonwealth could not intervene, but expected both governments to consider the guidelines.

“I am very confident that every state government, no matter their political persuasion, will consider the advice of our pre-eminent authority very closely,” he said.

But the Queensland health minister, Tim Nicholls, said the state government had “already undertaken an independent review and made a clear decision on the provision of these treatments in our public hospitals”.

“Our position remains unchanged and we will continue to prioritise the health, safety and wellbeing of young Queenslanders,” he said.

The Northern Territory government also said in a statement that its ban would continue, despite the advice.

Transgender Queenslander Betce Benjamin said the community would feel validation as a result of the interim advice and many were already celebrating.

“The number of people who’ve bombarded me today with happy exclamations of joy about this outcome [is extraordinary],” they said.

Benjamin said the bans had been harmful to young people in Queensland, but the true cost wouldn’t be known for years.

“I know of young people who have gone mute because their voices broke because they missed out on puberty blockers by a couple of weeks because of the ban,” they said.

The NHMRC explicitly rejected alternative approaches to gender-affirming care proposed by opponents of the approach – such as the “wait and see” model – as a form of withholding care for children, which it stated “can affect the young person’s physical and mental health, wellbeing, and ability to function”.

“Trans and gender-diverse young people experience unjust and avoidable health disparities arising from stigma, discrimination, and barriers to accessing health care,” it said.

Puberty blockers are a class of drugs used to stop the body producing oestrogen and testosterone.

They are covered by the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme for cisgender patients and have been used for decades.

The new NHMRC guidelines do not require a child to be distressed to be treated for gender incongruence and recommend a multi-disciplinary team “should be determined on a case-by-case basis”.

Doctors should “prioritise the best interest and wellbeing of the young person and provide care that respects their autonomy”, the advice says.

The guidelines describe gender incongruence as the clinical term for a difference between a person’s gender and their sex recorded at birth.

“It is not a mental health disorder,” it states.

“Some young people with gender incongruence will also experience significant distress, known as gender dysphoria.”

Dr Stuart Aiken, the medical director at Gender Health Australia, a Brisbane clinic founded in direct response to the ban, said the advice “makes an absolute joke of the Queensland government’s decision to inappropriately ban gender-affirming care for under-18s”, a decision he said has caused “huge amounts of harm”.

Aiken said the NHMRC advice was virtually “the reverse Cass” – the inverse of the UK’s controversial review by paediatrician Hilary Cass, which led to widespread bans in that country.

“I would also go so far as to say that this is also the antidote to Cass in that it refocuses back on gender identity as just another variation in human experience,” Aiken said.

“It makes their decision seem absolutely clinically inappropriate.”

Nicholls leaned on the UK review – which did not consider Australian health standards – to justify Queensland’s ban, in 2025.

The ban prevents the state’s public hospitals from prescribing puberty blockers or hormone therapy to new patients.

The Queensland supreme court ruled the ban was unlawful later in 2025, after reporting by the Guardian which showed Nicholls did not consult with health authorities before making it. Nicholls immediately reinstated the ban and later extended it until 2031.

Public consultation on the NHMRC’s interim advice, called “Advice on the use of puberty suppressants in the care of trans and gender-diverse people aged under 18”, is open until 18 January.

The full guidelines will address other aspects of care, including the use of gender-affirming hormones, and are expected to be available in 2028.
