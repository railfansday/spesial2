10 tech companies cut jobs in 2026, and AI explains only some of the reasons

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Ten companies, from Oracle to Visa, cut jobs in 2026. Some did it to fund AI, others to fix structure or a faltering product.

For much of the past decade, a job at a large tech company looked like one of the steadier paychecks in the U.S. economy. In 2026, the biggest employers in the industry cut jobs faster than they did a year earlier. By mid-May, tech companies had cut almost 110,000 jobs, close to the roughly 125,000 they cut in all of 2025, according to Layoffs.fyi figures reported by CNBC. Struggling startups are not the only companies making these cuts. Some of the richest companies in tech are among them, along with large firms in payments and entertainment. Anyone whose work depends on software, online platforms or a big tech employer has reason to treat this year's layoffs as a signal about their own field.

When a company ties its cuts to AI, it can use that label to please investors as much as to describe what happened to the work. Executives have a reason to credit machines whether or not machines took over any tasks. "Now the world understands that jobs are being replaced by machines, and if you're not doing that, shareholders are getting upset," Umesh Ramakrishnan, chief strategy officer at Kingsley Gate, told CNBC. The proof is much weaker than the motive. The Associated Press reports that Karan Girotra, a professor of management at Cornell University's business school, says nobody can yet tell how much of the cutting AI accounts for. With that much uncertainty, each company's reasons matter more than its headcount. Some companies are freeing up cash to build AI data centers. Others are undoing years of over-hiring or thinning out layers of management, and at least one says AI played no part at all. The headcounts are often incomplete when companies first announce them, and companies keep adding to them through buyouts, later rounds and plans they have not confirmed.

Yahoo Tech tracked which tech companies cut jobs during 2026, and their reasons, following cuts at companies as different as Oracle $ORCL, Disney $DIS, Visa $V and Epic Games. Side by side, these companies are cutting for distinct reasons. Some are trimming staff while they spend heavily on AI infrastructure. Others are removing layers of management under new leaders, or under leaders who want faster results. And some are cutting because a product lost its audience, with no machine involved. What follows takes each company's cuts on their own terms, asking what is driving them and what they mean for people who work in the industry or depend on it.

Oracle $ORCL is a much smaller employer than it was a year ago. The company's headcount fell by 21,000 over the past year, Yahoo Tech reports, and the loss reached almost 13% of its staff, according to CNBC. A reduction that deep changes how the whole company operates, well beyond the removal of one weak division. In its annual filing, Oracle says that putting AI to work across its business has led to these reductions and may lead to more. Taken at face value, that means software now handles work that Oracle employees used to do.

The cuts may also help Oracle pay for the data centers that AI runs on. Oracle spent far more than it brought in last fiscal year, with free cash flow of negative $23.7 billion, according to CNBC. A gap that large puts pressure on every other cost Oracle carries, including payroll. Yahoo Tech reports that the layoffs are tied to Oracle's data center spending, while Oracle's own filing points to AI adoption as the cause. If that reporting is right, the clearest link between AI and these lost jobs is the budget, not machines visibly taking over desks.

Oracle paid heavily to shrink. It spent $1.8 billion on restructuring, far more than it spent the year before, according to CNBC. The company also acknowledges that the cuts could leave it short of the people it needs. "These types of restructurings may also lead to shortages of sufficiently skilled employees in certain roles, loss of valuable institutional knowledge, and damage to employee morale and retention," Oracle wrote in its annual regulatory filing.

The cutting did not end with the annual tally. Oracle warned staff in March that thousands of positions would go, according to CNBC, and Yahoo Tech reports another wave of notices in September, with no size disclosed. For anyone who works at Oracle or depends on it, the layoffs are a running cost of the company's bet on AI infrastructure, and that cost may keep growing.

Microsoft $MSFT's October buyout offer reaches far more people than any other cut the company has made in 2026. The company offered buyouts to about 60,000 employees, according to Yahoo Tech. That offer covers about 27% of Microsoft's workforce, CNBC reports, and it follows 4,800 layoffs that Microsoft announced in July. Over the year, the company has moved from dismissing people outright to letting them choose to leave. Microsoft employees should treat these cuts as an ongoing process rather than a single event that has ended.

Microsoft is cutting staff while it spends heavily on AI infrastructure, not because its business is in trouble. In July, the company forecast capital spending above $50 billion for the September quarter, far more than it spent a year earlier, and its second-quarter free cash flow was 23% lower than a year before, according to CNBC. Even after that drop, Microsoft brought in more cash than it spent. Its cloud competitors Alphabet $GOOGL and Amazon $AMZN did not manage that in the same quarter. A smaller payroll helps Microsoft fund its data centers without weakening its finances. The layoffs look more like a way to help pay for data centers than proof that AI is doing the work of the people who left.

The October offer is voluntary, so its final size depends on how many employees accept it. Amy Coleman, Microsoft's executive vice president and chief people officer, said the offer was about "creating greater flexibility and, where possible, more ways for employees to shape their next chapter," as reported by CNBC. Her words present a cut tied to spending as a choice for workers. A person familiar with the plan says Microsoft has not set a goal for the number of departures. The October exits could therefore fall well short of the July layoffs or far exceed them. Employees who accept leave on Dec. 31, so Microsoft's true job losses for 2026 will remain unknown until the year ends.

Meta $META's biggest cut of 2026 is a decision to make the company smaller, not a routine trim. The company is laying off around 8,000 employees in its May round, according to Yahoo Tech, and CNBC reports that the cut reaches about 10% of its workforce. Meta also plans to scrap 6,000 jobs it had not yet filled, which means the company will hire fewer people as well as employ fewer. For anyone hoping to work at Meta, there are now fewer positions to apply for, and that loss never shows up in a layoff total.

Meta isn't saving the money these cuts free up. It is moving that money to other spending. Meta told employees in a memo, as CNBC reported, that the cuts are "all part of our continued effort to run the company more efficiently and to allow us to offset the other investments we're making." Those investments are large, and Meta expects to spend as much as $145 billion on them in 2026. Meta is trading staff for spending, which is not the same as tightening its belt. Inside the company, people and infrastructure now compete for the same budget, and Meta is giving more of it to infrastructure.

The May round is not Meta's only cut of the year, and it is unlikely to be the last. Meta had already laid off workers on March 25. CNBC reports that the 8,000 figure is a projection for the May round alone, and unnamed sources expect further layoffs in August and in the fall. No one can yet give a total for the year, and the people still at Meta face doubts about their jobs through the end of it.

Cuts that keep coming wear down the employees who survive them. Employees on Blind have rated Meta's culture far lower, with the rating down 39%, according to CNBC. Leo Boussioux, an assistant professor at the University of Washington's Foster School of Business, blames the discomfort on managers who haven't found a gentler way to bring AI into employees' work. If he is right, Meta is causing the morale damage through the way it runs the change, and the damage is not a fixed price of adopting AI.

Meta is betting that its new spending will be worth more than the goodwill it is losing, even though the AI specialists it most needs are also the employees most able to leave.

Amazon $AMZN ties its cuts to a push into AI, but it is also cutting the people who build that AI. Yahoo Tech reports that an unstated number of people on Amazon's artificial general intelligence team lost their jobs in July 2026. The company described the cut as a choice about which projects to keep. It did not say that software had taken over the group's work. "This is a fast-moving space, and we're sharpening our focus on the initiatives that matter most for customers, so we can move faster on what counts," an Amazon spokesperson told CNBC. At Amazon, AI work is a budget line that managers trim like any other, so "AI layoffs" is too simple a label for what the company is doing.

The Associated Press reports that when Amazon said early in 2026 it would lay off 16,000 corporate employees, it was adding to a downsizing already well under way. That round drew the most attention because of its size. In all, Amazon had cut well over 30,000 jobs since Chief Executive Andy Jassy signaled his push into AI. For anyone who works at Amazon or competes with it, the cuts reflect a long-running strategy set at the top of the company. They are not a reaction to one bad quarter.

Amazon's cuts reach software engineers and retail staff in several countries. About 250 people lost their jobs in Amazon's devices and services unit in October 2026, and most of them worked in software development, so the cuts reached the engineers behind the company's devices. Layoffs in the stores division reached the U.S., India and the United Kingdom. Amazon also filed notices of retail cuts in California, Maryland and Washington, according to The Associated Press.

Amazon is reshaping itself on many fronts at the same time, and a reading of its cuts as a story only about AI leaves out most of what the company is doing.

Uber $UBER's September 2026 cut is a deliberate change of course for the company. Uber went through the years after the COVID-19 pandemic without a large round of layoffs, Transport Topics reports, and this time it is cutting 3,300 jobs, about 10% of its staff, according to Yahoo Tech. One goal of the restructuring is a 20% reduction in the staff who work at least seven levels down from the chief executive. Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi said the company had built up "more layers, more coordination, more fragmented ownership, and in some cases structures that made sense when businesses were smaller but no longer serve us well at our current scale." The workers most at risk at Uber are the ones in the lowest layers of management, not the staff of any single department.

Uber is still spending heavily, but on new businesses rather than on headcount. Uber has committed more than $10 billion to partnerships with robotaxi companies, according to Transport Topics, and Khosrowshahi wants the company's workers and spending concentrated where he expects Uber to grow most. After the cut, Uber will employ about as many people as it did in 2021. Uber is giving back recent growth rather than shrinking below where it stood a few years ago. Its layoffs show a large company trimming the extra layers it added while it expanded, not a company in distress.

Uber's chief executive did not name AI as a reason for the September layoffs in his memo. Uber links AI to its hiring instead. Back in May, Uber said it would slow its pace of adding staff, according to Transport Topics, and the company connected that pullback to its own growing use of AI. Uber also cut customer service jobs in July, in a round separate from the September restructuring, according to Bloomberg reporting relayed by Yahoo Tech. The September cut is the latest of Uber's moves this year, not the whole of them.

For job seekers, the lasting change at Uber may be fewer openings rather than more layoffs, and that effect is quieter and easier to miss than a layoff announcement.

Apple $AAPL aimed its 2026 layoffs at two specific projects rather than cutting across the whole company. Yahoo Tech estimates that Apple laid off more than 200 people in August. 9to5Mac reports that the cuts split about evenly, with around 100 jobs from the Vision Pro headset group and around 100 from the Siri and software teams. Cutting two of its future bets at the same time points to a change in strategy more than an effort to save money. Apple wants the move read as a reshuffle. "While we will create new roles as part of this change, it will also impact a limited number of existing roles," an Apple spokesperson said. By describing the affected roles as limited in number, Apple signals that it sees the cut as redirection, not shrinkage.

Apple is setting Vision Pro aside, and the decision comes from the top. Incoming CEO John Ternus would reportedly put the headset on hold, according to Yahoo Tech. A pause is not a cancellation, and it leaves open whether Apple will come back to the headset. Apple is also scaling back its Immersive Video team, 9to5Mac reports, so the cuts reach the content made for the device as well as the staff who built the hardware.

Apple has turned its nearer-term attention to a different wearable. 9to5Mac reports that the company is focusing on smart glasses. People who work in virtual or augmented reality, or who build apps for Vision Pro, should expect less demand for that work from Apple in the years ahead.

The headcount says less about how much Apple is shrinking than about which projects it has chosen to fund under a new boss.

Epic Games is cutting jobs for the oldest reason in business: its biggest product lost part of its audience. Yahoo Tech reports that Epic is eliminating more than 1,000 positions. Players have been spending less time in Fortnite, CNBC reports, and Epic is shedding the jobs it built around the game. Epic Games Chief Executive Tim Sweeney has ruled out automation as a cause and says AI played no part in the cuts. Epic shows that a product losing players can cost a company many jobs without any help from machines.

Epic is cutting to keep itself solvent, not to invest in what comes next. Sweeney said the company faces a simple bind: "we're spending significantly more than we're making, and we have to make major cuts to keep the company funded." Epic is also looking for more than $500 million in additional savings, according to CNBC. The company is trying to lower its costs broadly, which means it is shrinking to fit what it earns.

Epic was already getting smaller before this year. The March 24, 2026, announcement follows a round of about 830 job cuts in September 2023, according to CNBC. Two major rounds in less than three years show a company that has been pulling back for some time, even while it owns a proven hit.

For people who work in games, a job on a famous title is only as secure as the time players choose to spend in it.

Disney $DIS's layoffs were among the first moves of its new chief executive. Disney announced 1,000 layoffs in April, two months into Josh D'Amaro's tenure as its CEO, according to Yahoo Tech. CNBC reports that the April cuts fell mostly on Disney's marketing department.

D'Amaro has framed the cuts as a technology question for employees, but he has given shareholders a plainer reason: Disney wants to spend less now so it has more money for growth. In a memo to staff, he wrote that the fast pace of Disney's industries forces the company to keep rethinking how its workers use technology. CNBC reports that in an Aug. 5 letter, he and Chief Financial Officer Hugh Johnston told shareholders that Disney was concentrating on cutting costs throughout the company to free up money to spend on expansion. The pressure reaches every budget, not only headcount. Some managers have been asked to lower their costs by as much as 20%, and Yahoo Tech reports that Disney has set a 20% annual cost-cut target. Layoffs are also only one route to a smaller payroll. Disney announced an early retirement program in August, which means some of the reduction can come from employees who accept a package rather than from people who are let go.

The cutting has spread beyond marketing since April. A September plan reached many workers in human resources and technology, CNBC reports, and in November Disney reportedly planned thousands more layoffs, separate from the April round. The company's own executives expect the cuts to continue. "This is one of several actions we're taking to reshape our organization, including involuntary staff reductions that have already begun in some areas and will continue into next year," Sonia Coleman, Disney's chief people officer, said in a letter to employees, as reported by CNBC.

For anyone who works at Disney or depends on it, the 1,000 jobs cut in April mark where the cutting began, not where it will end.

Within months of taking charge of PayPal $PYPL, Enrique Lores was planning a far smaller company. He became president and chief executive on March 1, 2026, according to PayPal. PayPal plans to eliminate nearly 4,800 jobs over the next few years, or around 20% of its workforce, according to Wall Street Journal figures reported by Yahoo Tech. A cut that deep goes well beyond trimming one weak unit and amounts to rebuilding how the whole company is staffed. The cuts would come gradually, and the figure comes from a press report, so it stands as a plan rather than a final count.

Lores already knew PayPal well before he took the top job. He had chaired its board since July 2024, according to PayPal, and spent well over a year overseeing the company from the boardroom. That experience may help explain how quickly a plan this large took shape. He also arrived with experience running another large company, having served as president and chief executive of HP $HPQ Inc. When he was named chief executive, he pledged to run PayPal faster and with more discipline, and to answer for steady results from one quarter to the next. A few months later, the plan to cut staff followed.

Lores named a leaner structure as his first priority and AI as his second. "First, we will remove duplication and layers from our organizational structure. Second, we will accelerate our AI adoption and automation across our operations," Lores said. His own ranking puts reorganization, not software, at the center of PayPal's cuts.

PayPal's cut reads less as a reaction to a single bad stretch than as the opening agenda of a new chief executive.

Visa $V is cutting jobs at a time when its leaders say the business is doing well. The payments company is eliminating about 2,600 jobs, according to Yahoo Tech. CNBC reports that Chief Executive Officer Ryan McInerney credits the company's choices in recent years with giving it a business that is growing stronger as commerce shifts. That makes the cut a choice rather than a response to weak results. Visa had roughly 34,100 people on its payroll when its last fiscal year closed, and losing about 7% of them is a reduction large enough to reach across the company. For anyone at a healthy company, that is an uncomfortable lesson: A strong year no longer guarantees a safe job.

McInerney gives AI a supporting role in the reorganization rather than the leading one. "AI is also helping to accelerate this evolution and shape the way work gets done at Visa," McInerney said. In his account, Visa is using AI to move faster on a change it had already set in motion. AI was a significant factor in the layoffs but not the only one, according to CNBC. Calling this an AI layoff overstates what software did. The cut shows a company deciding how it wants to work next, with AI as one of its tools for getting there.

Visa is shrinking its staff while it pushes into new lines of business. CNBC reports that the company is concentrating on growth in three areas: affluent customers, business payments and stablecoins. A smaller workforce, using AI, will have to build out all three of those areas at once.

Whether that bet on fewer people and more software pays off for Visa is still an open question.
