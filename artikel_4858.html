Extractive Hybridization and the Future of Human Autonomy

Suppose you could press a button that saves you 20 minutes today but carries a small chance of making you less capable five years from now. Would you press it?

Probably. Most of us would. Behavioral economics has spent decades showing that human beings are not the calculating decision-makers imagined in older economic models. We give disproportionate weight to what is immediate. We stick with defaults. We dislike losses more than equivalent gains please us. We take mental shortcuts when choices become complicated. And what people around us are doing gradually shapes what feels normal. These shortcuts allow humans to navigate a world far too complicated to constantly calculate from scratch. They are useful, and they become dangerously important when we introduce a technology that is 1) built around making things easier, faster and increasingly automatic, and 2) fine-tunes to leverage the soft spots of our thinking.

This is where extractive hybridization begins. It describes a trajectory in which artificial intelligence combines with existing human tendencies, incentives and institutions in ways that progressively extract value—from our natural resources; putting a mortgage on the capital that people and planet represent. We are making a bad bargain with our future selves

Consider one of behavioral economics' most familiar findings: we tend to discount the future. A benefit today often feels more valuable than a larger benefit later. The same imbalance applies to costs. The convenience of avoiding a difficult task is immediate; the possible loss of a skill through years of non-use is remote. AI creates thousands of these tiny exchanges. Why struggle for forty minutes to understand a difficult paper when an AI can summarize it in 40 seconds? Why draft the first version yourself? Why remember information that can always be retrieved? Why work through three possible interpretations when the machine can recommend one?

Often, delegating is sensible. The problem is that the transaction is asymmetric. We experience the benefit immediately—the saved time, avoided frustration, finished task—while any cost to competence arrives gradually and is difficult to attribute to yesterday's shortcuts. A 2026 study of cognitive offloading distinguishes between using AI while retaining control of the thinking process and progressively handing the governance of that process to the machine. Both can produce similar immediate benefits; their longer-term relationship with autonomy and competence appears quite different. Beyond asking Can AI do this for me? Should we ponder What happens to me when it repeatedly does?

Defaults matter. Humans disproportionately accept whatever option requires the least intervention. That is why automatically enrolling employees in pensions can dramatically change saving behaviour without removing anyone's freedom to opt out. Now consider the progression of generative AI. Over the past three years it has been gradually embedded in email, search engines, operating systems, classrooms, and workplaces. The outcome is that artificial assistance has shifted from being an isolated crutch to the default configuration. Instead of deciding whether to use AI, we may increasingly have to decide when to override it.

It is a small difference, with big consequences.

Experiments found that the way individuals evaluate AI-generated suggestions can produce systematic errors. Human oversight does not automatically solve the problem simply because a person remains formally “in the loop.” The quality of that human judgment matters. More strikingly, the interaction with biased AI could make humans more biased over time. Participants also underestimated how strongly the algorithm had influenced them. The machine was not simply reflecting human bias; interaction resulted in a hybrid feedback loop in favor of the artificial inject. As we are moving toward a future in which AI is increasingly pervasive, it is crucial to be aware of the lopsided influence that algorithms have on our judgement.

Here Extractive Hybridization becomes more subtle. Algorithms learn partly from traces of human behaviour: what we click, watch, buy, ignore and share. Yet behavioral economics tells us that observed behaviour does not always reveal what people genuinely prefer. Sometimes we click because we are tired, distracted, angry, or acting automatically. Algorithms learn from our choices. But when our impulsive behaviour becomes training material for the environment that shapes our next choice, it results in the best-case scenario in the automation of automatism, and in the worst-case scenario in a setting where our biases are the default.

The loop is bound to become circular: We behave automatically => The system learns from that behaviour => It reorganizes our choices accordingly => Those choices encourage more of the behaviour it learned from.

Nobody needs to intend the outcome; and yet the accumulation of short-sighted choices, creates the tragedy of the commons – where the majority suffer in the long-term because a critical mass benefited in the short term. This is the micro layer of the "Hybrid Tipping Zone": a period in which repeated human–machine interactions begin to trace behavioural tracks. Like a path through deep snow, every traveller makes the established route easier for the next.

The deepest driver of today's extractive economy is a mindset that repeatedly equates more with better, faster with progress, and easier with desirable. AI puts that mindset on amphetamines. If success means maximizing output, AI can generate more. If success means capturing attention, it can personalize persuasion. If success means maximizing consumption, it can predict desires and nudge purchases. Technology amplifies. Which makes our signals precious. They are where the alternative to the status quo begins.

Hybrid flourishing does not require humans to become perfectly rational, nor AI to become perfectly aligned. It starts with recognizing the peculiarities of both. We can design AI to introduce reflection where convenience would otherwise remove it. Instead of immediately answering, a learning system might first ask what the student thinks. Decision-support tools can expose uncertainty and counterarguments rather than presenting a frictionless recommendation. Systems designed to challenge people's reasoning can reduce over-reliance, by injecting friction. That extra effort may sometimes be exactly the point.

The crucial distinction is between friction that wastes human potential and friction that develops it. That makes Extractive Hybridization fundamentally a story about mindsets. AI will amplify our ability to pursue objectives, but it cannot decide which objectives deserve pursuing. If we enter the hybrid age carrying forward short-termism, automatic behaviour and an unquestioned preference for more, faster and easier, artificial intelligence can scale those tendencies magnificently.

If we become more conscious of our current priorities, the same technology can help us do something considerably more interesting: notice our biases, widen our field of vision, explore consequences and make better choices for people and planet.

Before asking what kind of AI we want, we may need to ask what kind of human we want it to amplify.
