Invesco Bitcoin ETF Adds BNY Mellon Custody While Keeping Coinbase Arrangement

The Invesco Galaxy Bitcoin ETF has added a major banking name to the arrangements supporting its Bitcoin holdings. A November 12 securities filing discloses a custody agreement with The Bank of New York Mellon, while retaining the trust’s existing relationship with Coinbase.

The development is about how an established fund safeguards assets. It does not announce a new ETF or a fresh Bitcoin purchase. For institutions evaluating crypto exposure, however, the number and identity of available custodians can matter well beyond a fund’s headline fee.

The Form 8-K says the agreement began on November 6 and has an initial five-year term, subject to its termination provisions. Invesco, the sponsor, anticipates using both BNY Mellon and Coinbase to custody the trust’s Bitcoin.

BNY will maintain segregated custody accounts on its books for the fund’s assets. The filing does not state how much Bitcoin had been transferred to the bank or how the holdings would be divided between providers. Those are separate operational questions from signing the contract.

Keeping the earlier arrangement creates the possibility of using more than one provider. It should not be read as evidence that Coinbase has been replaced or that every coin now sits within BNY’s custody system.

The custody agreement requires commercially reasonable insurance and sets a standard of care for BNY’s work. It also contains liability limits and exclusions. Its terms say the digital assets are not deposits covered by federal deposit insurance.

That distinction deserves attention whenever a bank enters crypto custody. Safekeeping involves controls over access, records and instructions; it does not remove Bitcoin’s market-price risk. An insurance requirement is also different from a blanket promise that every possible loss will be reimbursed.

For fund oversight teams, the practical work includes understanding who can authorise transfers, how holdings are reconciled and what happens if a service becomes unavailable. A familiar banking name does not make those operational details unnecessary.

BNY’s wider digital-asset custody platform dates to 2022. In an October 8 announcement, the bank described an expansion for selected institutions in the European Union under MiCA. That European development involves its regional entity and is separate from this US fund agreement.

The broader direction is nevertheless clear: established financial-service providers are building infrastructure for institutions that want crypto exposure within familiar operating frameworks. The work includes custody, administration and transfer processes, rather than simply placing a buy button next to Bitcoin.

NewsBTC’s reporting on Bitget’s open custody network reflects another version of this demand. Institutions increasingly have choices about how trading access and asset storage fit together.

A second custody relationship can offer a fund another route for storing and moving assets. Whether it reduces concentration in practice depends on how the sponsor allocates holdings and manages dependencies. Two signed agreements alone do not establish how much redundancy exists in day-to-day operations. The sponsor would still need to reconcile balances across providers, coordinate transfer instructions and ensure that its valuation and reporting systems account for the same assets.

The market-demand question is different. NewsBTC’s report on recent Bitcoin ETF redemptions concerns investors changing exposure. A custody contract describes the infrastructure behind that exposure. The two developments can occur at the same time without contradicting one another.

For BTCO, the next meaningful disclosures would clarify implementation, asset allocation and any material changes to fund expenses. The present filing establishes an additional banking relationship while leaving the existing Coinbase arrangement intact.
