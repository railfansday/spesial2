Samantha Morton Makes Every Minute Count

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The captivating actress only needed one scene to make her mark on The Odyssey.

Samantha Morton begins each day by letting the light in. If she’s in her house in the English countryside, she’ll creep down the stairs to open the curtains and let sunshine stream onto the peacock feathers on her vintage sofa, making them glitter and gleam like jewels. If she’s in London, she’ll start her day outside, people-watching with coffee and a pastry from her favorite café. “I’m all about the light,” Morton explains. “I need to let the morning light into my heart and be in nature.”

It makes sense, then, that even when she’s inhabiting shadowy figures and dark subject matter, she can still find the light in the women she portrays. Take her much-talked-about role as Circe, the witch who transforms Odysseus’s men into pigs in Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey. Morton delivers a haunting performance; just try to take your eyes off her as she builds the intensity, humming as she shapes the men’s faces into beasts with her hands. After the epic drama’s massive opening this summer, in a cast full of A-listers like Matt Damon, Anne Hathaway, and Robert Pattinson, it was Morton that everyone was talking about. Though she appears in roughly just 10 minutes of the nearly three-hour film, her scene sticks with you long after the credits roll.

Yet Morton saw there was more to Circe than her hardened exterior. Her Circe delivers a powerful monologue to Odysseus (Damon) about his men’s primal instincts to rape and pillage, exposing their loss of humanity and animalistic greed, quite literally, and forcing him to confront the truth about what his men have become—the terrifying and marauding sea people they themselves have been warned about. “I read the script and I just felt an affinity with this woman,” Morton says, getting choked up. “It makes me quite emotional thinking about it, because I just understood her. I understood the words she was saying.” And when the time came to deliver the pivotal monologue, “I closed my eyes and [harnessed] all the truth I felt in my heart,” she recalls. “I allowed the winds to take me—I allowed magic to happen.”

Though Morton has been acting for most of her life, her role as Circe has brought newfound attention. “She’s baaack,” Morton says of the buzz around her now. “I’ve always been there—I work quietly and carefully and meticulously, but having the opportunity to be in a Christopher Nolan film has meant that there’s more visibility.” After starting her career in British TV, she charmed audiences with her portrayal of a mute woman in 1999’s Sweet and Lowdown without her ever saying a word, earning Morton her first Oscar nomination. Leading roles followed in Morvern Callar and Minority Report. In 2004, Morton picked up her second Academy Award nod for In America, playing the matriarch of an Irish immigrant family; with every questionable decision her character makes, you wish you could reach through the screen and hug her. She’s worked steadily—in The Walking Dead, Fantastic Beasts and Where to Find Them, and another brief but memorable arc in The Whale—but The Odyssey is her first performance in over two decades that has made her the subject of awards chatter again.

You could say Morton is where she is now because other people saw the light in her long before the industry did. She grew up in poverty in Nottingham, England, and had cycled through 12 foster families before age 7. (Her foster experience inspired her BAFTA-winning television film The Unloved, which she directed, wrote, and produced in 2009.) Morton began acting because of a mentor at school who got to know her. “There was a teacher, Mr. Thompson, who said, ‘You should do drama.’ And I was like, ‘What’s drama?’ He gave us pages and we did a play and I loved it.” At her teacher’s urging, she auditioned for a local drama club, and nearly missed her shot: “The letter that they sent didn’t get to me because I moved foster families. So I did get a recall, but I didn’t know.” The next year, a social worker jumped in to get Morton another audition, and she got in, an experience that she says “saved me.”

Four decades later, Nolan told her he had her in mind for Circe from the start. Morton says she felt prepared to give the role everything she had, like an Olympian who had been training for years and had been given the opportunity to go for gold. “He’s a master at his craft, and there are not many masters in the world right now. As an older woman, I get better and better all the time at what I do, but the roles get fewer and farther between,” she says. “I was ready for Chris. I’m like, ‘I’m here. I’m ready. I’m waiting to do my best for you.’”

And that’s exactly what she did. Morton poured herself into Circe, garnering comparisons to Heath Ledger in The Dark Knight. As she did onscreen, she made the most of every minute on set. “I was so sad when the last day happened and Chris and Emma [Thomas] said goodbye,” she recalls. “I was like, ‘Well, if I never work again, at least I’ve worked with those two.’ It was magical. I was like the cat that got the cream every day.”

Romantic comedy, in a cute kind of way. I’m 49; that ship has probably sailed right into the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. But I do a lot of really heavy stuff, and I don’t mind that, but I think I can be quite funny as well. And also horror. I’ve never done horror, and I love that genre very much. And I’ve also never done a superhero film. It’s like when you go to the gym. If you go to the gym and do the same thing all the time, your body’s not benefiting. If you play the same kind of roles all the time, you’re not challenging yourself.

No, no, no, no. I have never and will never method act. I’ve got three children and a wonderful husband and lots of animals. I have four dogs, four cats, and a lizard, and I do lots of other things in my life other than act. When I wrap at the end of the day and go home, it’s lovely to be me. I like being me.

I once was offered a part in a movie, and I’m not going to say the director, but he’s very famous, and it was a really cool role. But the instruction from this particular director was that no family was allowed to visit the location where we would be filming, because he wanted all the actors to be in character. He said, “You’re not allowed to bring your partner or children to be with you at any point.” I was like, “Well, I don’t want the job then.” I need to be human. I’m a human being. I’m a worker bee, but I go home and be me.

I want to be really, really old and I want to be on a lot in Hollywood, because I still want Hollywood to be making movies in California. That’s a vision I have: I’m really old, but I’m working in Hollywood. That’s what I want. That’s what I hope. Maybe I’m just very romantic, but I just love working in California. I love Charlie Chaplin and I love the old days of Hollywood. I hanker for days like that, when there are lots of people making lots of cool movies in the sunshine.

Parity for female directors. I’m on a show at the moment, and there are no female directors: The female directors that were potentially approached were busy, and then ones that I recommended for my work were not cleared because they’re not experienced enough. But we need to get the experience. It’s kind of a catch-22.

I made my first TV movie and I won a BAFTA for it, for Best Single Drama, and two of the actors were nominated for BAFTA awards for their performances, and one won. And still, I can’t get my second film made. It’s really brutal being a female director. That’s the glass ceiling we need to break now. We need to be in that room.

British comedian Crissy Rock said, “Don’t let the bastards grind you down.” It is a brutal industry. When I was 19, I did a movie with her. She’s very Liverpudlian. She’s a stand-up comedian, but would work men’s clubs. She is hardcore, and she said that to me. Then whenever I didn’t get a role, or something didn’t go my way, or something awful happened, I’d just smile and go, “Okay, not going to let it get me down. Tomorrow’s a new day and I’m not going to let that ruin my life.”

Being a female actress is really complicated—from having your period on set, to the bathrooms always being quite far away, to if you do have gynecological issues, which a lot of women have, whether it’s endometriosis, adenomyosis, or fibroids. We have to do that working 16- and 17-hour days and six-day weeks. And we have children, and our breasts get bigger and smaller, and our bodies go in and out. It is incredibly tricky to be a female performer. The world is brutal to women, certainly now in regard to social media, the globalization of our image, cruelty, and the way that women are scrutinized for everything they do: how they are in interviews, how they are on the red carpet, what they look like, how big or how small they are, their relationships, whether those relationships last a long time or don’t.
