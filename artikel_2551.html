Why the inaugural Nations Championship finals weekend could be doomed to fail

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

It is the season of feasting, of plates piled high, of over-indulgence in the name of festive excess and collective conviviality. How much is too much? That is a question that may well be answered on the inaugural Nations Championship finals weekend, with a six-course tasting menu of global flavour whetting just about any appetite.

The Twickenham grounds staff may not give thanks – even if there is a new pitch for the super-sized portions to be served – but for any rugby lover prone to immoderation, the larder is stocked for three days of gluttony. The world's top 12 nations will take to the same surface in an ambitious array of Test matches that stretch from Friday afternoon to Sunday evening; this sport has not really known anything quite like it.

All 12 teams will play at Allianz Stadium, Twickenham over three days on Nations Championship Finals weekend (Reuters)

"It is amazing," the South Africa rugby captain Siya Kolisi beamed at a chaotic launch on Wednesday as he looked ahead to a final with France. "It gives a different feeling for it because you know you're fighting for something. Every game is important, but I think this has been really amazing because you're competing against the north, but you're also competing against the south in the same pools.

"I think it has given it more drive and it has given it more excitement. I think it's great to finally get that, the two best teams from the two hemispheres playing against each other."

Siya Kolisi is excited for the Nations Championship final (Getty)

Will it be a success? The jury is out; so, too, are a significant number of potential match-goers. One can, if that way inclined and prepared to pay a pretty penny, still buy tickets to each and every day, with the top tier of the Allianz Stadium expected to be shut off entirely for Friday's double-header. There may multiple mitigating factors, with England having just sold out three home games and fans elsewhere in Europe having had recent opportunity to watch their teams on home soil – but for a competition designed to grow the commercial potential of the sport, it is an inauspicious start.

The format does not help matters, with fixtures only confirmed late last Saturday, hardly providing consumer certainty. The meaning of each contest could be said to be quite different, too: while England and New Zealand, say, can get excited for another tango, and South Africa and France dance again with a trophy on the line, Ireland and Wales may feel they have little to play for with their most significant results of November clearly in the rear-view mirror. Hopes in the future that it could become a destination event – Spain is known to be interested in hosting the finals in 2028 or 2030 – may rely on showing genuine supporter interest in each and every game.

Frets over vulnerable fibres do not stop with the Twickenham turf, either, with the game's top stars asked to stretch their sinews again at Test level. Those that are here, at least: Will Jordan and Pieter-Steph du Toit are just two of the game's best to have joined the walking wounded. Custodians of the coffee shops of southwest London will have heard the creaks of floorboards and forwards as the burly men collected their flat whites this week.

Pieter-Steph du Toit has been ruled out of the final (Getty)

Why, then, has rugby taken this grand gamble? Well, context is said to be king. This cohesive competition across the July and November windows was seen as a more attractive proposition for broadcasters and sponsors, with a finals weekend particularly valuable to the former. While a deal with Qatar Airways fell through due to factors beyond the control of organisers, anecdotal and data-based evidence suggests interest has been strong – even if the delights of the Wallabies against Ireland have not been enough to shift other Australian-based celebrities off ITV1's Friday night schedule.

There has been interest among players, too, with England and New Zealand among the most ardent observers of events in Dublin and Paris last weekend. Some within the game have suggested the competition has forced teams to pick stronger sides: France, though, did things a different way with the two bonus points earned by a largely second-string against the All Blacks in July ultimately proving pivotal. As the biennial tournament beds in, it is thought that fans may more keenly consider the impacts of that first phase on the overall table; a two-year wait for the next edition provides a bit of time for organisers to look at what has worked and what hasn't.

New Zealand take on England for a second week in a row (Getty)

A new body of the founder 10 unions – the competition's entrants minus invitees Fiji and Italy – has been formed to do precisely that, and work more collectively to capitalise on what it seen as a vital opportunity. That is understood to have come on the back of a piece of work started by Six Nations chief Tom Harrison, formerly of the ECB. Senior sources within the game have downplayed any suggestion that "F10" may, in time, be a powerplay against World Rugby, which still governs the game globally, but The Independent has had conversations this week where reference has been made to the influence Uefa has within football – and its relationship with Fifa is hardly chummy at the moment.

What might this weekend reveal about the balance of power in the game? It is only really at the World Cup, when each side is rested and ready, that it actually becomes clear. But the northern sides have most certainly enjoyed some success across the last fortnight – to the point that were hemispheric supremacy not such a tenet of this competition, Six Nations sides would have finished first, second and third on a combined ladder.

France and South Africa will vie for the trophy this weekend (Reuters)

As is, it could well be the Springboks toting another trophy come Sunday night, even after back-to-back defeats. Their rematch with France promises to be another ferocious occasion, provided everything – fans, pitch and rugby foundations – is still standing by then.
