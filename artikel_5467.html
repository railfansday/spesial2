From the 1845 law to midnight votes, 15 facts about how Election Day works

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The reason Americans vote on a Tuesday goes back to an 1845 law and a farm calendar, and 15 slides trace the history through ballots, amendments and traditions

The U.S. picks its presidents on a schedule designed for a very different country. When Congress settled on a single national voting day in 1845, most Americans lived in rural areas and worked in agriculture. Many had to reach a county seat to cast a ballot, and that trip could take a day or more. The calendar lawmakers chose reflected long trips, harvests and Sunday worship — not office jobs, commutes or mail-in ballots.

That 1845 law fixed the date for choosing presidential electors as the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. It said nothing about why. The usual reasons come from historical accounts, not from the statute's text. They include a harvest finished by November, roads still passable before winter and a Sunday kept free of travel. Other stories, such as Wednesday being market day, get repeated so often that they sound settled. They are commonly cited explanations, not documented ones.

Knowing why Americans vote on a Tuesday also explains how the rest of the system grew around that date. Before 1845, states set their own dates within a window Congress allowed, and voting stretched across weeks. In 1872, Congress extended the same November date to House elections. Senate races joined after the 17th Amendment gave voters, not state legislatures, the power to elect senators. The date stayed put while the rules about who could vote, and how, changed around it.

The secret ballot replaced the often public practice of voting by voice or with party-printed tickets. Constitutional amendments protected women's right to vote, banned poll taxes in federal elections and set the voting age at 18. Congress passed the Voting Rights Act in 1965. Each shift happened on top of a calendar first drawn for a farming economy.

The first slides cover the calendar itself: the law, the month, the weekday and the specific phrasing that keeps Election Day from ever landing on Nov. 1. The next group traces how elections for Congress joined the same date and how access to the ballot widened. The last group looks at the traditions and rules that surround the day now. Those include a New Hampshire town's midnight vote and the fact that Election Day is not a federal holiday. They also compare how other democracies schedule elections and trace the weeks between the vote and the inauguration.

Americans vote on a Tuesday because of a federal law Congress passed in 1845. It set one national day for choosing presidential electors: the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. That rule still fixes the date of presidential elections.

The law covered presidential elections only. It did not set the timing of congressional races, which stayed under state control for decades afterward. Voters were also not choosing a president directly. They were choosing the electors who would cast the formal votes for president on behalf of each state.

Congress had clear authority to act. Article II of the Constitution lets Congress determine the time of choosing electors and the day on which they give their votes. It also requires that the electors' voting day be the same throughout the nation. The 1845 statute used that power to fix a single Election Day for the whole country.

President John Tyler signed the measure in January 1845. The 1844 race had already been decided under the older arrangement. The new rule first applied four years later. The 1848 contest was the first presidential election held under the single-day system. Zachary Taylor, the Whig candidate, won it.

The law also included a fallback provision. It allowed a state that failed to make a choice on Election Day to appoint its electors on a later day. The state legislature could direct how that later appointment happened. That clause stayed on the books for more than 170 years. Congress narrowed it in the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022. The newer law permits a state to extend its voting period only for extraordinary and catastrophic events.

The 1845 law set the timing of the choice, not its method. Each state legislature still decided how its electors would be appointed. South Carolina's legislature kept picking the state's electors itself, rather than holding a popular vote, until after the Civil War. The Election Day rule now sits in Title 3 of the U.S. Code, the part of federal law that governs presidential elections.

Election Day was built around the working life of the American farmer. According to the standard historical account, when Congress set a national voting date in 1845, the calendar had to suit a country where most voters lived and worked on farms.

Distance was the central problem. For many rural voters, casting a ballot meant traveling to the county seat. That trip could take a day or more.

Travel moved at the pace of a horse. In the 1840s, a voter generally made the journey on horseback, by wagon or on foot. Most roads were unpaved. Rain could turn those dirt roads to mud and lengthen the trip. Railroads were still new and covered only a small part of the country. Few farm families could count on a train to reach the polls.

A trip of that length turned voting into an errand measured in days, not hours. A voter had to leave home, reach the polling place, cast a ballot and then make the return journey.

For a farm family, each of those days carried a cost. Time on the road was time away from crops and livestock. In the standard account, a workable date had to leave room for the journey in both directions, not only for the moment at the ballot box.

That logic belonged to the electorate of the 1840s. The U.S. later became a mostly urban country. The 1920 census was the first to count more Americans living in urban areas than in rural ones. For that count, the Census Bureau treated places with 2,500 or more residents as urban. That crossover arrived 75 years after the 1845 law.

The federal voting date did not move with that shift. Election Day still falls on the schedule Congress fixed in 1845, when reaching the polls could mean a day or more on the road.

Election Day landed on Tuesday largely by elimination. Sunday was reserved for worship, and a Monday vote would have forced many voters to travel on Sunday.

The Sunday problem was the simplest to see. In the mid-1800s, Sunday was a day of worship for much of the country. Holding an election on it would have put civic duty in direct competition with church. The national date Congress set in 1845 kept voting off the Sabbath.

Monday created a second-order problem. Rural voters who lived far from the polls needed travel time before they could cast a ballot. A Monday election would have meant leaving home on Sunday. That ran into the same objection that ruled out Sunday voting itself. Tuesday gave those voters a full day to make the trip after worship.

Wednesday is where the history gets less certain. A frequently repeated account holds that Wednesday was market day in many places, when farmers brought goods to town to sell. Under that account, a Wednesday election would have cut into a working day that rural families depended on.

That market-day explanation is a commonly cited reason, not documented intent. The 1845 statute fixes the date without recording a rationale for any weekday. The reasons attached to it today are reconstructions built from the conditions of the period. They are not language written into the law.

Taken together, the three constraints point toward a narrow window. A voter could attend worship on Sunday, travel on Monday and cast a ballot on Tuesday. Under the market-day account, that voter could also be back for Wednesday's trading. The commonly repeated explanation does not address Thursday, Friday or Saturday, and the law itself is silent on them too.

The result has outlasted the farm economy that shaped it. The same Tuesday rule still governs federal elections, even though few Americans now farm or plan their week around a town market.

Election Day falls in November because the month offered a workable window in a farming country. The standard historical explanation holds that the harvest was largely finished by then, while winter had not yet closed the roads.

That explanation comes from historical accounts, not from the law. The 1845 statute that created a single national date for choosing presidential electors names the month without explaining it.

The farm side of the reasoning follows the agricultural year. Spring meant planting. Summer and early autumn meant tending and gathering crops. A vote during those months would have pulled people away from the fields at the busiest time. Much of that work was done by November.

The weather side concerns travel. Many voters went to the polls by horse, by wagon or on foot over unpaved roads. Snow, ice and mud could make those routes hard or impossible to use later in the year. An early November vote came before the worst of winter across much of the country.

Those conditions were not the same everywhere. Winter arrived far earlier in New England than along the Gulf Coast. The 1845 law still set one national date for every state.

The November timing also fit the federal schedule already in place. A 1792 law had let states choose electors during a window that closed before the electors met in December. The 1845 date fell inside that window, so the electors' December meeting did not have to move.

The first presidential election held on the uniform date took place on Nov. 7, 1848. Federal law still sets the presidential vote in November, in Title 3 of the U.S. Code. That vote comes every fourth year, in years divisible by four, such as 2024 and 2028. The date has outlasted the farm economy that is said to have shaped it.

The phrase "the Tuesday after the first Monday in November" fixes federal Election Day to a date between Nov. 2 and Nov. 8. That wording means the vote can never land on Nov. 1, even in years when the month begins on a Tuesday.

The rule works in two steps. Find the first Monday of November, then move forward one day. A simpler instruction, such as "the first Tuesday in November," would put the election on Nov. 1 whenever the month opens on a Tuesday. The extra step removes that possibility.

The 2016 presidential election shows the formula at work. Nov. 1, 2016, fell on a Tuesday, so the first Monday of that month was Nov. 7. Voters went to the polls the next day, Nov. 8. That is the latest date the formula allows.

The other end of the range appears when November starts on a Monday. In 2004, Nov. 1 was a Monday, which made Nov. 2 Election Day. The 2020 election, held on Nov. 3, landed near the start of the window.

The 1845 law that created the formula does not say why lawmakers wrote it this way. Two reasons for keeping the vote off Nov. 1 are commonly cited. Both are later explanations, not reasons recorded in the statute.

The first involves All Saints' Day. The Christian feast falls on Nov. 1. For Roman Catholics, it is a holy day of obligation, when attendance at Mass is expected. The explanation holds that lawmakers wanted to avoid scheduling a national vote on a major religious observance.

The second involves commerce. Merchants are often said to have used the first of the month to close out the previous month's accounts. Under this account, a Nov. 1 vote would have collided with a day many business owners set aside for bookkeeping.

The result is a window of seven possible dates. The formula depends only on the calendar, so any future Election Day can be worked out years ahead. The 2028 presidential election, for example, falls on Nov. 7.

Before 1845, there was no single Election Day for president. Each state picked its own date for choosing presidential electors, as long as it fell inside a window Congress set in 1792.

That 1792 federal law gave states a 34-day window. Electors could be appointed at any point during the 34 days before the first Wednesday in December, the day the electors then met to vote. A state could hold its contest early in that span or late in it.

The Constitution made this arrangement possible. Article II gives Congress the power to set the time for choosing electors and the day on which electors cast their votes. It requires that the electors' voting day be the same throughout the U.S. It sets no such uniformity rule for choosing the electors themselves. Until 1845, Congress left that timing to the states within the federal window.

Article II also leaves the method of appointing electors to each state legislature. In the early decades, many legislatures chose electors themselves instead of holding a popular vote. By the 1830s, South Carolina was the only state still doing so. Its legislature kept picking the state's electors through the 1860 election. Where a popular vote did happen, the state set its own date for it.

The practical result was a presidential election spread across weeks. Voters in one state could go to the polls well before voters in another. The national contest unfolded in pieces, with each state on its own schedule.

That staggered calendar created a timing problem. Results from states that voted early could become known before other states had voted at all. Voters in later states could cast ballots after some of those outcomes were already known.

That problem is a commonly cited reason for a single national day. Congress settled the question in 1845. It replaced the open window with one fixed date for choosing presidential electors in every state.

Congress put elections for the U.S. House of Representatives on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November through a law passed in 1872. That measure gave House races the same national date already used for choosing presidential electors.

The 1872 law arrived 27 years after the 1845 statute that first created a single national Election Day. The earlier law covered presidential elections only. It set no date for congressional contests.

Before 1872, each state picked its own date for House elections. Some states held those races on the same day as presidential voting. Others held them on different days. The country had no single date for choosing its representatives.

The Constitution's Elections Clause gave Congress the authority to make the change. The clause leaves the times, places and manner of congressional elections to state legislatures. It also lets Congress make or alter those rules. The 1872 law used that federal power to set one date for House races across the country.

After 1872, House elections shared the November date with presidential voting. The Constitution puts every House seat up for election every two years. As a result, the law placed a federal contest on the November ballot in years without a presidential race, too.

U.S. Senate seats were not part of this change. State legislatures still chose senators at the time, so there was no popular Senate vote to schedule.

Voters have elected U.S. senators directly only since 1913, when the 17th Amendment was ratified. Before that, state legislatures chose senators. The change moved Senate races onto the same national November date that voters already used for other federal offices.

The original Constitution set up the Senate differently from the House. Article I, Section 3 gave each state two senators. It also said they would be chosen by the state's legislature for six-year terms. House members, by contrast, were elected by the people from the start. The framers designed the two chambers to answer to different constituencies.

Under that system, a voter's influence over the Senate was indirect. Citizens elected state lawmakers, and those lawmakers then picked the senators. A Senate seat could hinge on which party controlled a state legislature rather than on a statewide vote.

The 17th Amendment rewrote that arrangement. Its text provides that the Senate is made up of two senators from each state, elected by the people of that state, for six years. It also requires voters in Senate elections to meet the same qualifications as voters for the most numerous branch of their state legislature. That wording tied eligibility for Senate voting to rules each state already used.

The amendment also addressed vacancies. It directs a state's governor to call an election to fill an open seat. A state legislature may empower the governor to make a temporary appointment until voters fill the seat.

Once senators were chosen by popular vote, their races joined the federal ballot on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. Presidential, House and Senate contests now share that date.

The six-year term did not change. Senate terms are staggered. About one-third of the seats come up in each two-year election cycle. As a result, each regular November general election includes Senate races in only some states. Every House seat, by contrast, is on the ballot each time.

The secret ballot let Americans mark a government-printed ballot in private, replacing a system in which many votes were cast in public. Massachusetts adopted the approach statewide in 1888, and other states followed over the next several years.

Before that shift, casting a vote was often a visible act. In some places, voters announced their choices aloud at the polling place. Elsewhere, they handed in tickets printed and distributed by political parties rather than by election officials.

A party ticket usually listed only that party's candidates. A voter could pick one up from a party worker, carry it to the polls and drop it in the ballot box. The paper itself could show anyone watching which side a voter supported.

That visibility had consequences. An employer, a landlord or a party operative standing near the polls could see how a person voted. Someone paying for votes could confirm that the purchase had been delivered. Voters who depended on others for work or housing had reason to think twice before breaking with them in public.

The reform became known as the Australian ballot. It took that name because the colony of Victoria, in Australia, introduced it in 1856, decades before it reached the U.S. Under the system, election officials, not parties, printed the ballots. Every qualifying candidate appeared on the same sheet. Voters marked their choices out of view of others.

Private marking meant a vote buyer could no longer check what a voter had done. A single official ballot listing all candidates made it easier to choose people from different parties in the same election. Government printing also meant the state now had to decide whose names appeared on the ballot. That pushed lawmakers to write rules for how candidates qualified.

The ballot most Americans fill out on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November descends from this late-19th-century design. The private voting booth, the uniform official ballot and the expectation that no one else sees a voter's choices all trace back to it.

The 19th Amendment, ratified in 1920, bars the federal government and the states from denying the vote on account of sex. The presidential election of Nov. 2, 1920 was the first in which women across the country could vote with that right protected by the Constitution.

The amendment's core text is a single sentence. It says the right of citizens of the U.S. to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." A second section gives Congress the power to enforce that guarantee through legislation.

The proposal took more than four decades to clear Congress. Sen. Aaron Sargent of California introduced the language in 1878. It became known as the Susan B. Anthony Amendment. The House approved it on May 21, 1919. The Senate followed on June 4, 1919, sending it to the states.

Ratification required approval from three-fourths of the 48 states then in the union. Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify on Aug. 18, 1920. That vote supplied the final approval needed. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified the amendment on Aug. 26, 1920.

The November vote that followed kept the schedule Congress had set in 1845. Nov. 2, 1920 was the Tuesday after the first Monday of the month. Republican Warren G. Harding defeated Democrat James M. Cox in that race.

The push for a national guarantee began long before 1920. The Seneca Falls Convention in New York issued a call for women's voting rights in 1848. Wyoming's territorial legislature granted women the vote in 1869. Several Western states extended full voting rights to women before the amendment took effect. The 19th Amendment replaced that state-by-state patchwork with a constitutional standard.

The amendment did not put a ballot in every woman's hand in practice. Many Black women in Southern states stayed blocked by the same discriminatory registration rules used against Black men. Many Native American women could not vote because the U.S. did not yet recognize them as citizens. Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act in 1924. Some states still restricted Native voting for years afterward.

Three changes between 1964 and 1971 widened who could cast a ballot on Election Day. The 24th Amendment, ratified in 1964, banned poll taxes in federal elections. The Voting Rights Act became law in 1965. The 26th Amendment, ratified in 1971, set the voting age at 18.

A poll tax was a fee charged as a condition of voting. By the 1960s it survived mainly in Southern states. There it had long served to keep Black voters and poor white voters away from the polls. The 24th Amendment covered only federal races — presidential, Senate and House contests. Its text left state and local elections untouched.

That gap closed two years later. In Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections, decided in 1966, the Supreme Court ruled that poll taxes in state elections violated the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment. The amendment and the ruling, taken together, ended the practice.

The Voting Rights Act reached further than the poll tax ban. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed it on Aug. 6, 1965. That came months after state troopers attacked civil rights marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama. The law targeted tools such as literacy tests, which local officials had used to turn away Black applicants. It also allowed federal examiners to register voters in some places.

One of its central provisions, known as preclearance, required jurisdictions covered by the law to get federal approval before changing their voting rules. In Shelby County v. Holder, decided in 2013, the Supreme Court struck down the formula that determined which jurisdictions were covered. Without a valid formula, the preclearance requirement no longer applies to the jurisdictions it once covered. Other parts of the act remain in force.

The 26th Amendment lowered the minimum voting age to 18 in federal, state and local elections. Before it, most states set the minimum at 21. The push gained force during the Vietnam War. Men could be drafted at 18, but in most states they could not vote. Supporters rallied around the slogan "old enough to fight, old enough to vote."

Congress had first tried to lower the age by statute. In Oregon v. Mitchell, decided in 1970, the Supreme Court ruled that Congress could set an 18-year-old voting age for federal elections but not for state ones. That split would have forced many states to run separate voting systems for different races. A constitutional amendment resolved the problem, and the states ratified it in 1971.

Dixville Notch, a small town in New Hampshire, casts its Election Day ballots at midnight, in the first minutes of the voting day. The town has held this midnight vote since 1960.

Federal Election Day falls on a Tuesday. In Dixville Notch, voting begins as soon as Monday night turns into Tuesday morning. Most polling places in the U.S. open hours later, in the morning. That timing puts the town's ballots in before most of the country has started voting.

The tradition started in a presidential election year. The town has repeated the midnight vote in the decades since. Its ballots are now among the first cast on Election Day in the U.S.

The early start changes only when residents vote, not the rules they vote under. Dixville Notch uses the same national date as every other community. Its ballots count toward New Hampshire's results the same way any other town's do.

Election Day is a regular working day in the U.S. Federal law fixes the date for federal elections, but no federal law makes that Tuesday a federal holiday.

That leaves time off to the states. Some states have their own provisions that give workers time off to vote. Others treat Election Day as a state holiday. The rules vary, so what a voter can expect depends on where they live and work.

The date and the holiday question sit in different parts of federal law. The 1845 law and the statutes that followed it set when federal elections take place. Federal holidays are listed separately, in Title 5, Section 6103, of the U.S. Code, and Election Day is not among them.

The list sets paid days off for federal employees. Federal law does not require private employers to close on any of those days. Federal offices stay open on Election Day, and it runs like any other Tuesday on the federal calendar.

One federal holiday sits close by. Veterans Day falls on Nov. 11. Federal Election Day can land no later than Nov. 8, so the two never coincide. Veterans Day arrives three to nine days after the vote.

For voters, working-day status shapes the time they have. People with fixed shifts who vote in person that day must go before work, after work or during a break, unless they get time off. Many states also offer early in-person voting or mail ballots, which let people vote without a Tuesday trip to the polls. Polling hours, which states set, decide how much room the day itself leaves.

The result is a split system. The date is national and fixed by federal law. Any time away from work to use it comes from state law, an employer's own policy or a voter's own schedule.

Germany holds its federal elections on Sundays, while U.K. general elections have traditionally fallen on Thursdays. Many other countries also schedule national votes on weekends. The American Tuesday is one answer among several to the question of when a country should vote.

Germany's Sunday schedule moves the federal vote outside the Monday-to-Friday workweek. A voter on a standard weekday schedule does not have to arrange time off, leave early or line up before a shift to take part.

The U.K. custom sits closer to the American model. Thursday, like Tuesday, falls in the middle of the working week. Voters with weekday jobs in either country have to fit a trip to the polling place around their hours on the job.

Weekend voting in other countries follows the same reasoning as Germany's Sunday elections. For people who work Monday through Friday, a Saturday or Sunday vote falls on a day off. The trade-off runs the other way for people whose jobs include weekend shifts. They face the same scheduling problem a weekday voter faces in the U.S. or U.K.

Set side by side, these approaches show that the day of the week is a choice each country makes, not a fixed feature of national elections. Germany and many other countries put the vote on a weekend. The U.K. and the U.S. keep it on a weekday.

Election Day does not install a president. Voters choose electors in November, those electors cast the formal votes in December, and the new term begins at noon on Jan. 20 under the 20th Amendment, ratified in 1933.

The electors meet in their own states rather than in one national gathering. The Constitution requires that arrangement. Under the 12th Amendment, each state's electors cast separate ballots for president and vice president. The results are sealed and sent to Washington, D.C.

Electors are chosen through the popular vote in each state. Their December ballots are the formal step between November's results and the official count in Congress.

Congress then counts the electoral votes in a joint session in January. The sitting vice president, acting as president of the Senate, opens the certificates. A candidate needs a majority of the 538 electoral votes, which is 270, to win outright. If no one reaches that mark, the House of Representatives chooses the president. In that case, each state delegation casts one vote.

For most of the country's history, the wait was far longer. Presidents took office on March 4, a date tied to the start of the federal government under the Constitution in 1789. That left about four months between the November vote and the transfer of power.

The long gap had real consequences. After Abraham Lincoln won in November 1860, seven Southern states seceded before he took office in March 1861. James Buchanan remained president through that winter.

The 20th Amendment cut the presidential wait by roughly six weeks. It also moved the start of new congressional terms to Jan. 3. As a result, a newly elected Congress is seated before it counts the electoral votes. The measure is often called the lame duck amendment. It shortened the period that outgoing officials, known as lame ducks, serve after an election.

Franklin D. Roosevelt was the last president first inaugurated on March 4, in 1933. His second inauguration, in 1937, was the first held on Jan. 20.
