An Iranian Woman Falls Pregnant to Avoid Execution in ‘Inherited’: Situation in Iran Is ‘Increasingly Complex’

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The director, whose latest film premiered at the Cairo Film Festival, says geopolitical instability 'makes it harder and slower for new talents to emerge' in Iran

A few years ago, Iranian filmmaker Morteza Alizadeh saw a photograph that he couldn’t shake from his mind: it showed mothers and their children going about their lives, which at first seemed routine, until the director realized it was taken inside a women’s prison.

From that first glimpse into a little spoken-about reality, Alizadeh spent years researching how common it is for Iranian women to have children while waiting to be executed in prison. In “Inherited,” world premiering at the Cairo Film Festival, the director tells the fictional story of one of them, Mina (played by Mina Sadati). Sentenced to death for murder and running against the clock of her scheduled execution, she learns the only way to postpone her fate is to become pregnant. “My main goal with this film was to portray people who find themselves in difficult and sensitive situations, where they are forced to make choices to survive,” Alizadeh tells Variety. “These choices affect not only their own lives but, more importantly, the lives of others.”

The director dedicated a long period to research, collecting an extensive archive of images from inside prisons, which included rare documentaries and footage from hacked surveillance cameras. He also spent months meeting and interviewing women who had been imprisoned and had either been released temporarily or were on leave. “With their help, we were able to design the entire prison environment in detail,” he notes. “Then our production design team recreated and built the whole prison set as closely as possible to the real thing.”

By posing a complex moral conundrum when Mina’s family pleads with the unmarried prisoner to abort the baby to preserve the family’s honor, the director wanted the audience to “ask themselves” how much right “does one person have to decide another person’s fate.” “What gives us the authority to decide whether to take someone’s life or give another person the chance to live?”

At the same time, it was important to me that the audience wouldn’t simply judge the characters for their choices, but would also try to understand the circumstances and the structures that put them in such difficult situations,” he adds.

The film’s strong moral core and an unwillingness to offer easy answers speak to a long tradition of Iranian cinema established by names such as Jafar Panahi, Asghar Farhadi and Abbas Kiarostami. “I believe the roots of this cinematic tradition, of creating morally complex situations, can be found in Iranian society and its social and cultural conditions,” says Alizadeh.

In that sense, the director feels a “deep connection” between “Inherited” and the work of his countrymen, which all come from a “shared reality of Iranian society” and are “influenced by the circumstances we live in.” “The experiences, contradictions and complexities of our society gradually build up inside us and find their way into our films, consciously or unconsciously.”

Speaking of his home country, Alizadeh says the situation in Iran has become “increasingly complex.” “Alongside the difficult economic conditions that have created countless problems for people, to the point where simply getting through everyday life has become almost impossible for the majority, two wars in less than a year, people’s resistance against these attacks, and public protests between the two wars have made the situation in the country even more complicated.”

“As a result,” he says. “It has become harder for artists to understand and respond to the social conditions around them.”

This week, major industry names such as Mark Ruffalo and Tilda Swinton signed a statement shared with Variety that says they are “deeply concerned” by reports of the new prison sentence of five years and one month issued by the Tehran Revolutionary Court against Panahi, as well as the confiscation of his assets, the freezing of his bank accounts, a two-year travel ban and a two-year social media ban.

“Iranian cinema is going through a very difficult period as it tries to redefine its relationship with these circumstances and overcome both internal obstacles, such as the relationship between filmmakers and society, and external ones, particularly censorship,” he goes on. “This makes it harder and slower for new talents to emerge. I believe the most important thing that can help move Iranian cinema forward and create opportunities for new filmmakers is their courage to tell stories while staying true to their own personal voice and style.”
