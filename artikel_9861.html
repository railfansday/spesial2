Offline reverse geocoding on iPhone with one memory-mapped file

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Disclosure: I work on ClearWhirl, an iPhone app that works out what is in your photo library on the device, with no upload. Places is one of its features.

Every photo with a location carries a latitude and longitude. Turning that into "Lisbon, Portugal" normally means CLGeocoder, which sends the coordinates to Apple's servers and is rate limited. For an app whose promise is that your library never leaves the phone, and which has to do it for thousands of photos, that was a non-starter. We replaced it with one bundled binary file, memory-mapped, and plain geometry. This is how it works.

Reading 4 MB into arrays at launch is wasteful when a lookup touches a few pages. NSData with .alwaysMapped lets the OS page the file in on demand:

Step 5 is where a naive nearest-city lookup goes wrong: a village next to a big city should usually read as the city, and a photo on the far side of a border should not. Scaling by population fixes most of it.

Photos taken in one afternoon share almost the same coordinate. A small dictionary keyed by a quantised coordinate answers repeats without redoing the polygon tests, behind an NSLock. The work runs on its own low-priority queue in chunks, never on the main thread, and is safe to repeat: an item that was read is not read again.

Do not store coordinates. Store a small packed number per photo (which state, which city, which zone) plus the data version and the rules version. Ours also never reaches widgets, notifications, Live Activities, share sheets or logs: log lines carry counts and timings only. A setting turns the whole thing off, and when off nothing is computed or kept.

The build script has its own reference implementation of the same matcher and writes places-golden.tsv: coordinates and expected results. The Swift unit test, which runs on a Mac with plain Foundation, compares your implementation to it. Any change to a radius or penalty bumps a matchRulesVersion, and the golden file is regenerated in the same commit.

These are why the UI says "Near" instead of pretending to precision.

Once you can name a place on the device, a library becomes browsable by country, region and city, and search ("Lisbon") can use it, without a single request leaving the phone. That is the point of doing it offline.
