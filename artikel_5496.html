Many workers break rules to help customers. Their bosses punish them anyway

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Most managers punish everyone the same way for breaking a rule. Employees step out of line for four reasons, and only one is personal gain

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s Leadership newsletter. Sign up here to get the latest leadership news and insights straight to your inbox.

Plenty of managers write up an employee as soon as they find out he or she broke a rule. They handle every case the same way because it seems fair, and they want the rest of the team to see that nobody gets a pass. And many of them assume the worker was only looking out for himself or herself.

Some employees do put themselves first. Others bend a rule to help a customer, cave to pressure from coworkers or a deadline, or follow their profession's ethics over a company policy, according to a review of more than 250 studies by Oxford's Michael Gill. Gill has watched a lot of managers treat all four kinds of employee alike.

"If your first move is to punish someone, you may be solving the immediate issue but missing the real cause," Gill told Harvard Business Review. "And that can hurt the business in the long term." Once the boss files the write-up, he or she never hears what the employee was trying to do. He or she also never learns whether others on the team have been making the same call for the same reason.

Front-line workers will often bend a company rule to help a customer. In an experiment by Su Kyung (Irene) Kim of the University of British Columbia and Yujie Zhan of Wilfrid Laurier University, service staff answered voicemails for a pretend children's dance school. One message came from a mother with two kids in a Monday class. She had missed a deadline by a few days, and her bill had gone up $142. She asked to keep last year's rate, and more than half the staff let her pay it.

Kim and Zhan also had retail clerks, nurses, hotel workers, and others describe the last time a customer wanted something their company didn't allow. Most had said yes anyway. Afterward, they felt more in control of their work and closer to the people they served. A month later, they were less worn out and liked their jobs more. Those clerks and nurses are often exactly the people a business can least afford to lose.

If a boss punishes everyone the same way, the employees most willing to help customers take the same hit as the ones cheating the company. So they stop helping, and the rule stays in place.

When everyone on a team keeps doing something they've been told not to do, Gill doesn't start by blaming them. "If a rule has been broken over and over again and that is an issue," he said, "then that speaks to a more fundamental problem about the rule itself rather than the rule breakers."

Managers write instructions for the job as it is at the time. Then the work changes, and nobody goes back to update them, Gill said. Front-line staff notice first. They're the ones who have to tell customers no, but many quietly start saying yes.

They usually know how the job should be done instead. Workers feel especially capable after going against instructions they already think make no sense, according to Kim and Zhan. They also suggest more ways to improve service afterward. They're the first people a leader should ask.

They won't say much, though, if they suspect they're about to be disciplined. "If your people believe they are in trouble, they'll shut down," Gill told HBR, "but if they think you're trying to understand what happened before coming to your final conclusions, they are more likely to open up." Once staff decide the boss doesn't care about their reasons, some follow the rulebook to the letter and stop making their own calls. Others keep doing the job their own way and make sure nobody finds out. In both cases, the boss never learns what needs to change.

Asking why doesn't mean letting everyone off. Gill said employees tend to explain themselves right away if they have a good reason, and say much less if they're looking out for themselves. He thinks managers should discipline workers who break a rule for their benefit, as long as they know what they're doing and what it could cost them. For everyone else, he said, the boss should start by explaining what's at stake for the company.

Some rules are worth keeping even when breaking them would help a customer. Delinea, a San Francisco cybersecurity company, sells through resellers, and they decide what buyers pay. Its own employees aren't supposed to talk price at all. Some answered customers' questions about cost on the spot anyway, instead of making them wait, CEO Art Gilliland told HBR. But if a Delinea employee names a figure, the company can break regulations and face price-fixing claims. Even though everyone involved was trying to help, they still had to stop answering those questions.

Gilliland responded by asking how often an employee gave a customer a number. If the answer was once, a manager would explain why Delinea leaves pricing to its resellers and coach the person on what to say next time. Usually, nobody needs a second talk. "If someone continues breaking a rule after coaching and support, eventually you must make the call to let them go," Gilliland said.

Gilliland blames the person when one employee keeps quoting prices after being shown the right way. Gill, by contrast, looks at the company's instructions once a whole team stops following them. Before rewriting a rule or letting someone go, a leader has to know whether one person is ignoring it or everyone is.

A CEO only hears about either problem if someone speaks up, and in many companies nobody does. Employees often keep quiet when they see a coworker break a rule, and managers rarely tell their own bosses. Gill would give staff an anonymous way to speak up, or someone neutral to go to. He also wants senior leaders to spend some time doing front-line work themselves. A rule that seems obvious from the executive floor can look very different behind the counter.
