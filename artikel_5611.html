5 snowy North American ski areas, from Alyeska to Little Cottonwood Canyon

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A regional snowfall roundup points to Alaska, British Columbia, Washington state, Utah, and California for deep powder.

Skiers start watching the forecast before the lifts begin turning, waiting for the first storm of the season to reset the mountains. A fresh powder day tops the wish list for many skiers, the sport's closest thing to a sacred prize. Snow decides whether a trip delivers soft, untracked turns or days on thin, worn cover, and no amount of planning can conjure it where the sky withholds it. Anyone picking a mountain for a vacation, a season pass, or a weekend drive has a stake in knowing where the flakes pile highest. The choice of destination sets the odds before anyone boards a chairlift.

Geography governs where those rare mornings arrive. Western North America collects the heaviest average totals on the continent. Still, conditions shift from one year to the next, and a resort that buries its slopes in one can run lean in the following. Two kinds of numbers capture the pattern. A long-run average shows what a visitor can expect in a typical year, while a one-year total reveals how high a peak can climb when the weather cooperates. Both matter to a traveler weighing where to spend money and time off. A resort with a strong average offers dependability. A resort with a towering record year marks the ceiling of what its terrain can hold. Together, the two figures sketch how much natural snowpack a place tends to supply and how wide its swings can grow.

OnTheSnow assembled a region-by-region tour of North American snowfall, moving from Alaska through Canada and the western U.S. before turning to the Midwest and the East. The roundup pairs historical annual means with standout single-winter accumulations, and it reports most values in inches, many of them as approximations or ranges. The piece offers no numbered ranking. It leaves out who took the measurements, which winters the means cover, and how anyone calculated them. The five entries below form a selection from that tour. Each one illustrates a separate way a region earns its standing in the hunt for new snow. Treat them as a field guide to reliable territory, and remember that any one winter can stray from the norm by a broad margin.

Alyeska collects around 670 inches of snow in a typical year, the heaviest average of any ski area in North America. The resort sits in Girdwood, 40 miles from Anchorage. The short distance turns the deepest snowfall on the continent into a day trip for Anchorage residents.

Alyeska's totals have climbed as high as 848 inches, a peak that outstrips its usual haul by a wide margin. The resort topped 700 inches during the 2023-24 ski season, a total the guide cautions Alyeska does not reach every year. Even that banner winter landed closer to the average than to the high-water mark. The top figure shows how far one exceptional season can stretch beyond a baseline that beats every other ski area on the continent. A visitor who plans around the norm stands on firmer ground than one chasing the peak.

Skiers who want natural snow they can count on, and plenty of it, should book a trip to Alyeska, the guide advises. The roundup delivers that advice alongside its caution about 700-inch years. The warning and the recommendation sit together without contradiction. The guide's endorsement leans on the long-run average. One quiet season does little to dent it. Other resorts may match Alyeska in a single big year, yet the guide reserves its promise of reliable snowfall for this one.

Alyeska offers 1,600 skiable acres, terrain the tour describes as rich in steep pitches and deep powder. Girdwood opens the way into Alaska's Chugach Mountains, which hold more than 700,000 acres of backcountry skiing. The lift-served ground forms a sliver of the wild country within reach of town. Skiers who outgrow the marked runs have a vast playground waiting in the surrounding peaks beyond the resort boundary. Girdwood functions as a base camp for an entire range, with Alyeska serving as its front door.

British Columbia owns Canada's single-season snowfall record, set when Revelstoke buried its slopes under 80 feet during the 1971-1972 ski season. The province's ordinary winters help explain how a mark of that size could form. Its snowiest resorts pile up hundreds of inches in a typical year. A heavy winter there builds on a high floor.

Revelstoke's normal season brings 400-500 inches. Converted to feet, the upper end of that range sits far below the 80 recorded in 1971-1972. A skier who books an average winter here meets a deep base. The record year marks how far past its usual haul the mountain can climb.

Whistler Blackcomb gathers 465 inches in an average season, a figure inside Revelstoke's range. Two of the province's resorts draw on similar supplies of powder. Snow totals give a visitor little reason to pick one over the other. Other priorities can settle the choice.

Revelstoke holds the record even though its range overlaps Whistler Blackcomb's figure. A national mark can emerge from one exceptional winter at a resort whose usual totals match its neighbor's. The 1971-1972 season shows British Columbia's ceiling towering over its norm.

The SkiBig3 resorts in Alberta receive about half the snow that British Columbia's snowiest mountains collect. The drop happens across a single provincial border. A skier who heads east trades the province's deepest terrain for a thinner supply. The province a skier picks shapes the snow outlook as much as the resort does.

Mt. Baker in Washington state piled up 1,140 inches of snow during the 1998-99 season, a world record for a single winter. The haul converts to 95 feet, and no ski area on the planet has matched it in one season. The total outstrips every annual average the roundup lists, including that of the continent's snowiest ski area.

Mt. Baker's summit tops out at 5,089 feet, a modest height for the holder of a global snowfall title. The peak stands less than a mile high, yet no taller mountain on Earth has topped its single-winter accumulation. The roundup cites that elevation as evidence that a mountain can amass enormous totals without soaring into the clouds. Altitude, by this measure, offers a weak forecast of how much snow a peak will catch. Skiers who equate tall summits with deep powder would overlook the place that set the mark.

Ocean air supplies what elevation does not. Moist currents drifting in from the Pacific run into the Cascade Range, which shoves them upward. The forced climb wrings heavy precipitation from the clouds over the Cascade Mountains of Washington state. Terrain in that path needs no great stature to gather a staggering load of snow. Its supply arrives from the sea.

Crystal Mountain and Stevens Pass in Washington state, along with Mt. Bachelor and Timberline in Oregon, each average well over 400 inches a season. Four separate resorts clearing that mark show the region's snowfall does not hinge on a single outlier. The depth spans two states and hands a powder hunter several targets in one stretch of the map. Mt. Baker's 1998-99 winter, set beside those neighbors, shows how far one season can overshoot a generous norm. A traveler who misses a banner year at one resort still lands in a corridor where deep seasonal averages are the rule.

Alta averages 548 inches of snow a season, and Snowbird, its next-door neighbor, takes in more than 500. Both figures describe a typical winter, before any record storms enter the picture. The two areas turn a single Utah canyon into a dependable source of deep powder.

The 2022-23 winter blew past those norms. Alta closed the season at 903 inches, and Snowbird broke its all-time record with 838. Several Utah resorts set new highs that year. The surge reached beyond one canyon, which points to heavy snowfall across much of the state.

Park City Mountain Resort and Deer Valley, both in Summit County, see 300-350 inches a year. Their totals trail Little Cottonwood Canyon by about 200 inches. The Summit County resorts sit about 1,500 feet lower, and that altitude difference accounts for part of the shortfall. A skier weighing Utah options can expect more snow by trading Summit County for the higher ground of Little Cottonwood Canyon.

Brighton and Solitude, two nearby Utah resorts, each receive about 500 inches a year. Both land close to the canyon's marks, putting four mountains at similar totals. Utah has long claimed the greatest snow on Earth, and a cluster of resorts near 500 inches gives that boast real weight.

Avalanche paths cross the most direct stretch of road between Snowbird and Alta and close the route more often than ice or collisions do. One slide broke loose from Mount Superior, crossed the canyon road, and reached Snowbird's Chickadee trail while skiers and snowboarders were on it. Ski patrol later declared that the slide had buried no one.

An interlodge at Alta confines everyone in an affected area to a permanent building, and no one may step outdoors until the order lifts. A day in Little Cottonwood Canyon can hinge on whether the road and the doors stay open.

Mammoth Mountain finished the 2022-23 season with close to 900 inches of snowfall at its summit, more than twice the 400 inches the resort gets in a typical winter. At the Main Lodge, the base tally reached 700 inches. Even the lower reading dwarfed the resort's usual total.

Only the summit measurement cleared the doubling mark. The base fell short of it, though 700 inches pushed the lodge area past its normal output by a wide margin. Skiers who stayed on lower runs saw a year beyond the norm, and those who rode to the top found a thicker pack.

Palisades Tahoe traced the same arc that year. The resort shares Mammoth's 400-inch baseline, and it topped 700 inches during 2022-23. Two California mountains that start from identical yearly expectations both cleared that mark in one season. The parallel totals suggest the storms behind them spread across more than a single corner of the state.

Sugar Bowl, a neighbor of Palisades Tahoe, collects 450 to 500 inches in an ordinary year. Even the low end of that range beats what Mammoth or Palisades Tahoe can expect from a standard winter. A traveler picking a California mountain by typical totals alone would lean toward Sugar Bowl.

Mammoth's Main Lodge and Palisades Tahoe each closed 2022-23 above the top of Sugar Bowl's yearly range. The two resorts with the smaller baseline posted figures that outstripped the best a normal year brings to their snowier neighbor. Mammoth's summit reading opened a wider lead over that benchmark.

Sugar Bowl's bigger yearly expectation makes it the stronger pick on paper for an average winter. Mammoth and Palisades Tahoe demonstrated in 2022-23 that a lower norm caps nothing once a heavy year rolls in. A smaller average sets a lower floor for an ordinary trip, and Mammoth's summit showed how little it limits the ceiling.
