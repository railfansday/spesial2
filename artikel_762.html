Does Georgia Still Want to Be the Hollywood of the South?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

At a time when the fight for productions is fierce, new governor Keisha Lance Bottoms faces a major question - and challenge.

By Steven Zeitchik, Ronda Racha Penrice November 18, 2026 10:46am

A few years ago, if you wanted to shoot a movie in Georgia, you’d better have made your reservations early.

The lack of an annual cap on credit payouts, the solid 30 percent rebate, the allowance of (some) above-the-line expenses, and the lower cost of housing compared with New York and California made it so that every inch of the state’s 21st century studio space and hotel inventory could get booked up.

MacGyver, Ozark, Stranger Things, The Resident and nearly every significant Marvel TV and film project were all among those calling Georgia home even when many of their stories were set elsewhere. And then there was Tyler Perry’s Fort McPherson studio and its 50,000 square feet of permanent sets, which opened in 2019 and housed numerous projects. The state’s film and TV growth was astronomical — over barely a dozen years beginning in 2010, studio square footage went from 45,000 all the way to 3 million.

But while Perry continues to book productions at his landmark location, Hollywood activity has thinned markedly in the Peach State since 2022. The Georgia Film Office reports that production spend has cratered from a peak of $4.4 billion in 2022 to $2.3 billion in 2025 — hardly peanuts (Will Trent, The Rock’s Lizard Music and Judd Apatow’s The Comeback King have been among recent shoots) but a major fall. Total productions saw a similar drop, from 412 to 245.

And the numbers continue to sink. Industry tracker ProdPro says that Georgia saw about $1.56 billion in film and TV production spend in the 12 months ending in September, down 15 percent from $1.84 billion in the prior 12-month period.

There have been other worrisome developments. Lee Thomas, longtime head of the Georgia Film Office and the major force behind the surge, announced her retirement this summer. Her replacement has not been named. And then in early November, another blow: James Gunn, who famously loved Georgia and brought DC projects like Peacemaker and Superman to the sprawling Trillith Studios in Fayetteville, was ousted as head of the superhero division under new Skydance ownership.

Gunn’s love affair with Georgia runs deep; he shot the first two Guardians of the Galaxy movies for Marvel and even owned a home there. The director’s big-budget Superman sequel Man of Tomorrow alone, which shot in the state this summer, gave Georgia its best production spend quarter for new shoots since early 2024, ProdPro notes. But his successor, Jeremy Latcham, and Latcham’s boss, David Ellison, could shop for preferred alternatives with less allegiance to the past.

Of course, there’s an overall decline in streamer and studio projects in the post-strike and -COVID era. And there have been bright spots even in Georgia, such as reeling in American Idol from Los Angeles to shoot its upcoming season in the state. But overall the news has been dark.

One big culprit of the Georgia drop? Generous tax credits overseas. A prominent TV producer says the location conversation with studios these days always starts with Budapest, then moves to Ireland before going to England. Marvel itself largely decamped for the U.K. in 2024. That puts Georgia in a tough spot.

This could change if a federal tax credit can gain momentum and pass in the new congressional session. But such a passage is far from guaranteed, and it still might not induce projects to come home. And New York is proving a formidable American rival to Georgia, seeing gains in many key metrics, with plenty of studio space being built in New Jersey to augment the effort.

Georgia’s lame-duck Republican governor and legislature are unlikely to follow through on longtime threats to reduce the credit before leaving office next month. But whether a new Democratic state house and new Democratic Gov. Keisha Lance Bottoms can or will reenergize the business remains an open question. One question: the new film commissioner could work under Pat Wilson, the state’s longtime commissioner of economic development — or Bottoms could find someone to replace Wilson. (Thomas will do some contract work in the meantime.)

A Georgia unraveling would spell more than an employment loss. Georgia crews tend to be more diverse than those in New York and Los Angeles, in particular with many Black women occupying positions they don’t hold in other places. And when the new soap opera Beyond the Gates won the Daytime Emmy for lighting direction in October, the winners were both Black men, a rarity in the category.

One encouraging sign for Georgia crews and the state’s Hollywood allies: Perry’s place in the Lance Bottoms administration. He was named to her transition team right after the election. But she has downplayed what that meant. “I think what people miss,” she told the popular morning program Yung Joc & The Streetz Morning Takeover when a host asked about the Perry appointment, “is that these are just co-chairs … we’ll probably have 100 more.” Lance Bottoms could have other priorities for tax money than putting it in producers’ pockets; the state recently ranked 49th in health care in one prominent annual study.

During his successful reelection bid, Democratic senator Jon Ossoff faced criticism from opponent Mike Collins that he was more concerned with Hollywood, California than [the small town of] Hollywood, Georgia, a criticism Lance Bottoms could be sensitive to as well.

And Perry himself is actually about to move to Puerto Rico to be closer to his son. He says activity at his studio will continue unabated, but what this all means for him and his Georgia advocacy remains to be seen. On the red carpet for his Tyler Perry’s The Gospel of Christmas recently he didn’t seem especially eager to talk about the credit. Asked about its future, he told THR, “Well as great as that’s been [and it] has been amazing, for me, my focus is being pro-Georgia, and Keisha is pro-Georgia. So I want to make sure that businesses, people are taken care of.”

The state’s Hollywood advocates could take comfort in at least one fact: AI hasn’t cut into physical production in Georgia — yet.
