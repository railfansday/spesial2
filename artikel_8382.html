Sure, I get the 'look at longest data' thing, but I am not quite sure that pre-Volcker efforts to promote employment by lowering rates even if it resulted in inflation is relevant after finding that employment is actually better fostered by having stable inflation because it allows companies to plan better.

Anyway, given their statement that:

As an aside: The examples thus far show why you need to use the longest available period of data when examining historical bond returns, and not s
hort, cherry-picked time spans. Be wary of those who ignore that imperative.

I wonder why they also say:

To avoid the complications posed by the deflation of the 1930s and the yield curve
control exercised by the Fed during the war years, we show only the post-1945 data.

I mean if we can assume that deflation won't happen again and we can assume that the Fed won't use yield curve control again, they why can't we assume the pre-Volcker policies won't happen again?

It just seems logically inconsistent but yeah, they favor some assumptions but not others. Ok, but surely it colors the results by omitting a massive deflationary depression in which duration would be a very desirable asset. Of course policy reactions to massive depressions are different today, so fine leave it out. But then again, policy reactions post-Volcker are different too.

And if we were being really honest, the rising rates of the "Forgotten Bear Market" are an effect of the yield control and artificially low borrowing rates used to fund the war effort.

Anyway, I'll guess the most likely future is debt-induced inflation and yield control to keep borrowing rates low. That of course affects currency value. AI might raise productivity to offset that but deflation is likely not going to happen (I mean so of course my guess is wrong and it will ...). Japan just crushed its currency with yield control to raise inflation. Using yield control to keep borrowing costs down will have the same currency effect. If several high-debt countries tried to simultaneously keep borrowing rates low, emerging markets and real assets would be the place to be.

(I truly do find the warning against cherry-picking to be hilarious given that an era they deliberately exclude - yield control - may be our most likely future to keep borrowing costs down. While it may not change the conclusion about duration, I do think it has implications about equities and how much of what to hold.)
