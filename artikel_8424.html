I'll ignore whether this is a good decision since you didn't really ask for advise there and others are covering it. Your comparison of 10% once vs 7% a year is missing some nuance. To illustrate I will assume the 2 options you are considering are: 1 take a penalty to withdraw from 401k or 2 take mortgage, then divert all retirement savings above the match to paying it off each year. I think this second option best aligns with the wiki page order of investments guidance.

Firstly, instead of 10%, what matters is the marginal tax rate difference of pulling all the money out in 1 year vs spreading the tax over multiple years with no penalty (cash flowing the mortgage payments by reducing retirement savings). This may still be just the 10% penalty if you are solidly in the 24% bracket and will be for the foreseeable future, or it could be more if you are able to realize some of the income to pay off the mortgage in the 22% or even 12% bracket in the future. There's also state taxes and NIIT and the whole rest of the tax code that could change your effective rate on this extra taxable income each year, and/or on the lump sum option.

Secondly, the yearly rate to compare against is not the full 7%, but rather the amount that 7% is larger than whatever you're investments in your retirement account are earning. Let's assume that's short term treasuries earning 4.5%, so really it's only 2.5% you lose out on with the mortgage. The other wrinkle in this side of the comparison is that the principal is shrinking as you pay the mortgage, so the faster you pay it off the less time there is for the interest over time to catch up to the initial penalty in scenario 1.

There's no easy formula to compare these options, so I'd suggest you make a spreadsheet showing the results of the 2 scenarios so you can play with the parameters and see how changing them impacts the decision. In scenario 2 you start with a 400k mortgage and 606k in 401k. Each time step (year or month) you add in the mortgage interest, pay off some mortgage with whatever you choose for a cash flow amount (after tax), and add earnings to the 401k. In the scenario 1 column you start with an empty 401k and add the pre tax cash flow to it each time step. Compare the 401k balances when the mortgage is paid off, whichever is larger at the time is the better choice. Change parameters to see sensitivity to your assumptions.

I made a quick spreadsheet assuming you divert your full 60k yearly retirement amount toward the mortgage and no additional tax rate arbitrage. Mortgage was paid off in roughly 14 years and the 401k balances were basically a wash. Paying off faster favors the mortgage option.
