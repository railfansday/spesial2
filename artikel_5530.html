Zillow’s Preview program changes who sees listings first, here’s why it matters

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Zillow Preview, launched March 2026, gives Zillow first access to pre market listings via deals with 400 plus brokerages. The article argues the arrangement, alongside a Realtor.com tie up and about 75% portal traffic share, warrants antitrust review.  AI Summary

This spring, Zillow assembled something the real estate industry has not seen before: exclusive, pay-to-play access to the freshest inventory in home sales. Through its Preview program, launched in March 2026, more than 400 brokerages and franchisors agree to display their pre-market listings on Zillow first, before they reach the Multiple Listing Service (MLS) — the information source that feeds competing platforms.

On the seller side, the contracted participants include household names like Keller Williams, REMAX and HomeServices of America. On the buyer side, Zillow extended the arrangement to Realtor.com, its closest competitor in traffic. This tangled web of exclusivity channels the market’s most attractive home sales listings to consumers exclusively through channels that Zillow owns or monetizes.

Whether this setup in the multi-trillion dollar home sales market complies with antitrust laws is a question federal and state enforcers have not yet asked. They should. And the question is timely, given that federal and state antitrust enforcers have just settled a case against Zillow over the actions it apparently took to push a competitor out of the rental listings market.

In the rental-market case, the FTC and five state attorneys general alleged that Zillow paid its competitor Redfin $100 million to stop advertising rental listings. The settlement requires Redfin to rebuild its rental advertising business. But Zillow gets to retain the syndication partnership, the customer relationships and the scale the deal produced which, advocates note leaves Zillow profiting from the behavior it has now agreed to cease.

Zillow’s arrangements in the residential sales market — where 4 million homes changed hands last year — raise additional distinct questions that no regulator has yet examined.

Start with the Preview program itself. Under these agreements, pre-market listings appear only on Zillow and its affiliates, and on the participating brokerage’s own website, before reaching the MLS. Indeed, in any sector, a dominant firm signing up hundreds of suppliers to exclusive early access should raise concerns.

Zillow says the program brings pre-market listings into the open rather than hiding them in agents’ pockets, and that argument is not frivolous. But Zillow is monetizing every pageview, not just via the Preview program itself, but also via a range of downstream revenue opportunities.

Adding Realtor.com into the mix amplifies the power of this arrangement. The two portals accurately describe themselves as the nation’s largest real estate websites; third-party data indicates they collectively account for approximately 75% of major U.S. real estate portal traffic. Giving them exclusive access leaves rivals such as Homes.com and Redfin without the listings that generate the most valuable consumer engagement. Whether that arrangement amounts to an unlawful agreement among competitors to allocate valuable inventory is precisely the kind of question antitrust enforcers exist to answer.

Zillow reports 235 million average monthly unique users, and by industry estimates, agents using at least one Zillow product touch an estimated 80% of residential transactions. For a brokerage weighing whether to sign an exclusive pre-market agreement, the practical realities of declining an offer from a platform of that size deserve attention, particularly when Zillow pays listing agents a share of the buyer-side commission when Zillow-aligned agents close deals off Preview listings.

Finally, a stack of court filings alleges that Zillow uses its platform to divert consumer inquiries away from listing agents and toward buyer’s agents who pay Zillow for leads — and who face incentives to steer those consumers toward Zillow Home Loans, too. An independent study found that Zillow’s mortgages cost borrowers approximately $4,600 more on average than comparable loans elsewhere.

These allegations are contested, and the courts will sort them out. But if they hold up, they would describe a business in which control of pre-market listings feeds control of leads, and control of leads feeds control of financing. That possibility is one more reason the listing arrangements deserve attention on their own.

Antitrust law has long been concerned with exclusive arrangements that deny rivals access to critical inputs, and with agreements among competitors that restrain trade — as in the commission-sharing arrangements that led to a $418 million settlement with the National Association of Realtors. Whether Zillow’s Preview program and the Realtor.com partnership fit those patterns is a question of national consequence, in a market that touches twice as many families as the rental market the enforcers just addressed.

Federal and state enforcers should investigate. Private litigants should take note as well; they showed in the NAR buyer-broker commission litigation that antitrust suits can reshape the real estate industry. The place to start is with questions, and the facts on the public record make a strong case for asking them.

Carter Dougherty is Senior Fellow for Antimonopoly and Finance at Demand Progress, a think tank and advocacy group.
