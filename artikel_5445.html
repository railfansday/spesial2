The best and worst cities for renters' bargaining power

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Zumper graded 44 U.S. rental markets on renter bargaining power. See how 16 of them, from Austin to San Francisco, fared in June 2026.

A lease renewal notice tends to arrive as a decree, with a new number printed and a deadline to sign. Most tenants absorb the increase, haggle over a fresh coat of paint, or start packing. Walking away carries its own bill in movers, deposits, and lost days. The balance of power at the signing table can decide whether a household keeps a cushion of savings or watches it drain. Across much of the U.S., the scales have tipped toward tenants since 2023. In June 2023, not one of 44 major rental markets earned an A for tenant leverage, and four managed a B. Three years later, three held an A and 16 held a B.

The stretch from 2023 to mid-2026 coincides with the largest wave of new apartment construction in decades. Over the same period, asking prices cooled and incentives multiplied. In the average city, yearly growth in one-bedroom rents swung from +3.7% to -1.8%. The share of large complexes advertising at least a month free climbed from 10% in June 2023 to 21% in June 2026. The relief proved uneven. Twenty-nine of the 44 improved their standing, and three slipped. What a household can win now depends on its city.

Zumper built its Renter Leverage Index to measure how much negotiating power renters hold, grading 44 major U.S. cities from A to F. The authors, Crystal Chen and Quentin Proctor, tracked every month from January 2023 through June 2026, a span of 42 months, and treat June 2026 as the headline month because it carries the latest Census vacancy data. Six signals feed each score. Four come from the company's listing data: the yearly change in median one-bedroom asking rent, the portion of large complexes offering a month or more free, the fraction of listings carrying price cuts, and the percentage of new units still available after 30 days. The U.S. Census Bureau supplies the other two, metro rental vacancy and new housing permits per 1,000 residents. An A means renters hold the most cards, and an F hands them to property owners. Each comparison sets June 2026 against June 2023. This article examines 16 of those cities, the ones the authors singled out for striking numbers plus several big coastal anchors for contrast, arranged by where each stood in June 2026.

Austin renters hold more leverage than tenants in any of the other 43 graded markets, and the median one-bedroom asking rent shows why, falling 12.5% in a year to $1,330. The decline trims $190 from the monthly bill against a $1,520 median a year earlier. Over a 12-month lease, the savings reach $2,280. Cheaper rent helped lift the city from a B in June 2023 to an A in June 2026.

Landlords have stacked giveaways on top of cheaper sticker prices. Some 42% of large apartment communities advertise at least a month free, a perk that drags the effective cost of a lease below its posted figure. Markdowns appear on another 8% of active listings, evidence that some owners keep cutting after a unit goes live.

Apartments in Austin sit unclaimed longer than in any other city tracked. Of new listings, 53% remain available 30 days after they appear, the highest share in the study. Each idle month costs an owner a month of income, and a prospect who watches a listing age has grounds to push for a better deal.

Austin's surplus stretches across the metro, where 16.9% of rental homes stand empty, a rate only San Antonio matches in the index. New upscale high-rise towers have reshaped the Rainey Street corridor, one visible piece of the local building wave. Beyond the core, affordability pressures have pushed most of the region's single-family construction into suburbs and exurbs outside city limits.

Permits in Austin have thinned since 2022. The city permitted 19.4 homes per 1,000 residents in the year to June 2022, then 12 in the year to June 2025 and 8.9 in the year to June 2026. Annual approvals shrank 54% across those four years. Today's bargains rest on units already built, and with approvals sliding, fewer new apartments are lined up to replenish the surplus.

Denver landlords cut the asking price on 16% of active listings in June 2026, a share only San Jose exceeded among the 44 cities in the index. The markdowns arrived alongside a 7.6% annual decline in the median one-bedroom rent, which now stands at $1,570. Denver's one-bedroom remains pricier than Nashville's, at $1,500, and Austin's, at $1,330. Over three years, the city climbed from a C to an A, a two-grade leap. Austin and Nashville, the other two A markets, each started from a B and moved up one step.

Free months show up more often in Denver than in any other city the study tracks. At 48% of large apartment communities, Denver's month-free offers outpaced the other 43 cities and ran more than double the study-wide rate. Nashville landlords, by comparison, offered a free month at 33% of big complexes, and Dallas landlords did so at 25%. A tenant touring large buildings in Denver could expect about half of them to dangle a free month before any haggling began.

Newly listed units in Denver lingered too, with 47% still open 30 days after they appeared, a share Nashville and Dallas matched. A unit that sits empty for a month gives a prospective tenant time to compare buildings and hold out for a better deal. Denver's landlords faced that kind of patience on close to half their fresh inventory.

Denver earned its A with a tighter physical market than its top-tier peers. The metro's rental vacancy rate stood at 7.3%, less than half of Austin's 16.9% and below Nashville's 9.6%. Dallas, with 11.7% of its rentals empty, finished a step lower at B. Permits ran at 10.2 homes per 1,000 residents, about half of Austin's 20.9 and short of Nashville's 16.7. Fewer permits per resident point to less incoming supply competing for Denver tenants than in Austin or Nashville.

Of the three A markets, Denver relies most on landlord concessions and least on empty apartments to tilt lease terms toward tenants.

Nashville's median one-bedroom asking rent fell 7.4% over the past year to $1,500, a cut that helped carry the city from a B in June 2023 to an A in June 2026. Austin and Denver alone finished ahead of it among the 44 markets, and all three now hold the top grade.

Nashville entered the period with an edge over most of the country. In June 2023 it shared a B with Austin, Dallas, and Portland, Oregon. Its climb of a full letter shows tenant gains compounding in a place where apartment seekers already enjoyed more sway than in most cities.

Idle units hand Nashville house hunters much of their footing. Vacancy stands at 9.6%, which leaves about one in 10 rental homes without an occupant. Nearly half of new listings, 47%, still sit unclaimed 30 days after they appear. Landlords there cannot count on filling a unit within a few weeks. Asking prices slipped on 11% of active listings, another sign that owners are competing for lease signers.

Nashville's metro authorized 16.7 housing units per 1,000 residents, the third-highest pace among the 44 cities, behind Raleigh and Austin. Permitting at this level signals more apartments on the way. Janelle Gallagher, a commercial real estate professional at CBRE, told CNBC in 2023 that Nashville had been drawing a growing mix of businesses in professional services, including law firms, banks, tech, automotive, and health care, alongside its music and entertainment industry. Sliding prices and empty apartments suggest new construction has outrun that demand.

Nashville landlords lean on move-in giveaways less than their counterparts in the other two A-grade cities, with 33% of large apartment communities offering at least a month free. Lower sticker prices and slow-filling units, more than concessions, explain the city's position. A Nashville apartment hunter stands to gain more by pressing on the monthly bill than by holding out for a free month.

Dallas held the same B grade in June 2026 that it carried three years earlier, and an 11.7% rental vacancy rate shows why tenants there keep real sway. No other market holding a B or better in June 2026 kept its June 2023 mark. Dallas shared that early B with Austin, Nashville, and Portland, Oregon. Austin and Nashville have since climbed to an A, and Portland slid to a C. Dallas stood firm as many other cities rose toward it.

The Dallas metro's share of empty units exceeds that of every market in this selection except Austin, San Antonio, and Tucson. Houston, a Texas market outside this selection, posts a higher rate at 12.9% and a steeper one-year rent decline of 14.1%. Dallas finished six places ahead of Houston, fourth against 10th, a sign that empty units and falling asking prices need backing from other measures to lift a city's standing.

Dallas landlords ask $1,350 for a median one-bedroom, 6.9% less than in June 2025, a drop of close to $100 a month. The Dallas median sits $20 above Austin's, leaving a basic apartment in the two Texas cities at near parity. Houston's median comes in $250 below the Dallas figure.

Free-month offers expose the gap between Dallas and the A-grade cities. Landlords advertise at least a month free at 25% of large communities, the thinnest share among the 11 markets in this selection earning a B or better. Price cuts appear on 10% of Dallas listings, and 47% of new units remain open 30 days after they hit the market. Owners there trim asking prices and absorb slower lease-ups, yet they ration free months.

The Dallas metro permitted 15.6 homes per 1,000 residents, the third-fastest pace among the 16 markets here, behind Austin and Nashville. Dallas tenants who want a better deal stand to gain more by pressing landlords on price than by waiting for a free month.

Charlotte apartments linger on the market longer than in any other graded city except Austin, with 51% of new units still listed 30 days after they appear. Austin's share reaches 53%. Fewer than half of new units leave the listings within a month, and that sluggish pace helped carry Charlotte from a C in June 2023 to a B in June 2026, good for sixth place among the 44 markets.

Property owners in Charlotte lean on perks more than on lower sticker prices. The median one-bedroom asking price slipped 1.4% to $1,460, a mild dip beside the double-digit declines in Austin and San Antonio. The 1.4% decline shaves about $21 off the monthly median. Concessions run deeper. Some 34% of large apartment communities advertise at least a month free, and 14% of listings carry a price cut. Among the 16 cities in this article, only Denver and San Jose show larger shares of marked-down listings.

Charlotte keeps building at a brisk clip. The metro permitted 15.1 homes per 1,000 residents, close to triple the pace in New York or San Jose and in the same range as Dallas and Nashville. Rental vacancy across the metro stands at 7.2%, a pool of empty homes that gives house hunters room to shop. Raleigh, the only other North Carolina city in the index, made the same climb from C to B while permitting 23.8 homes per 1,000 residents, the fastest pace among all 44 cities. Its median one-bedroom costs $1,290, or $170 less than Charlotte's.

Charlotte tenants gain the most ground by bargaining over concessions. Base asking prices have moved little, so haggling over the monthly figure may yield less. A free month offers a clearer payoff. At the $1,460 median, one free month on a 12-month lease works out to about $1,338 a month, a $122 discount worth close to six times the yearly price slide.

San Antonio's rental vacancy rate climbed from 5.5% in June 2023 to 16.9% in June 2026, tripling over three years and tying Austin for the highest share of empty units among the 44 markets. About one in six metro rentals now stands empty. Owners carrying that much idle space have ceded ground on lease terms, and the slack helped lift the city from a C to a B, good for seventh among the 44.

Landlords of big complexes answered the glut with giveaways. The share of large communities advertising at least a month free jumped from 6% to 38%, more than a sixfold increase. Asking prices slid too. The median one-bedroom dropped 10.4% to $950, about $110 a month below its level a year earlier. A free month shrinks move-in costs, and the lower median trims every payment that follows.

Listings sit long enough for house hunters to compare options. Of new postings, 46% remained available 30 days later, and 8% of active ones carried a markdown. Someone touring several buildings faces close to even odds that the first unit will still be open when the 30 days are up. Applicants gain room to weigh offers before signing, and a property manager who wants the unit filled has reason to bend.

Competition stiffens near the city's showcase districts. Luxury apartments beside concentrated developments such as Pearl and The Rim tend to fill faster than other properties, according to CoStar. Residents at The Rim have retail, dining, entertainment, hotels, and other services close by in the open-air shopping center. Bargain seekers stand a better chance of landing the deepest deals away from those amenity-heavy hubs.

Builders have pulled back. Permitting sank from 16.3 to 8.3 homes per 1,000 residents, close to half the earlier pace. Fewer approvals suggest today's concessions draw on buildings in the pipeline, with a thinner stream of projects queued behind them. Prospects chasing the current incentives are negotiating over a supply that new construction now refills at a slower clip.

Las Vegas landlords dangle at least a month of free rent at 43% of large apartment communities, a share that only Denver beats among the 44 graded markets. The giveaways helped lift the city from a C in June 2023 to a B in June 2026, good for ninth place in the current standings. Owners there appear more willing to hand out perks at signing than to reset the monthly charge on the lease.

Asking prices held close to flat over the past year. The median one-bedroom costs $1,190, down 1.6%. Among the eight cities that finished ahead of Las Vegas, only Charlotte posted a smaller decline, and only San Antonio offers a cheaper one-bedroom. A single free month on a $1,190 unit hands the tenant back a full payment, worth about 8% of a year's housing bill. The modest headline dip hides a larger discount for anyone who signs where a special applies.

Nearly half of fresh listings in Las Vegas sit unclaimed for a month. Some 49% of new postings remain available 30 days after they appear, and 12% of active listings carry a markdown. Units that linger give leasing offices a reason to sweeten deals, and the free-month offers look like their preferred tool. Markdowns reach a smaller slice of inventory, which suggests management guards the posted figure even as apartments go unrented.

Las Vegas lacks the glut of empty units that props up tenant leverage in Austin and San Antonio, with metro rental vacancy at 7.6%, less than half the rate in either Texas city. Builders pulled permits for 10.1 homes per 1,000 residents, and Denver permitted at nearly the same clip yet earned an A. Neither the vacancy rate nor the permit pace signals the kind of oversupply that forces deep cuts. Las Vegas apartment hunters stand to win more by asking about move-in specials than by haggling over base rent.

Tucson offers the cheapest way in among the 16 markets here, with a median one-bedroom asking $900 in June 2026 as 15.3% of metro rentals stood empty. Only Austin and San Antonio post higher vacancy among the 44 graded cities. A deep pool of open units and widespread move-in deals carried Tucson from a C in June 2023 to a B by June 2026.

Tucson's sticker price has held close to flat. The median slipped 1.1% over the year, a mild trim beside the double-digit declines in Austin, San Antonio, and Houston. Price cuts showed up on 9% of listings in June. Owners here have defended the posted figure and yielded ground through other channels.

Free months do most of the bargaining work in Tucson. Some 41% of large apartment communities advertised at least a month free, nearly double the share across the index. One free month on a 12-month lease at the median is worth $900, shaving about 8.3% off the year's housing bill. A discount of that size outstrips anything the drifting median has delivered.

Half of the new units posted in Tucson stayed available 30 days later, a pace that leaves applicants room to tour several buildings and weigh competing offers. Every month a unit sits idle costs its owner a month of income. A unit that has lingered for weeks points to an owner with reason to deal.

Tucson's apartment geography has shifted with student demand. Height restrictions in the city's center first steered student housing away from campus. Once new towers drew students to live near the University of Arizona, some of the older complexes farther out converted to ordinary rental apartments. Housing at the campus edge then attracted stronger demand. The conversions moved units from the student market into the general rental pool.

Tucson's surplus of open units exists without a matching construction surge. Permits came to 8.8 homes per 1,000 residents, against 14.7 in Phoenix, where vacancy held at 8%. Tucson builds less yet leaves nearly twice as large a share of rentals empty. A heavier pipeline, as Phoenix shows, does not guarantee a larger stock of vacant units.

Los Angeles renters pay a median $2,200 for a one-bedroom apartment, 4.4% less than a year ago, and the city moved from a C in June 2023 to a B in June 2026. The improvement stands out because the usual drivers of a softer market stay faint here. Rental vacancy sits at 5.1%, and builders pulled permits for 4.7 homes per 1,000 residents.

Los Angeles approves fewer new homes per resident than any of the 16 cities graded B in June 2026, and only Orlando's 3.7% vacancy undercuts its own. Both readings favor owners. Scarce openings and few new projects let landlords hold firm on terms. Supply cannot explain the city's better grade by itself.

Concessions fill the gap, with 29% of large Los Angeles apartment communities advertising a free month. Price cuts appear on 12% of listings, and half of units new to the market sit unclaimed 30 days later. Slow leasing and widespread discounts show property managers courting tenants even as few homes stand open.

San Diego charges $20 more than Los Angeles for a median one-bedroom, at $2,220, yet earned a C in June 2026, up from a D in June 2023. Its median slid 3.5% over the year, close to the Los Angeles decline, and markdowns hit 15% of its listings, a higher share than in Los Angeles. Free months and leasing speed split the two cities. Large San Diego communities offer a month free at a 15% clip, about half the Los Angeles share, and 41% of fresh listings stay available after 30 days. San Diego's 2.9% vacancy rate leaves even less slack.

Los Angeles reached a B on incentives, with no vacancy cushion or building boom behind the shift. Tenants there can push for a free month or a markdown when a lease comes up. An edge built on discounts lasts only as long as owners keep offering them, and the city's slim construction pipeline promises no surge of units to take over that role.

Miami's median one-bedroom asking rent sits 3.3% below its year-earlier level at $2,610, three years after it climbed 19.2% year over year. The swing spans 22.5 percentage points, about four times the 5.5-point shift in the average city. Miami rose from a D in June 2023 to a B in June 2026, a two-grade jump that puts it 14th among the 44 markets.

Free months, more than sticker cuts, drive Miami's turnaround. Some 37% of the city's large apartment communities advertise at least a month without charge, up from 9% in June 2023. The share has more than quadrupled. Price reductions appear on 8% of active listings. The pattern suggests Miami landlords would sooner surrender weeks of occupancy than trim the advertised figure.

Unfilled homes linger long enough in Miami to shift the balance at the leasing desk. Of fresh listings, 48% remain available 30 days after they appear. Rental vacancy stands at 9.1%. A tenant comparing two buildings can turn down the first offer and expect similar options to stay open. A request for an extra free month carries real weight in that setting.

Leverage and affordability remain separate questions in Miami. The city's one-bedroom median runs higher than in any of the other 18 markets holding an A or a B. It sits $410 above the next-priciest of them, Washington, D.C. Brickell City Centre shows what Miami's luxury tier can look like. The complex packs a five-floor mall, offices, and two upscale residential buildings, according to CNBC. Residents of its residential building have a direct path from home into the mall.

New construction plays a small part in Miami's shift. Permits run at 6 per 1,000 residents, the lowest figure among the 19 A- and B-graded markets apart from Los Angeles. Miami tenants owe their edge to idle units and concessions, and the thin permit count points to little fresh supply to extend it.

Washington, D.C. renters pay a median $2,300 for a one-bedroom, the same as a year earlier, yet the capital climbed from a C to a B between June 2023 and June 2026. Its 18th-place finish among 44 markets shows that tenants can gain bargaining power while the posted price holds steady. One-bedroom asking rents fell in 27 of the 44 cities over the same year. The District's flat median leaves its tenants with less sticker relief than renters in those cities get.

Landlords in the District hold the asking figure flat and give ground on perks. Some 36% of large apartment communities advertise a free month, outpacing the national share. On a median-priced unit, one free month returns $2,300 to the tenant, or about 8% of a year's rent. Another 10% of listings carry a markdown, and 47% of fresh units remain unclaimed 30 days after posting.

The District breaks from the pattern of the country's costliest rental markets. Its one-bedroom median is the sixth-highest of the 44 cities in the index, and among those six, only Washington, D.C. and Miami earn a B. In New York, Boston, San Francisco, and San Jose, no more than 10% of large complexes dangle a month free. New listings also stay open longer in the capital than in four of the five pricier cities, with Miami the lone exception. Tenants in Washington, D.C. face coastal-level costs with concessions closer to Austin or Las Vegas than to New York.

Washington, D.C. builds that leverage without a construction boom. The metro permitted 6.2 homes per 1,000 residents, and among the 19 markets holding a B or better, only Los Angeles and Miami authorized fewer. Rental vacancy lands at 6.2%, moderate beside the double-digit rates in Austin, Dallas, and San Antonio. Renters here draw their edge from buildings already standing, with little new supply lining up behind them.

Cincinnati climbed from an F in June 2023 to a C in June 2026, and its median one-bedroom asking rent slid 3.5% over the past year to $1,100. The decline trims about $40 from a typical monthly bill. The two-grade jump left the city 27th among the 44 markets in the index.

Slow-filling apartments hand Cincinnati tenants much of their new sway. Of newly posted units, 45% remain available 30 days after they appear, so a prospective tenant can tour several buildings before committing. Landlords cut the price on 8% of active listings, a sign that some owners would sooner shave the monthly figure than absorb an empty month.

Tight vacancy pulls the other way. The metro's rental vacancy rate stands at 2.3%, the third-lowest among all 44 cities and below every other market in this group except San Jose. Landlords with so few empty apartments face less pressure to fill space, which may help explain why Cincinnati's gains stopped at a C.

Move-in specials remain scarce. Some 14% of large apartment communities advertise a free month or more, against 25% in Columbus, the only other Ohio city in the index. Columbus also authorizes new homes at a faster clip, with permits at 12.6 per 1,000 residents compared with 6.7 in Cincinnati. Columbus' vacancy rate of 4.5% runs close to double Cincinnati's.

Columbus rose from a D to a C over the same three years, yet its median one-bedroom eased 0.8% to $1,180. Cincinnati's going rate now sits $80 below its in-state neighbor's, even though Columbus offers more specials and permits more construction. Both cities leave 45% of new units open a month after posting, so listing speed cannot explain the gap in price movement.

Cincinnati and Columbus share a C by different routes. Cincinnati's grade rests on falling asking prices, while Columbus' rests on more giveaways, more empty units, and more construction.

Richmond jumped two full grades, from an F in June 2023 to a C in June 2026, even as its median one-bedroom asking rent climbed 0.7% to $1,410. No other city among these 16 with a C or better posted a year-over-year price increase. Washington, D.C., the closest parallel, held flat. Tenants in Richmond gained their footing through perks while the sticker price kept rising.

Free months account for the most visible change. The share of large apartment communities offering a month without charge rose from 6% to 26%, more than quadrupling in three years. Richmond's current share outpaces the national figure for the same deal. A free month on a yearlong lease trims the total a leaseholder pays over the term and leaves the listed rate intact. Owners can compete for occupants without rewriting their posted prices.

Vacant apartments multiplied over the same stretch. The metro's rental vacancy rate swelled from 1.8% to 6.2%, more than triple its earlier level. In June 2023, fewer than one unit in 50 sat unoccupied, a squeeze that left owners little reason to bargain. Permits stood at 13.3 homes per 1,000 residents in the June 2026 data, a pace that points to more apartments on the way.

Sticker cuts remain rare. Price drops appear on 5% of Richmond listings, the smallest proportion among the 16 markets in this selection. Apartments also move faster here. Of fresh listings, 40% remain available after 30 days, below every other C-or-better market in this group. Zumper's authors conclude that Richmond renters can press harder for concessions than for a lower monthly price.

Virginia Beach shows what Richmond escaped. The only other Virginia market in the index held an F in both June 2023 and June 2026, and its one-bedroom median gained 7.6% to reach $1,690. None of its large complexes extends a free month, and 27% of its new listings stay open after 30 days. Its permit rate of 5.5 homes per 1,000 residents falls under half of Richmond's. Within one state, landlords handed out free months only where permits ran higher, and rent climbed in both markets.

San Jose slipped from a C in June 2023 to a D in June 2026, and its 0.5% rental vacancy rate sits below every other market among the 44 in the index. Half a percent leaves a sliver of empty homes for households hunting for a lease. Landlords with full buildings face little pressure to bargain, and the city's 40th-place finish shows how weak a hand apartment seekers hold there.

Asking rents kept climbing. The median one-bedroom reached $2,760 in June 2026, up 2.6% from a year earlier, or about $70 more each month. San Jose joined Portland and San Francisco as the only cities in the index whose grade fell between those two Junes. Portland's slide from a B to a C left it in the middle of the pack, while San Jose's drop pushed the city into the bottom tier.

Markdowns run hotter in San Jose than anywhere else in the index, with 21% of listings carrying a reduction in June 2026, five percentage points above Denver, the next-highest city. The cuts failed to pull the median down. The pattern suggests landlords open with ambitious asking figures and pare them back while the typical price keeps advancing. A reduced listing here reflects an owner adjusting an opening bid, and the rising median shows those adjustments have not reversed the upward trend.

Free-month deals are absent in San Jose. None of the large apartment communities tracked there advertised a month of free rent in June 2026, even as such offers spread across much of the country. Leasing also moves at a brisk clip. Of new units, 36% remained available after 30 days. The other 64% disappeared from listings within a month.

Construction in the San Jose metro offers little relief, with 5.6 permits per 1,000 residents, less than a quarter of the 23.8 recorded in the Raleigh metro, the most active builder in the index. Few new buildings are coming and the empty stock sits near zero, so markdowns become the main opening for bargain hunters. The leasing pace means a hesitant shopper risks losing a unit to the next applicant.

New York asks $4,660 a month for a median one-bedroom, the steepest price among the 44 cities in the index, and the figure climbed 4.0% over the past year. The increase cuts against the countrywide current, with one-bedroom rents in the average city sliding over the same stretch. A New York one-bedroom costs $600 more than one in San Francisco and $1,710 more than one in Boston, which holds the third-priciest spot at $2,950.

New York carried a D in June 2023 and the same D in June 2026, stuck at 42nd near the bottom of the table. Twelve of the 44 markets kept their grade across those three years, and New York belongs to that unmoved group. Boston, a D in every June the index tracked, shares the profile. The report's authors, Crystal Chen and Quentin Proctor, single out New York and San Francisco as places where the market works against renters, and the city's listing data back up that verdict.

Landlords in New York have little reason to dangle incentives. Free-month deals appear at 7% of large apartment communities, about one-third of the share across all 44 cities. Price cuts show up on 7% of active listings, less than half of Denver's 16%. Both readings suggest owners fill units at full price without bargaining.

New apartments in New York vanish from the market at one of the quickest paces in the study. Of new units, 33% remain open after 30 days, and San Francisco and Virginia Beach alone post a lower share. A tenant who spends a month comparing options will watch about two-thirds of fresh postings disappear.

New construction offers little prospect of loosening that grip. The metro permitted 5.2 homes per 1,000 residents, and its rental vacancy rate sits at 5.5%. Austin and Nashville, both A markets, authorized homes at three to four times New York's pace. Without a surge of additional units forcing landlords to compete, New York tenants can expect to pay the list price and sign within weeks of a unit's debut.

San Francisco's median one-bedroom asking rent jumped 21.9% over the year to $4,060, the steepest climb in the study. Chicago posted the next-fastest gain at 10%. San Francisco moved at more than twice that pace. The increase added about $730 to a monthly bill that stood near $3,330 in June 2025. Of the 44 cities, New York is the one that charges more for a one-bedroom. Boston trails in third at $2,950, more than $1,100 below San Francisco.

San Francisco landlords offer no move-in deals. Not one large apartment community advertised a free month in June 2026, the lowest reading possible. San Jose, Detroit, Tulsa, and Virginia Beach also sat at zero. Open units vanish faster than anywhere else in the index. A mere 25% of new listings remained available after 30 days, the least of all 44 cities and a notch under Virginia Beach at 27%. Tenants who pause to compare options risk losing the unit to another applicant.

Construction offers scant prospect of relief. The metro issued permits for 3.2 homes per 1,000 residents, the smallest rate in the full table, below Chicago at 3.5 and Detroit at 3.9. Vacancy stands at 4.2%, which leaves owners little pressure to fill empty apartments. A pipeline this thin gives tenants no reason to expect a wave of new buildings to tilt the balance.

Price cuts mark the one crack in the owners' armor, appearing on 12% of active postings. Las Vegas and Los Angeles, both B markets, show the same proportion. The markdowns have not kept the San Francisco median from rising by more than a fifth.

San Francisco's grade fell from C in June 2023 to F in June 2026, a two-step drop no other slipping city matched. San Jose and Portland each lost one step. San Francisco now sits 43rd, with Virginia Beach, the lone other F, below it at a far cheaper $1,690. No city in the index gave up more ground over those three years.
