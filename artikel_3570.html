10 Best Penélope Cruz Movies Of All Time, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Penélope Cruz can arrive halfway through a movie and make you forget who the lead is. Just watch Vicky Cristina Barcelona. The movie is ticking along quite nicely until Cruz appears as María Elena and turns a pleasantly steamy romantic comedy into an emotional contact sport. She won an Oscar for that performance, but anyone familiar with her Spanish-language films knows Hollywood took its sweet time recognizing what an extraordinary actress she is.

From sharing the screen with Johnny Depp in Blow to her enduring collaborations with Pedro Almodóvar, Cruz has built a career that refuses to be pigeonholed. And at 52, she's hardly slowing down. Her 2026 projects include La Bola Negra, Olivia Wilde's The Invite, and Bunker, a psychological thriller that reunites her with real-life husband Javier Bardem. The pair first worked together in 1992's Jamón, Jamón, long before becoming one of cinema's most recognizable couples.

Of course, not every movie worthy of Cruz's talents is necessarily worthy of her. Sometimes she's the best thing about an otherwise frustrating film. These 10 movies are ranked according to their overall quality, the strength of her performances, and their lasting impact. And as much as Hollywood loves her, some of her finest work has happened much closer to home.

For a movie about four attractive adults spending an evening discussing sex, Olivia Wilde's The Invite can feel surprisingly tedious. Seth Rogen and Wilde play Joe and Angela, a married couple whose relationship has gone painfully stale. When they invite their upstairs neighbors, Piña (Penélope Cruz) and Hawk (Edward Norton), over for dinner, the evening turns into an uncomfortable examination of marriage and just how much people are willing to reveal about themselves.

The Invite may overstay its welcome, but Cruz makes the dinner party worth attending.

Critics have largely embraced the film, but its endless conversational sparring sometimes feels more impressed with itself than it deserves to be. Thankfully, Cruz didn't get the memo. Her Piña arrives with enough confidence to make everyone else at the table question their life choices. Cruz also brings some much-needed spontaneity to the increasingly theatrical proceedings, especially opposite Norton. The Invite may overstay its welcome, but Cruz makes the dinner party worth attending.

Adam Driver might have the title role in Michael Mann's Ferrari, but Penélope Cruz has little trouble reminding audiences who actually holds the power in Enzo Ferrari's complicated personal life. She plays Laura Ferrari, whose marriage has been devastated by the death of their son and Enzo's infidelity. With the family business facing financial ruin, Laura is understandably running out of patience with her husband's excuses.

Cruz approaches Laura's anger with such conviction that her confrontations with Driver frequently overshadow the racing sequences. She's grieving, furious with her husband, and painfully aware that he would prefer to make decisions without her. Although Ferrari struggles to balance its domestic drama with the spectacle of motorsport, Cruz gives the story its emotional weight. Mann's film is an ambitious portrait of a man whose personal failures threaten everything he's built, and Laura makes sure he knows it.

Penélope Cruz doesn't have an enormous role in La Bola Negra, but it's difficult to imagine the movie without her. Directed by Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi, the Spanish drama draws inspiration from Federico García Lorca's unfinished work to examine the persecution of gay men across three periods of Spanish history. The ambitious film earned its directors a shared Best Director prize at the 2026 Cannes Film Festival.

Cruz plays Nené Romero, a flamboyant cabaret entertainer performing for Nationalist troops during the Spanish Civil War. Her arrival brings an explosion of theatrical energy to a story filled with repression and suffering. The film's sprawling structure occasionally threatens to overwhelm its characters, but its historical ambition and striking imagery make it a fascinating addition to contemporary Spanish cinema. Cruz makes the most of her brief appearance, reminding audiences that sometimes a supporting character can leave one of the biggest impressions.

Few movies have mocked the pretensions of the film industry quite as enthusiastically as Official Competition. The Spanish comedy stars Cruz as Lola Cuevas, an eccentric director hired by a wealthy businessman to make a prestigious movie. Her leading men, played by Antonio Banderas and Oscar Martínez, have wildly different approaches to acting and egos large enough to require their own dressing rooms.

Lola's increasingly absurd rehearsal techniques include suspending a massive concrete slab above her actors and subjecting their cherished awards to a particularly cruel experiment. Cruz clearly enjoys playing a woman who considers every outrageous demand perfectly reasonable. The film is merciless about artistic vanity, with Banderas and Martínez more than willing to make fools of themselves. But Cruz is the main attraction, presiding over the ridiculous proceedings with the confidence of someone who might actually believe her own nonsense.

Before Johnny Depp and Penélope Cruz were sailing around the Caribbean together, they were making considerably worse decisions in Blow. Ted Demme's crime drama chronicles the rise and fall of real-life cocaine trafficker George Jung (Depp), whose involvement with the Medellín cartel helps turn him into a major figure in America's drug trade. Cruz plays Mirtha, the woman who becomes his wife and shares his appetite for excess.

Their relationship begins with the intoxicating excitement of easy money and quickly deteriorates into something much uglier. Cruz brings a combustible energy to Mirtha, whose addiction and increasingly volatile behavior contribute to the collapse of their marriage. Blow occasionally follows the familiar trajectory of a gangster blockbuster, but its cast gives the story considerable appeal. There's something irresistible about watching two charismatic stars make such a spectacular mess of their lives.

Pedro Almodóvar's Pain and Glory belongs to Antonio Banderas, who gives one of his finest performances as Salvador Mallo, an aging filmmaker struggling with chronic pain and an inability to work. But some of the film's most moving moments belong to Penélope Cruz, who appears in childhood flashbacks as Salvador's mother, Jacinta. She's raising a sensitive young boy in difficult circumstances, doing her best to provide a loving home while dealing with the practical realities of poverty.

There's something wonderfully unforced about Cruz's performance. Jacinta is not portrayed as a saint, but as a hardworking mother who occasionally finds her son completely exasperating. Those memories help explain how Salvador became the filmmaker he is, giving the movie an emotional foundation that extends well beyond its story of artistic frustration. Pain and Glory is one of Almodóvar's most personal achievements, and the combination of Banderas' introspective performance and Cruz's warmth makes it particularly affecting.

Pedro Almodóvar has made some extraordinary movies about women, but All About My Mother might be his most compassionate. Cecilia Roth stars as Manuela, whose teenage son dies in a tragic accident. Her journey to Barcelona reconnects her with people from her past and introduces her to Rosa (Cruz), a young nun whose pregnancy and HIV diagnosis leave her facing an uncertain future.

Cruz gives Rosa a vulnerability that makes her situation especially heartbreaking. She's hopeful despite the circumstances, sometimes frustratingly naïve, and entirely believable as a young woman who hasn't yet grasped how difficult her future could become. The movie ultimately belongs to its ensemble, but Cruz is essential to the complicated relationship that develops between Rosa and Manuela. Winner of the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film, All About My Mother remains one of Almodóvar's greatest achievements, finding unexpected humor even in its darkest moments.

Penélope Cruz is so outrageously entertaining in Vicky Cristina Barcelona that it's almost unfair to the rest of the cast. Woody Allen's romantic comedy follows two American women, Vicky (Rebecca Hall) and Cristina (Scarlett Johansson), who become involved with a charismatic Spanish painter, Juan Antonio (Javier Bardem), during a summer in Barcelona. Things get considerably more interesting when his ex-wife, María Elena (Cruz), comes back into the picture. Suddenly, the romantic complications become much more entertaining.

Cruz is hilarious one moment and genuinely frightening the next, particularly when she's arguing with Bardem. The actors first met making Jamón, Jamón in 1992 and began their relationship while filming Vicky Cristina Barcelona, which makes their onscreen chemistry even more enjoyable in retrospect. Yet María Elena is considerably more than a volatile romantic rival. Cruz reveals a gifted artist whose insecurities threaten to consume her, earning an Oscar for a performance that practically steals the movie. She's magnificent, and the film is infinitely more interesting whenever she's around.

Few actresses can make a morally questionable character as painfully sympathetic as Penélope Cruz. In Pedro Almodóvar's Parallel Mothers, she plays Janis, a photographer who unexpectedly becomes pregnant and befriends Ana (Milena Smit), a much younger woman preparing to give birth at the same hospital. Their lives become increasingly intertwined when Janis makes a discovery that threatens everything she believes about her new family.

Almodóvar connects their experiences with Spain's troubled history, particularly families still searching for relatives who disappeared during Francisco Franco's dictatorship. It's a potentially unwieldy combination, but the film handles its intimate drama and historical reckoning with surprising assurance. Cruz allows Janis to make deeply questionable decisions without demanding the audience forgive her. Her fourth Academy Award nomination was thoroughly deserved, and Parallel Mothers stands among the strongest films of Almodóvar's later career.

If there's one movie that demonstrates everything Penélope Cruz can do, it's Volver. Pedro Almodóvar's 2006 film stars Cruz as Raimunda, a working-class mother whose already complicated life becomes considerably more difficult following a violent incident involving her teenage daughter. Meanwhile, the apparent return of her supposedly deceased mother threatens to expose long-buried family secrets. It's an outrageous premise, but Almodóvar turns it into a deeply moving story about the relationships between generations of women.

Cruz is magnificent as Raimunda, who has very little time for self-pity because somebody always needs something from her. She's fiercely protective of her daughter and occasionally so exhausted by the people around her that her frustration becomes genuinely funny. Her performance of the title song, with vocals provided by flamenco singer Estrella Morente, remains one of the film's most beautiful moments. Volver earned Cruz her first Academy Award nomination, and 20 years later, it's difficult to imagine anyone else playing Raimunda. More importantly, it's difficult to imagine the movie being half as good without her.
