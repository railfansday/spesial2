Robert Pattinson’s Crime Thriller Is Still His Greatest Movie 9 Years Later

With the recent announcement of his casting as James Bond, it's clear that Robert Pattinson has had a pretty good few years. Since 2020, he's portrayed Bruce Wayne in Matt Reeves' The Batman; appeared in two Christopher Nolan films, The Odyssey and Tenet; and garnered massive critical praise for performances in movies like The Drama, Primetime, and Mickey 17. While he had once been known mainly for his starring role in the Twilight franchise, he has emerged as one of Hollywood's best actors. But even amid this run of incredible performances, his greatest role remains the one that arrived nearly a decade ago.

Released in 2017, Good Time, directed by Josh and Benny Safdie, is a relentless, anxiety-inducing thriller that centers on a small-time criminal who, after a failed robbery, attempts to rescue his brother. Set in a grimy and disturbingly unromantic New York City, Good Time is a nightmarish descent into the criminal underworld, a landscape where desperation, violence, and self-destruction are commonplace. Though Pattinson has become even more well-known for his turns in some of Hollywood's biggest films, his performance in Good Time was his finest role, and one that has yet to be beaten in his filmography.

On the surface, the premise of Good Time is straightforward and has few complications. Pattinson's Connie Nikas, along with his intellectually disabled brother Nick (Bennie Safdie), attempts to rob a bank. The heist goes wrong almost from the start, and when Nick is arrested and sent to jail, Connie pulls out all the stops to get the money together to bail him out, regardless of the consequences.

Pattinson’s entire career has been building to Bond.

Much like one of the Safdies' other films, Uncut Gems, Good Time is relentless in its tension, drama, and thrills that have you on the verge of a heart attack throughout. Connie's journey through New York City on his desperate quest to free his brother is equal parts frustrating and devastating. Every decision he makes as he traverses the city only seems to make his situation worse, pulling him into more dangerous problems with hardened criminals and complete strangers.

The film unfolds with the momentum of a 100-minute panic attack, never letting you catch your breath or process what you just watched before it jumps into the next disastrous development. Its close-up shooting style and claustrophobic framing pull you right into the drama and feel genuinely suffocating. There is something uniquely exhausting about watching Connie operate. Like the viewer, he never pauses to think about what is happening; he is always moving, talking, and searching for a way out. However, much like a man trapped in quicksand, the quicker he moves, the deeper he sinks into the consequences of his actions.

What truly elevates the movie beyond its already exceptional filmmaking is Pattinson's performance. For a long time, Pattinson was brushed off as "the Twilight guy," but it was films like Good Time that helped him shake off that perception and show general audiences his true, vast range. Connie is hardly a likable protagonist and is anything but conventional, much less a person anyone should admire. For all the love he has for Nick, Connie is selfish, manipulative, and cruel, someone who is willing to exploit nearly everyone he comes across to accomplish what he's set out to do.

Pattinson's performance is built around this fascinating dichotomy: a personality that makes little sense, yet you can tell he genuinely loves his brother. He's resourceful yet incompetent, someone who is selfish and selfless while using what street smarts he has to escape danger without considering larger consequences.

The actor captures these contradictions with impressive skill, a test that many other actors would fail. His frantic physicality, aggression, and ability to shift from charm to intimidation at a moment's notice make him feel like a real human and not a caricature. At times, it's almost easy to forget you're watching "that guy from Twilight" as Pattinson disappears into this character with his bleached hair and disheveled appearance. He is so far removed from a polished romantic lead that you start to question whether it's truly the same performer.

Since the premiere of Good Time, Pattinson has become even bigger than he was at the height of the Twilight era. He is set to play James Bond, he's Batman, and he is a frequent potential nominee for numerous Academy Awards. For all the good he does now, it was films like Good Time that first showed audiences what he was made of.

Nearly a decade after its release, the movie remains an extraordinary achievement, not just because of its incredible direction, narrative, and cinematography, but because Pattinson delivers a performance that continues to define his abilities. He may have become James Bond, but Connie Nikas remains his most unforgettable creation.
