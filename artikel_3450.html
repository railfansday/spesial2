Terry Pratchett's 189-Minute Fantasy Adaptation Gets Discworld Right

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Even though author Terry Pratchett’s Discworld is an infamously tough literary epic to get right on screen, one underrated miniseries from 2008 nailed the writer’s unique mix of comedy and fantasy. Some books are considered unfilmable because they feature unconventional forms of narration, like James Joyce’s Ulysses, Ursula K. Le Guin’s The Left Hand of Darkness, or Catriona Ward’s The Last House on Needless Street.

The titles listed above run the gamut from grounded slice-of-life comedy to a grim serial killer thriller, but all of them are tricky to adapt because of the voice of their narrators, rather than the action of their stories. The same is not necessarily true for British author Terry Pratchett’s iconic 40-book Discworld series. Although Discworld has influenced classic fantasy shows like Prime Video’s Critical Role show The Legend of Vox Machina, adapting the books to the screen has proven remarkably difficult over the decades.

Pratchett’s work is filled with light-hearted, dry humor whose deadpan, sardonic wit is quite at odds with the life-or-death stakes of his stories. This results in a strange tonal mélange that feels a little like Mel Brooks rewriting HBO’s Game of Thrones. The puns, visual gags, and slapstick are all there, but there is still fantasy violence, a surprisingly cynical tone, and some jarringly dark twists. As tough as this balance is to get right, 2008’s Sky1 production The Color Of Magic took an admirable stab at it.

The Color Of Magic adapts both the titular novel, the first in the Discworld series, and its first sequel The Light Fantastic, since the book version of The Color Of Magic ends on a literal cliffhanger. The show focuses on Sean Astin’s cheerful tourist Twoflower, who brings his living luggage to the eponymous setting and is soon saddled with David Jason’s washed-up would-be wizard Rincewind as his guide.

Rincewind has failed to learn a single spell in four decades because he has one incredibly powerful spell in his mind, unbeknownst to himself, and the rest of the spells don’t want to go anywhere near it. If that sentence sounded a little strange or confusing, then it is perhaps clear why The Color Of Magic’s Pratchett adaptation ran the risk of losing something vital between the page and screen.

Despite boasting a starry cast including Tim Curry, David Bradley, Karen David, Jeremy Irons, Christopher Lee, James Cosmo, and Brian Cox, The Color Of Magic still had to contend with how bizarre and surreal Pratchett’s stories are. Brimming with absurd humor, subplots that peter out on purpose, and goofy asides, the Discworld books don’t seem, at first glance, to be too well suited to television adaptation.

However, The Color Of Magic proved that even Pratchett’s style could be captured onscreen. Much like Prime Video’s Lord of the Rings prequel The Rings of Power turned Tolkien’s dry, academic backstory for the original trilogy into an exciting epic, The Color Of Magic made Pratchett’s story sing onscreen more than any adaptation before or since.

2021’s The Watch was a complete misfire that rewrote major elements of the source books, much to the chagrin of many Pratchett fans. Meanwhile, 1995’s Johnny and the Dead and 2006’s Johnny and the Bomb were both fun but inessential, but their simpler family-friendly stories were based on Pratchett’s non-Discworld novels. 2006’s Hogfather, which preceded The Color Of Magic, was a far stronger adaptation, and not coincidentally based on a beloved Discworld book.

Meanwhile, 2010’s Going Postal was almost as good as The Color Of Magic, with a killer cast including David Suchet and Claire Foy. While the show is well worth seeking out for fans of Terry Pratchett, The Color Of Magic remains his true must-see adaptation on the small screen.
