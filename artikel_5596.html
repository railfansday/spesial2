10 car and racing stories tested against their headlines, from the Cybertruck to Monza

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Tesla ends Cybertruck production around mid-2027, and 9 other auto and racing stories get checked against what the facts support.

For several years, automakers spent heavily on a guess about what drivers would want next, and now they are paying for the guesses that missed. The cost is large, and two established carmakers on different continents have each paid billions to unwind their electric plans. Honda $HMC took a $16 billion charge in 2026 to restructure its electric-vehicle division, CNBC reports, and Stellantis $STLA took $26 billion in charges a year earlier, in 2025, to reset its EV strategy, according to CBT News. Neither company is giving up on electric cars. Each is deciding what to stop building so it can put its money and factory capacity where they will earn a return.

Pulling back from one bet means placing another, and American buyers are already showing carmakers where to place it. They are choosing hybrids over the full move to battery power. Americans bought nearly 20% more hybrids in the first six months of 2026 than in the same months of 2025, according to figures from the Center for Automotive Research, led by CEO Elizabeth Krear, reported by CNBC. Buyers are rewarding the cautious option. Baum & Associates, where Alan Baum is principal, expects fully electric models to stay a small part of the American market, with a forecast that EVs will reach only 9.5% of it by 2030. At that level, carmakers can't count on American buyers of electric cars to keep new production lines busy. When buyers split between drivetrains, a factory line becomes the scarcest thing a carmaker owns, and the real decision is what it builds there.

The Drive gathered a day's car news around the report that Tesla $TSLA will end the Cybertruck to make room for the Cybercab. Its roundup covered a wide spread of news, including a Hyundai airbag recall, a BYD hypercar's run in Abu Dhabi, Audi's planned U.S. plant and NASCAR's weekend in Miami. Each of those announcements can be held to one test: does the substance behind it match the headline? Some pass that test, and others prove smaller, slower or less settled than they first appear. The Cybertruck is the first place to apply it. Tesla is giving up one of the most recognizable trucks on the road. Two questions decide whether that trade makes sense: what Tesla gains from the factory space, and what the trucks already sold leave behind for their owners and for the company.

Tesla $TSLA is ending the Cybertruck to free factory space for the Cybercab, its driverless car, and it presents the change as a handoff rather than a retreat. The Drive reports that Wes Morrill, the Cybertruck's chief engineer, regards the outgoing truck as the testing ground for the technology that now goes into its replacement. Tesla plans to stop building the truck around mid-2027 and give its factory space to Cybercab production, according to CNBC. Morrill's account is Tesla's best case, and the truck's sales record shows whether the company is giving up much.

Tesla set a mainstream target for the Cybertruck, and the truck fell far short of it. Before launch, Tesla CEO Elon Musk predicted, CNBC reports, that the truck would eventually sell 250,000 a year, yet Cox Automotive estimates U.S. sales of just 20,237 in 2025. Some of that demand came from another company Musk runs. SpaceX spent $131 million on Cybertrucks last year, so even the modest sales total may overstate how much ordinary buyers wanted the truck. Tesla is not giving up a hit to chase a gamble. Most buyers passed the truck by, and Tesla loses little in sales by handing its line to the Cybercab.

Tesla's ties to the truck continue after production stops, because the trucks already sold stay on the road. Musk expects them to last. "True, but the Cybertruck fleet will be around for a long time," he said, as reported by CNBC. Those owners will keep needing fixes from Tesla, which has already issued 11 voluntary recalls for the 2024 model year of the truck, according to National Highway Traffic Safety Administration figures reported by CNBC.

For owners, the question now is how long Tesla will keep backing a truck it no longer builds.

Honda $HMC is teasing a hybrid wagon for Japan that American drivers have reason to notice. The Drive reports that the Avancier may hint at an Accord SUV for America. Honda has not confirmed that model, and buyers should treat it as a possibility rather than a car they can plan around. Read that way, the wagon is a clue to where Honda needs its next hybrid growth to come from.

Honda depends on hybrids for a large share of its American business. Hybrid models make up 31% of what American Honda sells, according to company figures reported by CNBC, and Honda's U.S. hybrid sales reached an all-time high in the first half of 2026. Honda is protecting momentum, not managing a decline. Even with that record, Honda ranks second among U.S. hybrid brands, behind Toyota $TM, and it cannot assume its hybrid buyers will stay with it.

Honda's next step in hybrids depends on larger vehicles. Elizabeth Krear, CEO of the Center for Automotive Research, says carmakers kept early hybrids small even as American buyers moved to bigger vehicles, CNBC reports, and Hyundai already sells large SUVs in its hybrid lineup. Honda plans a hybrid system for larger vehicles, but for now it exists only as a plan. Nothing yet ties the Avancier to it, and the wagon and a possible SUV simply point the same way as that plan. Gary Robinson, vice president of auto strategy at American Honda Motor Co., ties the company's hybrid ambitions to the bigger system. "At that point, there's really nothing to stop us in terms of battling it out with anybody in terms of hybrids," Robinson said. Robinson's "at that point" also concedes that Honda's chances depend on a system that has not arrived.

The size of the U.S. hybrid market makes the effort worth it for Honda. Hybrids will be a quarter of the U.S. market by 2030, according to Baum & Associates figures reported by CNBC, though that figure is a forecast. Between them, Honda, Toyota and Hyundai Motor Group control 86% of American hybrid sales. With so few sellers, any gap one rival fills leaves less for the other two.

American drivers should watch whether a bigger Honda hybrid reaches U.S. showrooms. Until one does, the Accord SUV remains a hint drawn from a wagon they cannot buy.

Owners of a Hyundai Veloster N or Kona Electric have good reason to wonder whether their airbags will work in a crash. The Drive reports that Hyundai is recalling about 38,000 of the cars from 2019-2023 because their airbags and pretensioners may fail. That recall covers only vehicles sold in the U.S., according to Seoul Economic Daily. The total measures how many American owners Hyundai must reach, and it says nothing about how the two models fare in other markets.

Most of the recalled cars are Kona Electrics, and Hyundai sells few of them today. Seoul Economic Daily reports that the recall includes 29,058 Kona Electrics from the 2019-2023 model years, while Hyundai sells only about 3,000 to 4,000 of the compact electric SUV a year in the U.S. Hyundai has far more recalled Kona Electrics on American roads than it sells in a year, so the work centers on cars that owners already drive. The rest of the recalled cars are Veloster Ns, a model Hyundai has discontinued. Every recalled Veloster N is now a used car, and Hyundai may need to track down people who bought theirs secondhand. For Hyundai, the main task in this recall is finding the owners.

The defect carries a serious risk but has not hurt anyone. The fault lies in the airbag control unit, the electronics that govern when the airbag deploys, and not in the airbag itself. A problem with the information stored in that unit might keep the airbag from doing its job in a collision, according to Seoul Economic Daily, and Hyundai has logged 331 reports of the fault. No crash, injury or death has been linked to the defect. Owners will get the fix as a free software update to the control unit, with no hardware to replace.

Owners should book the update without panic, since it is a free electronic fix for a real fault that has not caused any known harm.

Carmakers rarely get to boast in absolutes, and BYD reached for the biggest one available. The Drive reports that BYD took its Yangwang U9 Xtreme past 500 km/h in Abu Dhabi, reaching 312 mph and becoming the first production car above that mark. BYD said in a post on X $TWTR, as reported by Electrek, that it "has done the impossible." The speed is real. The open question is what that speed means for anyone who might want to buy the car.

The U9 Xtreme went faster than one of the best-known benchmarks in high-speed driving. Bugatti's Chiron Super Sport 300+ reached 490.48 km/h in 2019, according to Electrek, and BYD's car has now cleared 500 km/h. BYD took real risks to get there. During an earlier test, the U9 Xtreme struck a guardrail while traveling at about 280 mph, and BYD continued testing the car and later made its run past 500 km/h. The crash shows how dangerous testing at these speeds is for the company and its driver. It also suggests that no owner will drive the car anywhere near its top speed on a public road.

Very few buyers will ever own a U9 Xtreme, because BYD is building only 30 of them, according to Electrek. With so few cars for sale, BYD will earn little from selling this model. Most of what BYD gains from the speed run is attention for its name.

BYD now holds the fastest headline in the business, and whether it helps sell ordinary BYDs is a separate question.

Audi's American factory sounds like a settled decision because it comes with a price tag. The Drive reports that Audi is planning a U.S. plant, and the automaker has put the investment at $4 billion for a site in Georgia, according to Reuters. That is enough for a full assembly plant rather than a token facility. Audi has not committed the money yet, though. Volkswagen's supervisory board still has to give final approval, and until it does, the amount Audi will spend remains open to change. Audi chief executive Gernot Doellner describes the plant as the result of months of planning and negotiation. He declined to say whether the deal includes relief from tariffs.

Tariffs are the reason a Georgia plant makes sense for Audi. The company has no U.S. factory, so the cars it sells to American buyers come in from its plants in Europe and Mexico and face import duty at the border. Audi paid about €1.2 billion ($1.4 billion) in those charges last year, according to Reuters. Georgia production won't begin until the early 2030s, and until then every Audi sold in America will still be shipped in from abroad. The factory is a bet on where U.S. trade policy will stand in the next decade.

The investment also faces scrutiny in Germany, where Volkswagen workers are dealing with layoffs and the prospect that some factories will close. Audi says the Georgia plant will create about 4,500 jobs, according to Reuters. German officials want any expansion abroad to leave employment at home intact. "Growth in America must secure jobs and value creation here and must not displace them," said Olaf Lies, the state premier of Lower Saxony.

How much Audi finally spends in Georgia depends on a board vote, on U.S. trade terms and on political pressure in Germany.

Europe looks like the market that could absorb the electric cars American buyers have been slow to adopt. Battery-powered cars accounted for 34% of the new cars Europeans bought last month, a far larger portion than a year before, according to Automotive News figures reported by The Drive. A carmaker deciding where to send its electric models would look first to a market where buying habits shifted that quickly. That 34% covers a single month in Europe, though, and it records a moment rather than a level that drivers have settled at.

The size of Europe's shift is still in dispute, and a broader count puts it lower. The European Automobile Manufacturers' Association, an industry group known as ACEA, puts battery-electric cars at 22.7% of new registrations in the European Union, CNBC reports, well short of last month's 34% across Europe. The two figures cover different ground, with one month for the whole continent on one side and an EU tally on the other, and the gap between them may come from that difference as much as from what buyers did. Neither figure settles how far European drivers have gone. A carmaker moving production toward Europe is betting on the direction of the market, not on a continent where electric cars already hold a known share.

European drivers are clearly moving away from petrol and diesel. Those cars dropped steeply in a single year to 28.2% of the EU market, according to ACEA figures reported by CNBC. Hybrids hold the largest share of the market, at 36.3%, which is more than either battery-electric cars or petrol and diesel models hold in the same count. Europe's mainstream buyers are choosing the cautious option too.

Europe is moving toward electric cars one step at a time, and its buyers are leaning on hybrids along the way. A factory line built only for battery-electric models has no guaranteed outlet there either.

A carmaker can lose a whole factory's output over one part made a layer or two down its supply chain, by a company nobody at headquarters was tracking. The Drive reports that Stellantis $STLA is asking suppliers for parts data, as Ford $F and GM already do, to limit supply disruptions. Stellantis has a live case to test the idea against. It has stopped building vehicles at its plant in Toluca, Mexico, after a supplier halted shipments of suspension modules, according to CBT News. A part that comes from one supplier, with production unable to continue once its shipments end, is exactly the risk a parts map is meant to flag in advance.

The Toluca shutdown had nothing to do with a hidden part, though, because Stellantis knew exactly who made the modules. The supplier is a joint venture of ZF and Foxconn, CBT News reports, and Stellantis had been paying to keep the parts coming, with its U.S. affiliate sending the supplier over $26 million in December. Stellantis alleges in its lawsuit that the supplier then sought $70 million more in cash and price increases, though no court has established that amount. Stellantis could map every part in its chain and still face the same demand from a supplier it can't build without.

So far, Stellantis has turned to the courts rather than a database. A Mexican court order could allow it to restart production at Toluca, according to Stellantis statements reported by CBT News, and its lawsuit against the supplier goes to a hearing on April 6. Stellantis also has its plant in Windsor, Ontario, to protect from the dispute, and the company says that plant has had no downtime so far. That record rests on a very small cushion, because Stellantis said it had "less than a few hours’ worth of inventory" of chassis modules there. A stoppage at Windsor would idle a workforce of 5,500 and turn a single plant's problem into a much larger one.

A parts map can show Stellantis where one supplier and a thin stock of parts stand between it and an idle plant. Seeing that gap and closing it are separate decisions.

The Craftsman Truck Series race in Miami decides more than its cable listing suggests. FS1 is carrying the race to a national audience, according to The Drive, and the race itself settles the series title. NASCAR crowns its truck champion at Homestead-Miami Speedway in Homestead, Fla., according to Greater Miami & Miami Beach. The truck title is also part of NASCAR's whole season finale, with three NASCAR championships decided at Homestead over a single weekend. Anyone following the trucks should treat the Homestead race as the end of a season's work, with a national title at stake.

Fans can see that title decided in person without buying the entire weekend. The championship weekend is set for Nov. 6-8, 2026, according to Greater Miami & Miami Beach, which gives a fan time to plan the trip. Single-race tickets are sold in advance, and a single day at the track starts at $51.08, so a fan can come for the truck championship alone. A fan who wants to watch every title settled that weekend can buy a three-day pass, which starts at $117.47 and is also sold ahead of time.

For a fan, the truck championship is an affordable way into NASCAR's championship weekend.

Most of the industry's big announcements ask the public to trust a promise that only time will test, but fans need no such trust to follow a race. NASCAR's O'Reilly Auto Parts Series races in Miami the weekend of Nov. 6, 2026, according to The Drive, and fans can watch it right away. Carmakers who commit to a new model or a new plant will not know for years whether the decision paid off. A factory line has to be built, staffed and filled with orders before anyone can judge it, and the buyers it depends on can change their minds in the meantime. NASCAR will crown a race winner before this weekend ends, and fans will know the result the moment the cars cross the finish line.

For fans, the useful question is how to see the race, not what it means for the auto industry. CW/ESPN broadcasts it, so following the series this weekend takes finding the right channel rather than booking a trip to Miami. Viewers do not need a ticket, a pass or a plan made months in advance, and anyone with access to those networks can watch the finish live. Fans can make that decision on the day, without waiting on a board vote, a court ruling or a sales forecast.

Most claims in the auto business cannot be tested yet, but fans can check this race's winner for themselves this weekend.

The weekend's race worth an early Sunday alarm for American endurance fans is the 6 Hours of Monza, which streams on Max at 5 a.m. Eastern, according to The Drive. The race is a replacement, not a planned stop. The FIA World Endurance Championship brought in Monza, Italy, as a stand-in circuit after it relocated rounds because of war in Iran, according to ABC News. The championship moved this race because of a conflict, not for sporting or commercial reasons. Fans will wake up for this broadcast only because the series lost another event.

Both of the championship's replacement rounds are on European tracks. Barcelona's Circuit de Catalunya is the other stand-in alongside Monza, and the new rounds fall in October and November, according to ABC News. That schedule puts the closing stretch of the endurance season in Spain and Italy. Fans who follow the series will see its year end at two circuits they already know well.

Endurance racing has already made the move that Formula 1 is still weighing. Formula 1 faces a mid-September deadline, ABC News reports, on whether to hold its Qatar and Abu Dhabi races, while the endurance series has moved its rounds already. Formula 1 chief executive Stefano Domenicali sees Europe as the only fallback if the Gulf races can't happen. Formula 1 has not made its decision, so Monza shows which way the choice leans, not how it will end.

Race organizers, like carmakers, are rebuilding their year around what can actually happen rather than what they had planned.
