Webb traces a mysterious cosmic signal to a tiny ancient galaxy

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope traced the most distant fast radio burst ever detected to a tiny, star-forming galaxy in the early universe.

Today’s Image of the Day from the James Webb Space Telescope reveals a tiny galaxy with an extraordinary story to tell.

Hidden among countless distant galaxies, this faint patch of light is the source of one of the most mysterious signals in the universe.

The signal, known as a fast radio burst, traveled across billions of years of cosmic history before reaching Earth.

It lasted only a fraction of a second, yet it carried clues about the galaxy where it began.

Astronomers have now traced the burst to a surprisingly small galaxy that existed when the universe was just three billion years old.

The discovery could help scientists understand what produces these powerful cosmic flashes.

Fast radio bursts, or FRBs, are incredibly brief explosions of radio waves that arrive from distant regions of space.

Although they typically last only milliseconds, they can release enormous amounts of energy in that short time.

Scientists first discovered these signals in 2007, but their origins remain uncertain.

Some bursts repeat, while others appear only once. Their unpredictable behavior makes them particularly difficult to investigate.

One of the biggest challenges is identifying the galaxy where a burst originated.

Without that information, astronomers cannot easily determine what kind of environment produced it.

The signal featured in Webb’s latest discovery, called FRB 20240304B, was detected on March 4, 2024, by the MeerTRAP team using the MeerKAT radio telescope in South Africa.

Initial observations suggested that the burst came from an exceptionally distant location.

However, even the largest ground-based optical telescopes could not detect a galaxy at its position.

Astronomers needed a more powerful instrument to uncover its source.

NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope provided the missing piece of the puzzle.

Using its Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam), Webb detected a faint galaxy precisely where the radio burst had originated.

The accompanying image shows a field filled with distant galaxies.

A white box highlights the region containing the burst’s host galaxy, while an enlarged view reveals its faint blue glow.

A white cross marks the location of the radio signal.

Webb’s Near-Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) then measured the galaxy’s redshift, which describes how much its light has stretched as the universe expanded.

The measurement, 2.148, confirmed that the burst originated when the universe was only about three billion years old.

That makes FRB 20240304B the most distant fast radio burst identified to date.

The galaxy itself turned out to be just as remarkable as the signal.

Astronomers had expected to find a large, well-developed galaxy containing an enormous population of stars.

Instead, Webb revealed a dwarf galaxy roughly 1,000 times less massive than anticipated.

Despite its small size, the galaxy was actively producing new stars.

It existed during a period known as “cosmic noon,” when star formation across the universe reached its peak.

The researchers estimated that most of the galaxy’s stars may have formed within just 30 million years.

This youthful environment offers an important clue about what might have generated the mysterious burst.

“We thought it would be a big, nicely formed galaxy with lots of stars, and instead it was a little dwarf galaxy, although it was actively forming stars,” said Manisha Caleb of the University of Sydney, who led the study.

Astronomers have proposed several explanations for fast radio bursts, but two possibilities are particularly relevant to this discovery.

One involves the collision of two neutron stars, the extremely dense remnants left behind when massive stars die.

Such mergers can take billions of years to occur, making them more likely in older stellar populations.

Another explanation involves magnetars, neutron stars with extraordinarily powerful magnetic fields.

A magnetar can form after a massive star explodes as a supernova. Under certain conditions, activity within or around the magnetar could generate an intense burst of radio waves.

Unlike a neutron star merger, this process could happen relatively soon after the original star’s death.

The newly discovered galaxy’s youthful population makes the magnetar explanation especially compelling.

“Our work suggests that it’s very unlikely that this FRB was produced by a merger,” Caleb said.

Although the finding does not establish exactly what produced the burst, it strengthens the case for young magnetars as possible sources of these mysterious signals.

The burst also provided astronomers with information about the vast stretches of space between its host galaxy and Earth.

As radio waves travel through the universe, they interact with matter along their path.

Those interactions leave subtle signatures that scientists can study to investigate otherwise difficult-to-detect cosmic structures.

The researchers identified evidence of two such structures in the burst’s signal.

One was a previously unknown galaxy cluster approximately 3.5 billion light-years away. The other was the much closer Virgo Cluster, located around 54 million light-years from Earth.

These findings demonstrate how fast radio bursts can serve as probes of the cosmic web, the enormous network of galaxies, gas, and dark matter that stretches across the universe.

“A fast radio burst is almost like a cosmic flashlight. It lights up everything along the path,” said study co-author J. Xavier Prochaska of the University of California, Santa Cruz.

Astronomers expect future radio telescopes to identify many more distant fast radio bursts.

As those discoveries accumulate, Webb will help researchers examine the galaxies that produced them, revealing how their environments and stellar populations differ.

Each new detection could bring scientists closer to understanding the physical processes responsible for these extraordinary flashes.

For now, this image captures a remarkable achievement: a tiny, faint galaxy that remained invisible to powerful ground-based telescopes has finally come into view.

And within that distant galaxy lies a clue to an explosive event that occurred billions of years before our solar system formed.

The study was published in the journal Science.
