We Asked 6 PCT Thru-Hikers What Gear They’re Carrying

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

A 22-liter pack that looks like a school daypack. A fully custom pack from a small Japanese brand. A freestanding tent picked out six years ago on an REI employee’s recommendation, still in use 1,800 miles into the Pacific Crest Trail.

We asked six members of the PCT Class of 2026 about the gear they’re carrying: why they chose their backpacks and shelters, which items they love, and what’s starting to wear out. Their setups range from tiny packs and minimalist tarps to familiar gear carried over from years of backpacking.

This article is adapted from a video on The Trek’s YouTube channel. Responses have been condensed and lightly edited for clarity.

Meet the hikers: Bumper (Jaycee), Captain (Morgan da Silva), Teach (Devin Boggs), Broadway (Michael Grant), Sage Brush (Sage Wagner), and Mash (Jacob).

Planning your own hike? Our Pacific Crest Trail thru-hiker gear list covers building a complete kit. Here, the hikers explain how their choices have worked out on trail.

Their choices ranged from a tiny 22-liter pack to larger framed backpacks, including a fully custom build from Japan.

Bumper carries the Gossamer Gear Gorilla. Food takes up most of the pack, which has made packing a challenge, “but it’s been a lot of fun and kind of has given me something new to play with out in the woods.”

To address squeaking, people from Gossamer Gear suggested tightening down the corners with the Velcro inside where the frame sits. So far, that adjustment has helped.

Bumper has heard other hikers say the Gorilla is uncomfortable, but suspects some of them have the wrong hip belt size. “It’s going to be different for everyone.”

Captain has used an Osprey as an everyday pack for years, so the Osprey Exos 58 was a natural choice. “I was like, let me just get a new Osprey that can last me at least this five months, if not more.”

Captain likes the pockets-to-weight ratio and the straps. A foam sleeping mat strapped to the bottom works like a kickstand, so the pack stands up on its own. “I see all these people with their other bags that don’t stand.”

Captain appreciates the frame and support, despite the extra weight compared with some lighter packs. “I love it. It feels very comfortable. I haven’t had any issues. Maybe some marks on my back, as I saw in the mirror the other day.”

Teach’s Gossamer Gear Mariposa is about six years old and has been on many backpacking trips, including the Long Trail last year, when Teach chose it for the extra capacity needed to carry gear for a dog. The goal is for it to last to the end of the PCT.

“Very nice pack. Really comfortable for its weight,” Teach said. “I would say it’s kind of at that threshold of ultralight but still comfortable.” The huge external mesh pocket gets packed so full that other hikers joke about Teach’s “pregnant pack.” The sit pad on the back is another favorite feature.

The downsides showed up in the Sierra. Heavy loads strained Teach’s shoulders, and the rough terrain tore holes in the bottom of the pack, which Teach has patched. One lesson learned: “I shouldn’t put the bear can immediately on the bottom of the pack, ’cause that’s just way too much structure.”

Broadway bought the REI Co-op Flash 55 at REI on an employee’s recommendation, originally for the W Trek in Patagonia, then brought it on the PCT rather than spend money on new gear. It started out yellow, matching a blue-and-yellow color scheme. “It’s kind of more of a brown now because of trail, but that’s trail life.”

“I love it. It’s super cute. I love the color,” Broadway said. The pack may be a little big, so Broadway tends to fill it up. Food takes up at least half of the space.

At 22 liters, Sage’s Meadowphysics Grasshopper was the smallest backpack our interviewer had ever seen. Sage described it as “kind of a JanSport” with custom thru-hiking features: bottle pockets, a bottom pocket like packs from Pa’lante, and a bladder sleeve. “It’s almost like a meme. People say that you can hike the PCT in a JanSport.”

Sage said the pack is intended for base weights under seven pounds, but added, “I think a lot more people could get away with smaller packs if they were to challenge themselves.”

Mash carries a fully custom 50-liter pack from KS Ultralight Gear, a small brand out of Japan. “This is like my fifth backpack. I knew exactly what I wanted. I had the exact design laid out.”

That design includes an Omega exterior frame, a thick hip belt, and a bottom pocket so the poop kit is always within reach “whenever I suddenly have to go.” The pack looks small but stretches. Through the Sierra, Mash fit a fully loaded bear canister inside with nothing strapped to the outside.

The main complaint is noise: the exterior frame squeaks as parts of the pack rub against it. Mash described hearing it with every step as “a little bit maddening.”

Mash also has a caveat for anyone tempted by a custom build: “I would recommend someone get a custom backpack if you can afford it. But definitely not for your first one. Probably not for your second. Maybe not your third. You can think about it if it’s your fourth.”

The shelter choices include two Durston X-Mids, two freestanding tents, and a flat tarp that mostly stays packed while its owner sleeps under the stars.

Mash’s Durston X-Mid Pro 1 is the same tent from a previous Appalachian Trail hike. “It’s getting a little worn down right now, but I love it. It’s kept me warm and out of the rain.”

Mash originally had a five-pound one-person tent and wanted to cut base weight, so the X-Mid Pro 1 was “one of the lightest and simplest trekking pole tents I could find.” Mash hasn’t needed to pitch it often on this hike, with rain occurring only a few times, and appreciates the space inside and in the vestibules.

The biggest complaint is the zippers: one side no longer opens. Other hikers suggested that sand getting into the slider contributes to the problem. “I think this happens to all tents. It’s just the extra tension doesn’t help.” Mash joked about going to harass Dan Durston, the brand’s founder, about it.

Teach had a first-generation Durston X-Mid 1 before the PCT, but it was torn up on the last day of a trip to Patagonia, prompting a replacement with a newer version.

The ease of setup and the headroom are the big wins. “When I wake up in the morning, I’m completely unaware of my surroundings,” Teach said. In an older, lower tent, that meant constantly banging into the ceiling. “Having the extra head space has been a godsend.”

The one complaint: Teach preferred the zipper on the first-generation X-Mid and doesn’t like the dual zipper system on the replacement. “Everything else has been pretty easy and convenient about the tent.”

Captain chose the freestanding Big Agnes Copper Spur UL2 for “a little bit of psychological safety.” Having grown up with freestanding tents, Captain said trekking pole tents “kind of give me a little bit of the ick.”

The familiar setup, numerous pockets, and option to sleep without the rainfly are all selling points. Captain rarely uses the fly, preferring to keep the stars in view. “It’s very utilitarian.”

The downside is durability. Captain described it as “a little fragile” after granite in the High Sierra left tears in the mesh. Despite that damage, Captain still enthusiastically recommends it.

Broadway picked the NEMO Dragonfly 1P about six years ago for a first backpacking trip, on an REI employee’s recommendation. “I actually don’t regret it. I love it.”

As an inexperienced backpacker, Broadway went freestanding and hadn’t even heard of trekking pole tents until about a year before the interview. Going lighter could mean trying one someday, but for now, the Dragonfly’s setup and the option to sleep without the rainfly are reasons to stick with it.

The downside is space. Broadway finds the tent narrow, with room for the sleeping pad but not the pack. “It’s just a little tight, a little cozy.”

Sage carries a Zpacks 7-by-9-foot flat tarp that weighs about five ounces, but mostly cowboy camps. The tarp stays in the pack as “kind of a safety net when times are getting a little tough.” It’s paired with a DCF bathtub groundsheet. “It’s kind of a bougie ground sheet, but it hasn’t had any holes in it.”

The biggest selling point is the weight. Sage likes sleeping outside and believes tarps handle condensation better. A flat tarp is less intuitive to set up, but “it really allows you to be creative.” Sage has pitched it in several configurations, including an A-frame setup.

“I would not necessarily recommend this to anyone who hasn’t done any tarp camping before,” Sage said. For experienced tarp users looking to lighten their packs, however, it’s an option Sage recommends.

Bumper’s pick is a sit pad, the Gossamer Gear Thinlight Foam Pad in the 1/8-inch thickness, wrapped in Tyvek. Bumper learned quickly that bare foam on the ground “just accumulates everything.” The Tyvek keeps a layer between the foam and the dirt.

Sage has used Sawyer and Platypus filters, but the Katadyn BeFree fits Sage’s hiking style best: scoop a liter on the go, then filter while walking. “I really like to just do as much as I can while walking.”

“Ten out of 10 on this one, and no one is going to like this,” Captain said. Before the hike, Captain bought “the most ludicrously big phone” for social media, and the only thing that carries it is a Bucket Boss pouch hooked onto the hip belt with Velcro. Captain admitted it’s “not particularly proper gear related.”

Teach started carrying a laser pointer heading into the Sierra. As an astronomy enthusiast, Teach uses it to point out objects in the night sky, including some of the larger nebulas visible in dark-sky areas during Milky Way season.

Teach also loves a Western Mountaineering 10-degree sleeping bag, though the model name escaped Teach on camera. “It does the job and 10 times more.” Teach describes it as hardwearing and quick to dry after getting wet, and frequently uses it for cowboy camping.

Mash’s pick is a pair of Patagonia pants, though the model name was forgotten on camera. They’re lightweight with a silky feel, and “it’s cool during the day and very warm at night.” They’re also shredded and have been sewn up a dozen times, but Mash is still wearing them.

Mash has also carried a Fujifilm X-T5 camera, which has been “fantastic for capturing a lot of memories.”

Teach’s hat came from San Diego. Its wide brim provides shade in alpine areas and on hot, sunny days, while Teach appreciates how the leather inside handles sweat.

The hat has also been reshaped with an arrowhead crown as a nod to the national parks. “I’m always very passionate about our national parks.”

Captain’s advice is to weigh both physical and mental comfort. That includes deciding whether to try an unfamiliar trekking pole tent or stick with a familiar shelter and accept a little extra weight.

“Does it make me feel comfortable? Is it worth it in the long run?” Captain said. “Make sure you find your creature comforts.”

Mash sees hikers using trekking poles with poor technique: setting them too high or too short, or throwing them out in front and trying to drag themselves forward. The advice is to learn how to use them properly.

For a starting point, see these physical therapist-approved hiking techniques, which include trekking pole tips.

Teach said not to focus only on buying brand-new, shiny gear. Years ago, Teach found a Big Agnes pad in REI’s used gear section, and it has come on every backpacking trip since.

“I was able to save a lot of money by buying it used, and honestly it’s been more solid than a lot of stuff that I’ve seen a lot of my friends use on trail.”

“The best bit of gear advice I’d give to future PCT hikers is that it doesn’t need to be ultralight,” Broadway said. “You can do the PCT just with your normal gear, your regular gear. You don’t need to invest thousands.”

Broadway sees benefits to lighter gear but hasn’t felt the need to replace an existing setup: “I’m doing the PCT with what I’ve had before the PCT, and I’m 1,800 miles in and still doing well.”

Sage’s advice: “Be very conscious with the advice you take, and trust your judgment, and have fun out there. You’ll figure it out.”
