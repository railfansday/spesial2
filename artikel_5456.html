From Brooklyn rooftops to a Norfolk battleship, 20 holiday light displays drawn from a regional roundup of 28 across the U.S.

A car creeps down a residential street on a December night while the kids in back press their faces to the glass, hunting for the next glowing roofline. People have chased that kind of radiance through long winters for centuries, first with Yule logs and candles. Thomas Edison brought electricity into the custom in 1880, when he strung bulbs outside his laboratory at Christmastime. Two years afterward, his friend Edward H. Johnson wound red, white, and blue electric bulbs around a Christmas tree that rotated. Several more decades passed before strands of that sort grew common and affordable. Estimates now put annual U.S. sales at 150 million sets of lights, a sign of how deep the ritual runs in American households.

Homes and businesses across the U.S. decorate halls, evergreens, and exteriors, and a few build spectacles large enough to draw repeat crowds. Popularity creates a planning problem. The holiday window lasts a matter of weeks, and the most ambitious productions compete for the same short stretch of evenings. Some charge admission and require buying tickets online ahead of time, a few with set entry slots. Others cost nothing at the gate yet bring traffic and congestion that can swallow an evening. Travelers $TRV must weigh dates, closing days, parking fees, and the walking involved against the stamina of the youngest people in the group. Out-of-town visitors add a hotel search to the list, turning a quick drive past a few decorated houses into a full itinerary.

U.S. News Travel gathered 28 seasonal light displays and tours in a roundup that Marisa Méndez wrote, Suzanne Mason edited, and Rachael Hood reviewed. The publication updated the guide Oct. 18, 2026. The guide sorts its entries into six regions: Northeast, Southeast, Southwest, Midwest, West, and Hawaii and Alaska. It assigns no numeric rank or score and states no selection method. Each entry assembles the practical details a trip demands, including price, season, logistics, and lodging, alongside impressions from previous visitors. The 20 entries below come from those 28 and follow the guide's regional order, moving from Maine to Hawaii.

Coastal Maine Botanical Gardens in Boothbay, Maine, strings more than 750,000 lights along a milelong path for Gardens Aglow, its seasonal display. The count averages more than 140 bulbs for every foot of walkway. The strands form Christmas trees, round baubles, mushrooms, and other shapes. Two giant trolls, Roskva and Lilja, tower over the grounds. Past visitors describe the gardens as a thrill in any season, yet they save their warmest praise for this winter show.

Admission starts at $20 for adults and $10 for children. The gardens sell every ticket online in advance, which rules out a spontaneous stop at the gate. Children's tickets cost half the adult rate, so a family of two adults and two children pays at least $60. Choosing a weekday or a Sunday can trim a few dollars from that total.

Gardens Aglow runs Thursday through Sunday from Nov. 14 to Jan. 2, plus select other dates. The season stretches about seven weeks, but the weekly calendar opens the gates on four nights of each. Guests can enter between 4 and 7:30 p.m., a 3½-hour arrival window on each open evening. The trail goes dark on Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day, which removes four peak celebration days from the run.

Boothbay and Boothbay Harbor stage their own celebration, Boothbay Lights, alongside Gardens Aglow. Residents decorate homes across both towns, and the communities host parades, special events, and holiday shopping sales. Visitors can fold a stroll through town into the same trip, before or after an evening on the garden path.

Boothbay, a small town, sits about 60 miles northeast of Portland. The gardens occupy 105 Botanical Gardens Drive, Boothbay, ME 04537. Between online-only tickets, a Thursday-to-Sunday calendar, and four closures, Gardens Aglow favors travelers who lock in a date before setting out.

The open gate moves spending away from admission and toward dinner and gifts, and each household sets its own total for the evening.

Bavarian Lodge and Posthotel offer overnight rooms, and Posthotel operates as a wellness retreat. A night in town spares out-of-town guests the drive home after the evening ends.

Winter Karneval extends Leavenworth's calendar past Christmas Eve, filling January weekends with ice carvings, fireworks, and winter sports. The two events give the town a festive stretch from late November into January, and travelers who miss the December lights still have a reason to make the trip.

Honolulu City Lights keeps its displays glowing from 6 p.m. to 6 a.m. each day between Dec. 5 and 31, and admission costs nothing. U.S. News Travel describes the event as a beloved Oahu tradition. The run spans 27 evenings, which gives a visitor room to come back more than once during a stay.

The Star-Advertiser reported that city crews spent a week in 2018 installing Christmas lights, decorations, and displays on the Frank Fasi Civic Grounds by Honolulu Hale ahead of that year's Honolulu City Lights. U.S. News Travel lists the event's address as 530 S. King St., Honolulu, HI 96813. The surrounding area opens at 8 a.m. and closes at 10 p.m. Guests get a four-hour window each evening, from 6 to 10 p.m., to roam among the scenes while everything glows. The bulbs keep burning for eight more hours after closing time. Late-night arrivals and predawn visitors will find the lights on and the site closed.

Opening night on Dec. 5 packs several events into a single evening. The City Lights Block $SQ Party brings food vendors and Santa photos, and the Public Workers' Electric Light Parade rolls through the same night. A tree lighting ceremony completes the program. Additional events fill the rest of December. Anyone who wants the parade and the tree lighting on one trip needs to be in Honolulu for the first night.

Travelers $TRV rave about the outdoor displays, and many of them wait all year for the season's return. Some visitors say the scenes are fun in daylight. The 8 a.m. opening lets those guests take in the decorations with the sun out. A visitor could walk the site in the morning and return after 6 p.m. to watch the same scenes light up.

Aston at the Executive Centre Hotel and AC Hotel Honolulu both sit about a half-mile from the event. Waikiki, to the southeast, holds many of Oahu's top hotels. A room at either of the closer properties puts the lights within walking distance, which makes a morning look and an evening return simple to fit into one day.
