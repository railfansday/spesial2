Chickasaw Nation archaeology shows what happened to the iron de Soto’s army left behind

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Researchers think Chickasaw craftspeople reshaped iron left by de Soto’s fleeing army in 1541 into their own tools and ornaments.

At Native American sites in the 1500s Southeast, archaeologists usually find only a little European metal, rarely reworked and often buried with the dead.

In northeastern Mississippi, researchers think the Chickasaw ended up with far more of it than usual after Hernando de Soto’s Spanish army fled in 1541. Many of the pieces show signs that local craftspeople reshaped them into tools and ornaments.

Charles Cobb, a historical archaeologist at the Florida Museum of Natural History, led the study. His co-authors came from the South Carolina Institute of Archaeology and Anthropology, the University of Mississippi, and the Chickasaw Nation itself.

Cobb and his co-authors say the reworked shapes seem to follow Indigenous conventions rather than European ones. The tie to de Soto has a limit, though.

Radiocarbon dating can’t confirm mid-1500s layers at the site, so the dating relies on the types of objects found and Spanish accounts of the expedition.

De Soto set out in 1539 with more than 600 people and never built a permanent settlement. By December 1540, his army had reached a Chickasaw town that Spanish accounts call Chikasha. The leader of the surrounding region let him keep his troops there for the winter.

It went badly. Cobb’s team writes that the Spaniards fell into their usual pattern of violence and constant demands for supplies.

Just before the army was due to leave in spring, de Soto demanded hundreds of porters to carry its gear. The Chickasaw answered with a surprise attack and set the town on fire.

Spanish accounts the team cites say at least a dozen Europeans died and scores of horses and pigs burned. De Soto’s forces got away after a short, violent fight.

Brad Lieb is a co-author and directs Chickasaw archaeology for the Chickasaw Nation’s Heritage Preservation Division. He said the finds show how the Chickasaw adapted to the invasion and earned a name as a people who couldn’t be conquered.

The Chickasaw Nation launched the project in 2015 to find ancestral sites in its traditional territory in northern Mississippi and to give Chickasaw college students fieldwork experience.

At a cluster of sites called Stark Farms, the team brought metal detectors, and iron tools, reworked axes, and rolled copper beads showed up almost immediately.

Across five seasons, 2015 to 2019, crews swept the core of the sites in lanes about 5 feet (1.5 meters) wide and marked every metal object from before modern times.

The total reached at least 83 pieces of iron, copper alloy, and lead, likely dating to the 1500s. Others are a mouth harp and a gilded-cross medallion, probably from a bridle.

Stark Farms looks like other towns of its time, with farmers who relied on maize and a way of life that seems related to the Mississippian culture.

But the team recovered an unusually large amount of European metal there, more than at other 1500s sites, except in towns Europeans built themselves. Detectors found almost none at other nearby sites.

With no graves found, Cobb’s team believes the metal came mainly from everyday settings, not from burials of high-status people. Stark Farms “must have been more of an open season,” Cobb said.

Cutting and scraping tools were the most common reworked pieces. Celts, which are ground axe heads with no hole for a handle, came next.

On two axes, people bent the metal until the eye where a handle fits broke off. Other celts came from horseshoe pieces and fragments that look like rapier blades.

Two iron celts go further. One nearly copies a traditional stone axe head, with smooth, even grinding across its whole surface. The authors write that both required a large piece of iron and significant labor.

The copper-alloy pieces, mostly brass in an early chemical analysis, became pendants, beads, and small cones that people probably attached to clothing.

Two cones still held cordage. The makers didn’t rely on a forge. They bent and folded pieces instead of cutting them and spent a lot of effort grinding the edges smooth.

The expedition also carried a large amount of chain, some of it for shackling Native Americans as captives and porters. A few modest chain links turned up at Stark Farms.

Some finds look like battlefield leftovers. They include lead shot sized for an early musket and a cast-iron ball of about 4.3 ounces (122.5 grams) that may have come from a small cannon. Such items don’t normally turn up as trade goods, the authors write.

Mapping where metal like this landed also helped a team in Pennsylvania place a 1758 skirmish on one stretch of ground.

The authors say such events may not have been rare. Months earlier, de Soto’s men lost even more equipment in a pitched battle at the town of Mabila in western Alabama.

In the Southwest, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado’s expedition fought the Tiguex War from 1540 to 1542 and left military artifacts at Pueblo sites.

Coronado’s men also abandoned a bronze cannon. In late 1541, settlers from that expedition fled the town of San Geronimo III in southern Arizona without it. Archaeologist Deni Seymour, who found the cannon, said the Spanish didn’t return to that area for 150 years.

Mississippi tells the same story. Cobb’s team sees no sign of a significant European presence there for about 150 years after de Soto left.

Most of the metal came from plowed soil, so the team lacks a tight record of what lay around each piece. They say they wish they had that record for every object.

They also don’t think Stark Farms is Chikasha itself, where the Spanish camped. So far, they’ve found no sign of widespread burning, no horse or pig bones, and no European pottery.

Their working idea is that the metal reached people in a spread-out settlement some distance from the camp. Those residents traded with the Spaniards over the winter and gathered what the army left at the battle site. The study doesn’t say which pieces arrived which way.

A site with burned buildings, horse bones, pig bones, and Spanish pottery would be a stronger candidate for Chikasha itself. Until one turns up, the route the metal took from de Soto’s camp to Chickasaw homes stays a hypothesis.

The full study was published in the journal American Antiquity.

Look for International Archaeology Day events through the Archaeological Institute of America. The group and its local societies celebrate it every year on the third Saturday in October and list events on a public calendar.
