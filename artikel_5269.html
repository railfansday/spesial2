4 lessons from Nvidia's board reversal on Jensen Huang's return

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Nvidia's board reversed course on Jensen Huang within days. Four lessons show where that cost the board, its interim CEO and its credibility.

Boards exist to hold chief executives to account, and their final check is the power to remove one. That check works only if someone else can run the company once the chief executive is gone. When investors, employees and the public see a founder as the company itself, nobody can be sure that anyone else can. Jensen Huang left Nvidia $NVDA and was brought back by its board after an absence of 11 days, according to The Robot Report. That was too short a time to show whether Nvidia could manage without him. The episode is less a story about one chief executive than about how far a board's authority reaches once a founder's standing exceeds the board's own.

Investors made their view clear as soon as Huang left. They sharply cut Nvidia's market value after his departure, according to The Robot Report. The drop put the board at odds with the shareholders it answers to, and the costs of that mismatch spread well beyond the boardroom.

Fast Company drew out the governance lessons boards can take from Nvidia's brief leadership upheaval. They range from the thankless position of the executive who stands in for a departed founder to the gap between a board's credibility and its company's stock price. In each case, Nvidia's board made a decision it lacked the standing to hold. Huang got his job back, but the board came out of the reversal with less authority than it had going in.

A statement on an earnings call becomes a board's promise to every investor at once. Nvidia $NVDA Board Chair Harvey Jones used that setting to rule out Huang's return, then reversed himself within days, according to Fast Company. The Robot Report reports that Jones spoke on the Nov. 17 call and that the board voted to reinstate Huang six days later. "The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead NVIDIA, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers," Jones said. The board had gone back on its own word, and investors now have reason to doubt the next thing it rules out.

Jones made his promise while the pressure against it was already public, and that pressure kept up until the board gave in. President Donald Trump voiced support for Huang's possible return on Nov. 16, the day before the earnings call, and again on Nov. 22, the day before the board voted, according to The Robot Report. Support for Huang also reached well beyond Washington, as backers put more than 200,000 signatures on a "Stand With Jensen" petition, according to . Nothing on record shows which of these pressures changed the directors' minds, but the board staked its word against a campaign that was already under way.

A board's oversight of a chief executive rests on one implied threat: if the CEO fails, someone else can do the job. Boards lose that ordinary power once a founder seems too vital to replace, according to Fast Company, and Nvidia $NVDA met that condition. Duncan Clark, speaking on CNBC's "Access Middle East," judges that no single person could take over Huang's role. Nvidia's directors had the least leverage at the moment the founder mattered most, which is exactly when they would most want to use it.

Nvidia $NVDA's shareholders and its critics reached opposite verdicts about the same week. Critics see the episode as a governance failure while shareholders celebrate the stock's rally, according to Fast Company. Nvidia shares were up more than 30% before the market opened Monday, after the board reversed course and reinstated Huang, according to ABC News. Shareholders were celebrating the founder's return, not the judgment of the directors who had tried to do without him.

Shareholders celebrated before anyone had answered the questions behind the upheaval. The SEC and the Department of Justice are continuing their investigations, ABC News , and the board's own committee investigation is set to proceed to its conclusion. The directors brought Huang back before their own inquiry had finished. Shareholders now hold stock whose value depends on findings nobody has seen yet, and nothing in Monday's gains protects them if those findings go badly.

Not one director held to the chair's position when the vote came. The Robot Report reports that the decision to bring Huang back was unanimous. A unanimous reversal in less than a week leaves two explanations, and neither reflects well on the directors. Either the board never fully supported the original position, or the pressure left no director willing to defend it.

Any board can leave itself room to change course, because "not now" can survive pressure that "never" cannot.

At Nvidia, the board's check on its founder had barely been built. The company had no board chair at all before Jones, according to CNBC, and Clark calls the situation a governance failure. The founder did not overpower a board that had been working. The board had never been set up to oversee him in the first place, and the crisis brought that weakness into public view.

A founder's fame calls for more board independence, not less, in the view of Linda Ford $F, chief executive of The Chartered Governance Institute in the U.K. "A high-profile leader can be an asset, but the board needs to work hard to protect its independence and provide the scrutiny shareholders are entitled to expect," Ford told CNBC. Nvidia's board now has to build that independence after its founder has already shown he outranks the directors, which is the hardest point at which to start.

Huang won this round, and Nvidia has yet to show that a new chair can give real scrutiny to a founder back in command.

Nvidia $NVDA's stand-in chief kept the company steady while speaking for a decision the board would not keep. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress led Nvidia through the turmoil and then smoothly returned control to Huang, according to Fast Company. She took the job after a long run as the company's finance chief, a post she has held since 2013, according to CNBC. As interim chief, she also gave the board's refusal her own voice in public. "The board has determined that they will not reinstate Jensen," Kress said. She then handed the company back by carrying out the opposite of what she had told the public, and that gap is the thankless part of the job.

The board's refusal had to hold up inside Nvidia, too. A petition urging Huang's return drew more than 10,000 signers who described themselves as Nvidia employees, out of a workforce of roughly 42,000, according to CNBC. Kress assured the public that employees were committed, yet a sizable part of the staff she was meant to lead had put their names behind Huang. At an internal meeting, Kress changed the subject when Huang came up.

When the board reversed its position, the executive who had voiced it was the one left exposed, not the directors who set it. With control back in Huang's hands, Kress works for the man whose return she ruled out in public.

A board that makes its stand-in the public voice of a stance it cannot guarantee is spending that executive's credibility, not its own.

The board will have to rebuild its credibility in Washington, where a share price carries no weight. Doubt about the board's assurance that Huang poses no security risk reaches into the Republican Party. "No national security risk? Based on what?" Texas Republican Sen. John Cornyn said, as ABC News reported. Democrats bring institutional power to that scrutiny, since Sen. Elizabeth Warren is the incoming chair of the Banking Committee. Lawmakers with that kind of authority can keep asking their questions long after the news cycle moves on.

Shareholders got their founder back. The directors still have to win back the trust of regulators and Congress, and no gain in the stock can do that for them.
