7 Dystopian Books To Read if You Love 'The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping'

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Dystopias are back. The genre that took over an entire generation of young readers in the early 2010s is making a return, with the premiere of the movie adaptation of Suzanne Collins' Sunrise on the Reaping, the second prequel novel to The Hunger Games franchise, as well as the release of The Sixth Faction, a new alternate-universe book in Veronica Roth's Divergent series. These stories are entertaining adventures that follow a hero's journey, but they also include political commentary about propaganda and authoritarian states.

Suzanne Collins might be the best-known modern dystopian author, but the genre encompasses hundreds of other great stories. The books below, from cult classics to recent bestsellers and from YA to adult fiction, share their commentary on the impact of propaganda and discussions of young people's futures being sacrificed by the systems meant to protect them. If you walked out of Sunrise on the Reaping wanting more, start here.

In about 200 pages, Belgian author Jacqueline Harpman tells the story of a girl who has grown up in a cage, accompanied by 39 women and surrounded by male guards, who watch them day and night. She does not remember how or why she is there. No one else does either. One day, an alarm goes off, and the guards flee, leaving the door open. The women are able to escape, but find themselves in a deserted world, with no one else around and no information about what has happened. The book shows that the shortest stories can sometimes be the most hard-hitting.

This story would be the equivalent of Haymitch waking up in the middle of the arena, with no clue about how he got there and why. I Who Have Never Known Men's unanswered questions make the reading experience much more powerful. It's a story that questions who humans are and what their purpose is, under the guise of a story with a dystopian environment. Much like the tributes in the Hunger Games arena, the women in this universe seek to hold on to their humanity, even when the society around them has collapsed. Nonetheless, readers coming from Panem should expect a slower, stranger, and more haunting book.

Far from the Hunger Games, Neal Shusterman envisions a world with no hunger. Humanity is at peace and has ended disease and misery. The world even has no death and, instead, this society has tasked scythes with ending people's lives to prevent overpopulation. Citra and Rowan are two teenagers apprenticing for this terrible yet seemingly vital role. Only one will be chosen at the end, and the winner's task is much bleaker than either imagined.

Much like Haymitch's arena, the world of Scythe looks perfect at first. However, its deathless society soon shows its flaws. The book shows a Scythedom split between those who kill out of duty and those who enjoy it. The story then turns into an examination of power, who has it and whom it corrupts. For a book about death, it is much less bloody than some of the titles on this list. However, its reflections are no less meaningful.

Sometimes violence is eerily quiet. This is the path chosen in Kazuo Ishiguro's acclaimed novel Never Let Me Go. It is perhaps a surprising choice for fans of Sunrise on the Reaping, but Ishiguro's book features a similar theme: a society that has agreed that certain children are expendable. Instead of a spectacle, however, this world hides its abuse of children behind the polished doors of a boarding school named Hailsham.

Kathy is much older than most heroes on this list. At 31 years old, she lives in an alternative 1990s England and has been a carer for 11 years. In the novel, she relives the memories of her time at Hailsham, where she was raised by guardians who encouraged her to be creative and stay healthy. Kathy's childhood friends, Ruth and Tommy, grew up together believing in a world of possibility. They eventually learn the terrible truth: the students are actually clones created to donate their organs when needed. There is no rebellion, only the quiet desperation of a life that is not one's own. Shortlisted for the Booker Prize and adapted into a 2010 movie starring Carey Mulligan, Andrew Garfield, and Keira Knightley, it's one of the most heartbreaking books on this list, from a writer who later won the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Let's go to Ancient Rome in space — kind of. A version where people are divided by color, marking their class and career. At 16 years old, Darrow is a Red and a miner, living deep beneath the surface of Mars. He believes they are waiting underground until the planet becomes habitable. When his wife, Eo, is hanged for singing a forbidden song, Darrow learns that the Golds have actually been living on the surface for centuries, benefiting from the Reds' forced labor. After surviving his own execution, Darrow is tasked with a secret mission that will help a rising rebel group: infiltrate the Golds' elite Institute, where the higher-class teenagers are pitted against each other in a brutal war game.

Pierce Brown, the book's author, told The New York Times he was "inspired by the plight of Irish immigrants in the 19th century and by the disenfranchisement of working classes" when writing his dystopian science fiction series. The books that continue this saga are more violent than The Hunger Games, but are a great place to go after Collins' work.

Imagine a race with no finish line, in which failure means death. The Long Walk brings the reader into an imagined authoritarian United States. Every year, the regime supervises a competition which forces 100 boys to walk at a minimum speed of 4 miles per hour. Any person who falls below this speed four times is killed, until there is only one person left. The last boy standing wins the Prize: anything he wants.

Stephen King wrote The Long Walk when he was still a freshman at the University of Maine, and published it under his pseudonym Richard Bachman in July 1979. The Long Walk and Sunrise on the Reaping have more in common than one might think. For one, their two movie adaptations were directed by the same person, Francis Lawrence. However, this story is much more spare and unforgiving. There is little plot and no wide rebellion arc. The story is, instead, focused on the bonds between the boys and the impact of authoritarianism on their lives.

Inesa's mother is in debt. Immensely in debt. So much so that she has signed her 17-year-old daughter up for the Lamb's Gauntlet, a livestreamed 13-day hunt in which a debtor's "Lamb" is pursued across the wasteland by an engineered assassin called an Angel. The competition is run by Caerus, the corporation that controls all aspects of a society built on apocalyptic wastelands. Inesa feels despair at learning the news, but trusts that her hunter brother will help her survive. That is, until she meets the girl tasked with killing her: Melinoë, an experienced assassin desperate to redeem herself after breaking down on camera during her last mission.

The blurb for Fable for the End of the World describes it as "The Last of Us meets The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes," and it could not be more accurate. Ava Reid's novel is the closest thing to a Hunger Games story, with a sapphic romance that one can find in a bookshop. Two elements make the story completely unique: its romance between hunter and hunted, and the alternation of their perspectives. Inesa and Melinoë’s growing feelings for each other turn a death match into an enemies-to-lovers romance, while reflecting on the failures of a system that punishes poverty with death.

A group of junior high students is preparing for a field trip. The group includes rock star-to-be Shuya Nanahara; beautiful Mitsuko Souma; transfer student Shogo Kawada; and sociopathic prodigy Kazuo Kiriyama. They live in a fictional 1997 fascist Japan. Its authoritarian government has established the Battle Experiment No. 68 Program, a military program where young students are kidnapped and forced to kill each other until only one remains. The government claims it is research into survival skills and battle readiness, but the population knows the fear it creates is a useful method of control.

Suzanne Collins said she had never heard of Koushun Takami's Battle Royale before writing The Hunger Games. Nonetheless, the two books have a surprising number of elements in common. What sets Takami's story apart is its scale. Instead of focusing on a single point of view, his book follows dozens of students, allowing the reader to get to know their backgrounds and perspectives. Because of this, the world of Battle Royale is much more expansive, and its commentary on military regimes is much more impactful. The deaths are also much more graphic. In 2000, one year after publication, Battle Royale was adapted into a manga series, written by Takami himself, and a feature film.
