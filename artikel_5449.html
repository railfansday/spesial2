The 20 best places in the world to travel in 2027, according to National Geographic

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A selection of 20 destinations from National Geographic's Best of the World 2027, from Norway's fjords to Jaipur's 300th birthday.

Picture someone at a kitchen table, staring at an empty calendar for 2027 and trying to decide where a few hard-won weeks of vacation should go. The familiar answers arrive in a rush. Crowded capitals, overbooked beaches, and the same handful of postcard views fill social feeds and guidebooks. Each option feels safe, yet safe bets tend to send people home with thin stories. A worthwhile getaway gives a traveler a reason to show up at a particular moment, a culture to learn from, and scenery that repays effort. The right spot can turn an ordinary week off into a journey a visitor recalls for decades, while the wrong one blurs into every other trip.

Timing raises the stakes. Some places change little from one season to the next, but others shift in ways that a late arrival can miss. Anniversaries happen one time and pass. A festival pegged to a centennial or a millennium gathers performers and audiences that will not reassemble in the same form. New museums, trails, and observatories open after lengthy planning and construction, and the first months after a debut offer a chance to see them fresh. Anyone who learns about a milestone after it ends has no way to catch it. An itinerary built around such windows earns its place in memory.

National Geographic assembles its annual Best of the World list with input from its community of writers, photographers, and explorers, and it pitches the selections as a guide for letting curiosity steer the year to come. The 2027 edition, which appeared Oct. 20, 2026, names 25 destinations across six continents. It assigns no scores, metrics, or numbered positions. Each entry offers a short case for going and carries the byline of the writer who reported it. This article presents 20 of the 25 in the magazine's own order, so their sequence below signals no judgment that one outshines another.

Vestland, in Norway's southwest, contains the Hardangerfjord, the second longest fjord in Norway and the fifth longest in the world. Sheer cliffs, turquoise water, and cascading waterfalls crowd the rest of the region, and narrow inlets thread through all of it. The scenery could pass for a page of Norse mythology. Big wilderness waits in every direction, and Vestland lets visitors pair demanding outdoor adventures with wellness-focused trips in one of Europe's most dramatic settings.

Folgefonna National Park shares the southern district with the Hardangerfjord and protects one of the largest glaciers in Norway. Just east of there rises Trolltunga, a name that translates to troll's tongue. The rock formation stands 2,300 feet high and juts out over a sapphire blue lake. Within one corner of Vestland, a visitor can move from tidewater to ice field to cliff edge.

The Nærøyfjord, in northern Vestland, runs more than 10 miles between steep walls. Kayakers paddle the channel, and eco-friendly cruise boats ply the same route, some of them zero-emission electric vessels. Passengers and paddlers scan for seals, porpoises, and white-tailed eagles. The electric boats carry those watchers through the gorge without venting exhaust into it.

Seven peaks encircle Bergen, which sits beside a fjord at the center of Vestland. The city belonged to the Hanseatic League, and its merchants controlled the dried-cod trade. Bryggen, the medieval wharf quarter, keeps that history in view through cobbled lanes, crooked gables, and timber facades painted in bright colors. Much of Bergen's medieval standing rested on cod.

Gaptrast, the city's flagship restaurant, has won a second Michelin star. Chef Kristian Vangen builds seasonal tasting menus of modern Nordic food from ingredients that farmers, fishers, and foragers gather nearby. His kitchen serves the surrounding land and sea, course by course. Across Vestland, the same terrain that draws hikers, paddlers, and wildlife watchers stocks the dinner table.

Miles of rainforest trails extend past the summit of Penang Hill, giving the Malaysian state a deep reach of tropical forest. Crowds gather at the top. Quiet side paths lead hikers away from that congestion and into the trees.

Certified Penang Hill eco-heritage guides offer another way in. They walk visitors through the forest and explain the conservation work happening nearby. The guides give a walk through the canopy a purpose beyond the view.

Nature Green Farm moves the lesson from the forest to the field. Guests learn the principles of organic farming, then sit down to a hill-to-plate meal built around an interactive cooking demonstration. The format connects what grows on the hill with what lands on the plate.

George Town, Penang's capital, wears its mixed heritage in public. Traditional Chinese lanterns hang before the Acheen Street Mosque, a single view that sets two cultural lineages beside each other. Many of the city's restaurants cook dishes that draw on both Cantonese and Peranakan traditions. Diners taste the same blend that the streets display. Street art and heritage-house tours remain open to everyone who explores the city.

Soori guests receive extras beyond those public sights. Their perks include historical tours, visits to traditional teahouses, and private shows at the Teochew Puppet and Opera House. Each perk extends the heritage on display in George Town.

Across Penang, the past survives in active use, from a mosque street strung with lanterns to restaurant menus that merge two culinary lines.

The population falls short of 290,000, and the University of Wisconsin shares the ground with state government.

The 10th Echigo Tsumari Art Triennale will spread installations across some 200 villages in Niigata Prefecture from July through November 2027, and the event bills itself as the world's largest outdoor art festival. Fermentation offers a second attraction, one shaped by centuries of harsh weather. Long, cold winters nurtured sake brewing in the area, along with a wider habit of fermenting food. Ski resorts claim the freezing months, and swimming and snorkeling fill the warmer ones. A summer visit can pair the triennale's artworks with time in the water. Leipzig's Music Trail expands in 2027, when a second loop of 4.5 miles joins the existing 3.3-mile route. Together the two routes cover 7.8 miles, more than double the original length. The trail takes in Gustav Mahler's residence and the choir's campus. Walkers can follow the city's musical history on foot between concerts.

The Museum of Fine Arts holds a large collection of 17th-century Dutch Old Masters, along with many works by Lucas Cranach. Auerbachs Keller supplies a literary connection. Goethe ate there and set part of Faust in its 500-year-old dining room. The menu lists sauerbraten and wild boar with dumplings.

The Karl Heine Canal lies half an hour away by tram and attracts paddlers. Augustusplatz, the city's largest public square, frames the 36-story City-Hochhaus beside University of Leipzig buildings. One short trip can catch the Beethoven and Bach tributes in neighboring months, but the Mendelssohn Festival demands a return in November.

Baltimore's National Museum of Railroad History & Innovation displays the largest U.S. collection of 19th-century locomotives. Many of those engines stand inside the Baldwin roundhouse, which went up in 1884. The building dates from the same decades as the machines it shelters, so the structure itself becomes part of the exhibit.

Rail buffs form the museum's natural audience, yet the city also courts sports fans and art lovers. Lexington Market illustrates that broader appeal. Founded in 1782, the market predates the roundhouse by 102 years. It now trades from a modern hall decorated with local art. Dozens of merchants sell there, among them Faidley's Seafood, a crab cake specialist that one family has owned for 140 years. The market's age and its new home together show a city that keeps old institutions running in fresh surroundings.

A new promenade with pocket parks, shops, and restaurants replaces an aging mall along the Inner Harbor. The waterfront also holds the National Aquarium and the U.S.S. Constellation, a ship from 1854. The Constellation is 30 years older than the Baldwin roundhouse, giving the harbor a relic of its own from the era the railroad museum covers. Lexington Market outdates the ship by 72 years, so these three landmarks together stretch across more than a century of the city's past. Trading a shopping center for a public walkway opens those historic attractions to anyone strolling the water's edge.

Baltimore Peninsula takes shape in stages around the Sagamore Spirit whiskey distillery, with green spaces and restaurants such as Nick's Fish House filling in the neighborhood. The Baltimore Blueway has launched kayaking trails across 20 miles, letting visitors approach the city from its water.

Across the market, the harbor, and the peninsula, Baltimore gives long-standing places new purposes, and the roundhouse, now a home for vintage engines, follows the same pattern on the railroad side.

Malawi offers safari travelers the big five without packed viewing areas, and the landlocked East African country pairs its wildlife with beaches and trails that few visitors reach. National Geographic calls the country Africa's best-kept secret, a place where nature comes without the throngs.

Government initiatives and conservation groups such as African Parks have been restoring habitats and strengthening the country's tourism infrastructure. The work addresses two needs. Healthier habitat sustains the animals that bring visitors in, and sturdier facilities get those visitors to the animals. Progress on both fronts suggests Malawi intends to make wilderness the core of its travel economy.

Lake Malawi supplies the beaches in a country that touches no ocean. Senga Bay, a village on its shore, draws travelers with sand and lodges. Fishing boats dock there at sunrise, so a guest up early can watch the village's working day begin. The magazine counts Malawi's beaches among the least crowded, extending the quiet of its parks to the water's edge.

Walking routes give a Malawi itinerary a third setting beyond wildlife and lakeshore. National Geographic singles out Malawi's trails as some of the most exhilarating, which makes hiking a reason to visit on its own merits. A traveler can split one trip among game drives, mornings by the lake, and days on foot, and each part trades on the same open space. The tourism build-out aims to draw more people, so the elbow room that defines Malawi today could narrow as that work succeeds.
