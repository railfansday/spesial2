Typical claiming strategy for SocSec is for the lower earner to claim at 62 and the higher earner defers to 70 to claim, which supposedly maximizes joint-lifetime benefits presuming some minimum life-expectancy (e.g., age 85). Check your specific recommendation at https://opensocialsecurity.com.

On estimating retirement expenses, start with @KlangFool's formula to estimate your current expenses: Annual Expenses = Gross Income (1040, Line 9) - Taxes (1040, Line 24 + State + Local + SocSec + Medicare) - Annual Savings (Roth 401k, Roth IRA & Taxable). Note that suggested accounting for annual savings does not include deductible Trad 401k/IRAs because those are already accounted for in 1040, Line 1 (from W-2, Box 1) which feeds Line 9. Then adjust that by what will change in retirement (retirement savings and SocSec/Medicare taxes go away, but savings for home repair/upgrade, new cars, gifts to children remain; principal & interest eventually go away when mortgage is paid off, but property tax & hazard insurance remain; commuting costs might go down but vacation spending might go up; term life insurance payments might go away but costs for long-term care in late retirement might go up).

Typically you're doing this exercise before retirement, but if you're using this method and are already collecting SocSec, then Gross Income = 1040, Line 9 + (1040, Line 6a - Line 6b) because not all of your SocSec is Taxable and the exempt difference must be added back in to get from Taxable income to Gross (thanks to @Wrench for providing that correction for SocSec income).

The beauty of Klang's formula is that if you take out taxes and known savings you are spending all the rest of it. We've seen posters that had a detailed budget in a spreadsheet or phone app and were surprised to see Klang's formula show them as much as $50K of unaccounted spending!
You can tailor where taxes get accounted for (e.g., you could just consider it all as "retirement savings" or "lumpy expenses"), just make sure all your expenses are accounted for somewhere among taxes, savings, and monthly expenses. Don't forget stuff that gets deducted from your paycheck besides income taxes like health insurance, life insurance, pension contributions, etc.

----------
If you have a significant discretionary component to your all-in expenses, then consider an alternative to the Const-$ withdrawal strategy of the Trinity Study, such as the TPAW Planner's variable Amortization Based Withdrawal (ABW) or @longinvest's Variable Percentage Withdrawal (VPW). These variable spending approaches eliminate (or for ABW significantly reduce) SORR in exchange for a "required flexibility" (i.e., a portfolio draw reduction after bad market years that's absorbed by discretionary leaving mandatory intact). Variable spending also lets you spend more of your assets in your lifetime (including more gifting while alive) leaving a smaller residual to heirs/charity, while Const-$ tends to leave an outsized residual in the 90% of cases where it does not fail (which in practice is a forced spending reduction, not actually running out early).
2.Fixed / Bond part of portfolio – this was just rolled to Fidelity do looking for advice - MYGA bridge to Social Security??
With $3.2M and being over age 59.5, you do not need a bridge to SocSec (so no MYGA needed but could do that if it gives you a better Sleep Well At Night or SWAN factor). Just draw what you need from the Trad accounts. If you're concerned about IRMAA surcharges, then draw a mix of Trad & Roth to manage the taxable income liability. However, you should mostly have bonds, and perhaps at most 1-2 years of expenses in cash (with preferably all bonds & cash in Trad, leaving Taxable & Roth at 100% stocks for best tax-efficiency and optimization of tax-free growth). Holding 30% of your assets in cash is just reducing your overall portfolio return through Cash Drag, which is why it's stocks & bonds for the long-run, not stocks & cash.

Stocks & Bonds vs Stocks & Cash

In the long-term, lack of correlation matters more than expected volatility... in the upper chart both fictious funds A and B have high volatility of ±19.7%, but since they have perfect negative correlation of -1.00 they have zero volatility when paired together in equal amounts. There's no such investment with negative correlation & two positive returns, but even uncorrelated assets (e.g., stocks & bonds) provide a higher return than bonds alone, for a similar risk as bonds alone (e.g., 50/50 @ 9% return > 0/100 @ 7% return, but volatility is still only around ±9.5% for both in the lower chart). Cash is also uncorrelated with stocks but the long-term return on 10y T-Notes (bonds) from 1928-2017 is 5.2±7.7% while for 3m T-Bills (cash) is 3.4±3.0%, so the higher return of bonds is preferred over cash for a long-term portfolio since the expected return matters more given both are uncorrelated with stocks (thus stocks & bonds, not stocks & cash).

3. Payoff mortgage or use cash to do Roth conversions?
3a) How many years remaining on the mortgage if you stick to the original schedule? Use that to compare the risk-free of a Treasury Note of similar maturity/duration at Bloomberg. If your mortgage rate is higher than the matching Treasury rate, then pay it off, otherwise it's "leverage" because your home is an appreciating asset and your borrowing rate is lower than the risk-free return rate. See Prioritizing Investments and note that "low-interest" debt is the last priority to pay off. Certainly if your mortgage rate is than the 3m T-Bill @ 4.10%, I would not pay that off early.

3b) Roth conversions will increase your taxable income but you can't spend it (yet still counts for ACA subsidies and Medicare IRMAA surcharges), so typically conversions are to avoid a tax time-bomb when RMDs come due at age 75. I don't see a tax-bomb in your future given your current balance is < $5.4M and you'll be spending from Trad throughout retirement (and perhaps gradually reducing your AA, but all the bonds will likely remain in Trad slowing growth of that account type), but model that out.
