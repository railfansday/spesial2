Bayern Munich are at war with themselves over 'unacceptable' treatment of Jamal Musiala, writes CRAIG HOPE - now the £20m-a-year star faces a bitter fight for his future after seizure and injury hell

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Published: 02:00 EST, 28 November 2026 | Updated: 02:00 EST, 28 November 2026

It was four years ago this week that I spoke to Lothar Matthaus - and all he wanted to talk about was Jamal Musiala.

The Bayern Munich midfielder was 19 and the poster boy of Germany's impending World Cup campaign in Qatar. Indeed, quite literally, there were posters and shirts bearing Musiala's face and name in Matthaus' Bavarian apartment.

My plan was for the Germany legend to open up on his own World Cup stories, but he enjoyed more slapping me down on Musiala, who had spent much of his childhood in London and played for England Under-21s.

'I like to provoke you again,' he said, having already crowed, playfully, over penalty shootouts. 'You gave the best player from the young English generation to Germany - Jamal Musiala. I have a son who is eight years old. I have to buy the Bayern Munich shirts with No.42 on the back, and I have to buy the Germany shirt with No.14 - my son is crazy about Musiala.

'So, thank you, England, for giving us Musiala. I do not know why you did not fight more to keep this player in the English shirt. We are so happy to have him. When he is on the field, this is like Messi three years ago. He has everything. Fast, good dribbling, the last pass, he can score. He is becoming the complete player. In the future, he will be one of the very best players in the world.'

Today, you wonder which shirt adorns the wall of his son's bedroom. If it is still Musiala, it will be because of loyalty and hope that his father's belief can still be true. That, however, would contrast with the loyalty and belief of Musiala's paymasters at Bayern Munich.

Jamal Musiala has been the subject of a huge row engulfing Bayern Munich this week

The German club's hierarchy have undermined their star and the terms of his contract

For what is playing out in Bavaria right now is an unedifying and divisive debate centred, fundamentally, on the health of a 23-year-old. Musiala has, since April of last year, been under the care of neurological experts after experiencing 'absence-like' symptoms. This only became apparent to the public in August of this year when he collapsed twice in the space of four days during pre-season friendlies.

He revealed a neurological dysfunction but described it as treatable. Last week, away from the cameras, there was another seizure. And then, on Friday of this week, confirmation of a thigh injury that will keep him sidelined for several weeks.

There have been other injuries too, including a broken leg at the 2025 World Club Cup, and a 120mph car accident in April of 2025, for which Musiala lost his license. But also, two months prior, there was a new contract signed until 2030, said to be worth upwards of £20million per year. This, for Bayern, is the complication.

To put it bluntly, Musiala is now paid too much money for what the club gets in return. Since the start of last season, there have been just 14 starts, 16 substitute appearances and seven goals.

'We are in a dilemma,' said Uli Hoeness, Bayern's honorary president, earlier this month. 'He has an illness that cannot easily be cured, but can be managed reasonably well with medication. But whether he will ever again have the level of performance he once had…'

Fellow board member Karl-Heinz Rummenigge added his opinion, with all the subtlety of the goals he used to hammer for Bayern and West Germany.

'It's also well known that he has a contract that involves very substantial sums of money,' he said. 'I think it's responsible and completely normal for a club to consider how to handle this. Because then these sums are, of course – sorry, with all due respect – no longer feasible. Unfortunately, what we know suggests that it will be difficult for him to get back to the level he so often showed.'

I was at Bayern's training ground in May. Everywhere, from the club shop to the fencing around the pitches to the messaging in the media suite, are the words - 'Mia san mia'. Translated, the Bayern motto means, 'We are who we are'. At its core is an essence of family, a bond uniting players, staff and supporters. When Vincent Kompany collapsed on the training ground last month, the exhausted head coach was afforded rest and support.

Only, these past couple of weeks have not felt very 'Mia san mia', especially not for Musiala and his family. Nor, it would seem, for his team-mates.

Musiala was viewed as a future star when he first broke onto the scene with the German club

On Wednesday night, after Musiala played the final 14 minutes of Bayern's 2-1 defeat at Lille, captain Joshua Kimmich said: 'The club is always paramount and greater than any individual player - but not greater than the person. That's absolutely unacceptable and not in the spirit of Bayern, which stands for being a family and sticking together. This (Musiala's situation) is something that should have been discussed and resolved together at a table.

'Jamal has our full support 100 per cent, and I'm convinced that he has the club's support as well, even if one or two people have expressed different opinions.'

A truce, of sorts, has since been called.

'FC Bayern and Jamal Musiala's management have agreed to continue their discussions in a spirit of partnership and confidentiality,' said a club statement. 'The player's health is the top priority for both parties. Jamal Musiala will receive full support throughout his recovery.'

At 11am on Tuesday, Musiala's representatives had entered the Sabener Strabe training ground for crisis talks and the above statement was the outcome. It still leaves many more questions than answers, for Musiala and Bayern.

For starters, the treatment of the player risks unsettling and unnerving others in the dressing-room. What if Harry Kane had damaged his ankle ligaments before his new contract was signed, rather than two weeks after? Would Hoeness and Rummenigge have advised against committing to the Ballon d'Or winner? Facetious that may sound, but the thought of contracts being revisited in the event of illness or injury is a dangerous seed to plant.

Since Hoeness' comments surfaced, Bayern have drawn at home to Cologne and lost at Lille. Revelations about goalkeeper Manuel Neuer's private life have hardly helped, either. 'Cheating on pregnant women is a whole different level of evil,' posted his estranged wife. The club is unaccustomed to the headlines of late.

Christian Falk is the head of football at leading tabloid Bild and he has followed Musiala's career, and recent events, as close as anyone. So, what happens now for Musiala?

'That is the biggest question,' he says. 'There are two Bayern Munichs right now. The one Musiala has trust in on the sporting side, including Vincent Kompany and director of sport Max Eberl. Then, on the other side, the big bosses, the elder statesmen (Hoeness and Rummenigge). But at Bayern, this is where the decisions are made.

But the diagnosis of a neurological health condition has been a major setback for the star

Insiders say he has not been the same player since his long-term injury owing to a leg break

But his team-mates are rallying around him, with captain Joshua Kimmich branding his bosses' comments as 'unacceptable'

'So while everything now is calm, and Bayern have 'back-pedalled in the boat' and are offering support and help for Musiala, if there is no improvement in the next four to six months, the bosses will talk again. Everyone knows this.'

I was at Bayern's Champions League semi-final second leg against Paris Saint-Germain last season and have revisited my match report, noting the sole comment concerning Musiala, 'one run apart, he struggled'. He was, from memory, peripheral. The game was quicker than him.

Does Falk, then, believe we will ever see Musiala regain or exceed his previous level?

'Kompany has said he was at his very best in training recently,' he says. 'But everyone else is not so sure. It is not just the illness, but also the broken leg. That injury alone would have been enough to impact his level. After that, we have seen that he is not dribbling the same, he does not have the trust in his skills. A lot of the doubt is in his head. If he can remove that, he can be a very, very good player. But the same or better? Maybe not.

'Musiala always had the plan that the next step could be Real Madrid or Manchester City and winning the Ballon d'Or. If he is to be the player he wants to be, the chances are he cannot stay at Bayern, because I don't think he will forget this situation.'

Matthaus has also had his say this week, defending Kimmich.

'I think it was a fantastic interview,' he said. 'Perhaps it didn't sit well with the Bayern bosses, but it should serve as a wake-up call for them to handle situations like this differently in the future. Maybe he will be fined, but you don't resolve things by handing out fines. That would only open up another can of worms. He showed that he is a true captain because he is not only standing up for Musiala, but wants to protect all his players.'

For now, Musiala must protect himself. When he was nine years old at Corpus Christi Catholic Primary School in London, he wrote a poem, 'Moment', which won a competition. One line from that feels very poignant today.

'Suddenly the car stops,' he wrote, referencing his arrival at Chelsea's academy. 'I close my eyes. I take a deep breath. I'm not nervous anymore. I'm happy. I know what I've to do.'

This time, the challenge for Musiala is not to prove how good he can be, but to rediscover how good he was.
