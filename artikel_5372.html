20 states where the minimum wage ranges from $5.15 to $18.40 an hour

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A look at the legal minimum wage in 20 states and territories, from the District of Columbia's $18.40 to Georgia's $5.15 state rate.

Most Americans picture a single national minimum wage that protects every worker the same way, wherever that worker happens to clock in. Congress has left that federal minimum at $7.25 an hour since 2009, even as 19 states raised their own floors on Jan. 1, 2026, alone, according to CNBC. The freeze has lasted 17 years, longer than any stretch without a raise since the federal pay floor began in 1938, according to FOX 11 Los Angeles. States have stepped in, and for many of them raising the floor is now a routine part of the calendar. The District of Columbia and 30 of the 50 states require more than the federal rate, according to the St. Louis Fed. The lowest wage an American can legally earn now depends far more on which state they work in than on anything Congress does.

The typical state no longer treats the federal number as its standard. Counting the District of Columbia, the median state minimum wage is $11.85 an hour, according to the St. Louis Fed, well above the frozen federal rate. Other states have kept their floors at the federal minimum, and their workers get a raise only if Congress acts or their own state sets a higher rate. In many of the states with higher floors, voters or lawmakers tied the wage to a fixed schedule or to inflation, so it increases without a new vote each year. As a result, the distance between the two groups grows every January. States on one side raise their floors on schedule, while the floor on the other side stays where Congress left it.

The U.S. Department of Labor set out to show each state's and territory's legal minimum hourly pay and overtime rules, from Guam and Florida to Alabama and the District of Columbia. Read place by place, those legal floors show the split clearly. Some places sit exactly on the federal minimum, and others have moved well beyond it and keep climbing. Each place's dollar figure is where its story starts. How a state reached its rate, where that rate is headed, and what local pay and prices mean for a worker who earns it matter as much as the number itself.

Alabama has no minimum wage law of its own, so the lowest wage an Alabama worker can legally earn is set in Washington. Just five states have no minimum wage law, and the federal rate generally covers the workers in them, according to National Conference of State Legislatures data reported by CNBC. Besides Alabama, they are Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina and Tennessee. All five are in the South. The U.S. Department of Labor reports that the Alabama floor for employers covered by federal wage law is the federal rate of $7.25 an hour, with no state amount added. The department's listing shows no Alabama rule for premium pay after a set number of hours either. Any raise in the Alabama floor has to come from Congress.

If Congress raised the federal minimum, Alabama workers would get the raise directly, with no state law in between. A bill before Congress, the Living Wage for All act, would set a $25 hourly minimum, according to CNBC. Alabama workers are owed $7.25 today. "No one working full time should be struggling to survive," Rep. Analilia Mejia (D-N.J.) said. In Alabama, a full-time worker on the lowest legal wage earns the federal minimum and nothing more. Even if the bill passed, an Alabama worker at a smaller employer would not reach the full rate until 2038.

Congress has considered raises for years without changing the Alabama floor. The Raise the Wage Act was first introduced in 2017, according to CNBC, and Alabama workers are still owed the federal $7.25. For them, the $25 proposal is a hope, not a forecast. With no state law to fall back on, the Alabama floor stays where it is until Congress acts.

Alaska is one of the states that have left the federal floor well behind, and its current rate is one step on a schedule that voters set. The state's legal minimum is $14.00 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. At the start of 2025 it was $11.91, Alaska Beacon reports, and that rate was meant to last only until July 1, 2025, when the increase from voter-approved Ballot Measure 1 took effect. Voters, not the Legislature, set the schedule that lifted pay for Alaska's lowest-paid workers so steeply in less than two years. A worker who was on the minimum in early 2025 now has a noticeably larger paycheck for the same hours without any new law passing in Juneau.

More raises are already written into law. Alaska's minimum is scheduled to reach $15 an hour in 2027, and the rate adjusts for inflation in the years after that, according to Alaska Beacon. Once inflation adjustments begin, no lawmaker has to cast a vote for the floor to keep up with prices. Alaska's lowest-paid workers will no longer depend on the Legislature to protect their pay from rising costs, and that is the state-by-state split in its plainest form.

The measure raises pay for salaried workers as well. Alaska sets its salaried minimum at twice a full week of minimum-wage pay, according to 2024 reporting by Alaska Beacon. Each increase in the hourly rate therefore raises the minimum salary too. That wide reach helps explain why trade groups representing restaurants and bars, tourism, and oilfield services opposed Ballot Measure 1. Voters approved it anyway.

Alaska's rising floor is a choice voters made over the objections of some of the state's best-known industries, and the schedule keeps that choice in force each year without another vote.

American Samoa has no single minimum wage that a worker can look up and set beside a state's. The U.S. Department of Labor lists no basic hourly minimum for the territory and refers only to special rates. That means a worker in American Samoa has no one dollar amount to point to as the least an employer must pay. Most of the country falls on one side or the other of a clear divide: floors held at the frozen federal rate, and floors that keep rising past it. American Samoa fits neither description. Its minimum pay is set through special rates rather than one figure, so it cannot be called pinned or climbing. That makes the territory the exception to the divide, not a point somewhere along it.

The same gap extends from base pay to overtime. No premium pay rule is listed for American Samoa either. A worker who puts in a long week has no single stated standard to check their extra pay against. For a low-wage worker there, the useful question is not whether the floor is high or low. It is which of the special rates covers their job. A headline figure cannot answer that, because no single headline figure exists.

Across most of the U.S., the question is whether the lowest legal wage is stuck or rising. In American Samoa, the more basic question of what that wage is has no one-number answer.

Arizona's minimum wage is built to change every year, so the figure posted for the state marks a point along the way rather than a resting place. The state's basic rate of $15.15 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor, already puts Arizona well past the federal floor. The Arizona Daily Star reports that employers must pay $15.65 starting in January, even though no one votes on the increase. Arizona ties its floor to national consumer prices, which rose 3.4% in the year to August, so the raise depends on prices rather than politics. A worker earning the Arizona minimum can count on more pay each January without any campaign or new law.

Many Arizona workers are owed more than even the January rate, because cities can set higher minimums of their own that do not appear in the state figure. In Flagstaff, Ariz., employers must pay at least $19 an hour from Jan. 1, according to the Arizona Daily Star. The lowest legal wage in Arizona therefore depends on the city where a worker clocks in. The state rate is the least an employer anywhere in Arizona can pay, not the going minimum in every town.

Voters, not lawmakers, put Arizona on this path. The jump behind today's floor came through a 2016 ballot initiative backed by the advocacy group LUCHA, the Arizona Daily Star reports, and the yearly raises since then have been tied to prices. A bigger increase would take another ballot campaign, and those can fail, as a 2024 plan backed by SEIU did when its organizers scrapped it. Advocates are weighing another measure. Danny Seiden, executive director of the Arizona Chamber of Commerce and Industry, counts each year's raise among several costs employers already absorb, and business groups say they would oppose further increases.

Each January the floor goes up with prices, and whether it goes up faster is a question for Arizona voters, not the statehouse.

Arkansas requires employers to pay more than the federal minimum, and Arkansas workers get more for that pay than the dollar figure suggests. The state minimum wage is $11.00 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. That puts Arkansas above the federal floor but well below the highest rates in the country. Arkansas workers earn that wage in the state with the lowest prices in the nation, according to the St. Louis Fed. A minimum-wage worker in Arkansas can therefore buy more with an hour's pay than a worker at the same rate could in most other states. Anyone who judges Arkansas by its dollar rate alone misreads where its lowest-paid workers stand.

Prices in Arkansas are low enough to make a real difference to what a worker can afford. On average across the state, a typical household's basket of goods costs about 13.1% less in Arkansas than the national average, according to the St. Louis Fed, though prices vary from place to place within the state. Because prices there are below the national average, the $11 wage buys more than $11 would buy in a typical state.

Adjusting for prices can lower the value of a minimum wage as easily as raise it. Employers in New Hampshire need pay only the federal minimum, and once the state's higher prices are counted, that wage is worth just $6.96 an hour, according to the St. Louis Fed. In Arkansas the same adjustment works in workers' favor. A fair comparison between states asks what each minimum wage can buy, not where its dollar figure falls on a chart.

The posted rate tells only half the story, and Arkansas workers on a middling minimum wage can afford more than the number suggests.

California is the clearest example of a state that raises its wage floor by formula rather than through a new political fight each year. Its minimum wage is $16.90 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor, and state law adjusts it every year by a set formula. When Gov. Gavin Newsom took office, California's floor was $12 an hour, FOX 11 Los Angeles reports, and the state's lowest-paid workers have won a large raise over his tenure while the federal rate never changed. State officials announced the next step in July 2026, a scheduled statewide rate of $17.40. The figure a California worker reads today is already on its way out.

California has kept adding jobs while its minimum wage climbed, which cuts against the usual worry that a floor this high leads employers to stop hiring. The state added more than 131,000 jobs over the prior year, according to state figures reported by FOX 11 Los Angeles. Hiring and a floor that rises each year have gone together in California, though that does not show the higher wage caused the hiring. Newsom treats the gap with Washington as a deliberate choice about what work is worth. "They think $7.25 an hour is enough. We don't," Newsom said in a statement, as reported by FOX 11 Los Angeles.

The statewide rate is the least a worker anywhere in California can count on, and many workers in its cities are owed more. Local governments may set higher minimum wages of their own. How the next statewide raise will fit with those higher city rates and with wage rules for particular industries remains an open question, according to FOX 11 Los Angeles. A California worker who wants to know their real floor has to check the rules for their city and their industry as well as the state figure.

In California, the minimum wage goes up every year by law, and even so it is only the bottom layer of what local rules may require.

In Colorado, the legal minimum wage goes up every year without a vote by lawmakers. Colorado workers are owed at least $15.16 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. As recently as 2019, The Denver Gazette reports, the state minimum was $11.10. Colorado's lowest-paid workers have therefore seen their legal floor rise well above its 2019 level. Today's rate is also scheduled to be replaced in 2027 by $15.71 an hour. No lawmaker has to act for that to happen, because the rate is adjusted each year to follow the consumer price index for all urban consumers. For a Colorado worker, a yearly raise is built into the law.

For some Colorado workers, the state rate is not the one that sets their pay. Local governments may set minimums above the state's, and Denver's minimum is set to be $19.84 in 2027, according to The Denver Gazette. A Denver worker will be owed well above even the state's higher 2027 rate. For a minimum-wage worker in Denver, the statewide figure is not the floor that applies.

Restaurants depend on hourly staff, and their trade group is pushing back against those higher local floors. "Increasing the minimum wage to $19.84 (in Denver) in 2027 will add tens of thousands of dollars in new annual costs for every local restaurant, shrinking their impossibly tight margins even further," Sonia Riggs, president of the Colorado Restaurant Association and Foundation, told The Denver Gazette. For restaurant owners, the same increase that raises a Denver worker's pay is a new yearly cost. The state rate is adjusted by a formula, so that argument returns every year and no vote ever settles it.

In Colorado, each year's increase is certain, and the open question is who absorbs it.

Connecticut is among the states that have moved past the federal minimum wage, and it raises its rate often enough that any quoted figure goes out of date quickly. Employers in the state must pay at least $16.94 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. The St. Louis Fed reports that workers reached that level only this year, with a raise of 59 cents an hour over 2025. A rate this new shows where Connecticut's floor stands today rather than what the state has long paid its lowest-paid workers. For anyone setting pay or weighing a job in Connecticut, the more useful question is what the next raise will be.

Connecticut's raises have come unevenly, not as a steady yearly increase. The state minimum stood at $14.00 an hour in 2022 and stayed there in 2023, according to the St. Louis Fed, so the state's lowest-paid workers went a full year without a raise. In 2024 those workers got a raise of $1.69 an hour all at once, the largest increase in this stretch. The raises since then have been smaller, but workers have received one every year. A worker who compared the 2022 and 2023 rates would have seen no change at all. A worker who checked again a year later would have found a much higher number.

Connecticut has held its minimum flat, raised it sharply and then kept raising it in smaller amounts, so any figure for the state should always carry its date.

Delaware belongs with the states that have left the frozen federal minimum behind, not with the states still pinned to it. The least a Delaware employer can legally pay is a round $15 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor, and that state rate stood as of Nov. 5, 2026. A Delaware worker at the bottom of the pay scale is therefore not living on the national floor described earlier. Their minimum is a state-set figure well above it, and state law, not federal law, decides what they earn.

The difference changes where the decisions that matter to that worker get made. In states pinned to the federal minimum, a worker's next raise depends on a Congress that has left its number untouched for years. In Delaware, Washington's long freeze is mostly beside the point for the lowest-paid workers. Their pay already starts above the federal number, so a modest federal increase that stayed below $15 would leave their paychecks exactly where they are. The question that matters for them is when the state chooses to move its own floor, not whether Congress ever moves the national one. A worker who wants to know where their wage is headed has to look to the state capital in Dover rather than to Capitol Hill.

Delaware's lowest-paid workers start from $15 an hour, a floor far above anything Congress now guarantees.

The District of Columbia had the highest minimum wage in the country in 2026, according to the St. Louis Fed. The U.S. Department of Labor reports that employers in the District must pay at least $18.40 an hour. That rate puts the capital at the opposite end of the split from the frozen federal minimum. A worker earning the District's minimum is guaranteed more than the minimum in any state. For the lowest-paid workers in the capital, a job across the border in Maryland or Virginia would come with a lower legal floor.

The $18.40 rate will not stay in place for long, because the District adjusts its minimum wage every July 1. Workers can expect a new floor each summer. A set formula decides the size of each raise, which makes the climb a standing rule rather than a new decision that has to be argued over every year. Workers in the District have already seen several of those raises in their pay. The minimum stood at $16.10 an hour in 2022, according to FRED, and it has risen to today's rate within a few years. An employer setting wages or a worker weighing a job offer should make sure a quoted figure reflects the most recent July adjustment.

In the capital, the lowest legal wage climbs on a fixed schedule, while the federal minimum has not moved in years.

Florida voters, not the Legislature, decided what the state's lowest-paid workers earn. Employers in Florida must pay at least $15.00 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. The News Service of Florida reports that voters set that rate through a 2020 constitutional amendment, which raised the floor by $1 a year until it reached $15. Workers could see each raise coming years in advance. Voters wrote the schedule into the state constitution, so each increase took effect on time without lawmakers in Tallahassee having to vote on wages.

Florida's floor will next rise with prices rather than by a fixed dollar each year. The $1 steps end at $15, and after that the rate grows based on inflation, according to the News Service of Florida. The first annual formula adjustment does not take effect until Jan. 1, 2028, and a worker earning $15 should not expect another automatic raise before then. Once the state starts indexing the rate, raises will be small in years of low inflation and larger in years of high inflation. That design helps a paycheck keep up with living costs rather than get ahead of them.

Florida's climb toward a $15 floor has not come with a boom in the jobs most likely to pay near the minimum. Florida's hotel and food-service employers had 2,900 fewer jobs than a year earlier, according to the News Service of Florida, in the year before the final step to $15 took effect on Sept. 30, 2026. Nothing in those figures shows that the rising wage caused the losses, and the two trends simply ran side by side. The state's own forecasters expect pay to rise faster than hiring. Amy Baker, the state economist who coordinates the Legislature's Office of Economic & Demographic Research, expects average annual wages in Florida to grow far faster than employment over the next four years. Florida's outlook, then, rests on workers earning more rather than on many more people finding work.

In Florida, the law can guarantee what a job pays, but it cannot guarantee the job itself.

Georgia's own minimum wage is lower than the federal one, so the floor written into state law is not the floor most Georgia workers actually stand on. Under state law, employers with six or more employees must pay at least $5.15 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. Employers covered by federal law must pay $7.25. For most jobs in the state, the real minimum is the federal one, and Georgia workers are owed more than their own state promises them. For those workers, the state wage is a number on paper.

The $5.15 rate still applies to some people: workers whose jobs federal law does not reach. "The State law excludes from coverage any employment that is subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act when the federal rate is greater than the State rate," the department's Wage and Hour Division said. Georgia's own number sets the pay floor only for workers at employers that state law covers and federal law does not, and for them that floor is $5.15. Everyone else in the state is owed the federal rate, because Georgia has set no higher rate of its own.

Georgia law adds nothing of its own on overtime either. The state lists no premium pay after a set number of hours, so state law promises a Georgia worker nothing extra for a long week. On the hourly rate, Georgia's lowest-paid workers rely on federal law, and on extra hours their own state offers them nothing at all.

For most low-wage workers in Georgia, the pay floor that counts is the one set in Washington.

Guam's lowest-paid workers are not on the federal minimum. The territory's legal floor is $9.25 an hour, above the national rate, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. Being above the federal floor is different from climbing past it, though. Guam has no listed annual adjustment formula, so its rate is not raised automatically as prices go up. That puts the island apart from the places where the floor is lifted every January without a new vote.

On Guam, the minimum wage shapes pay across whole occupations, not just a few entry-level jobs. Food preparation and serving workers on the island earn a mean of $11.28 an hour, according to BLS, only a little more than the legal minimum. For a large group of workers, then, the floor is close to typical pay rather than far below it. With no formula in place, nothing in the law lifts that floor for them as living costs climb. Any increase in their pay has to come from employers or from a change to the law.

Typical pay on Guam is low by national standards. Across all jobs on the island, workers averaged $22.21 an hour in May 2025, well short of the $33.54 nationwide average, according to BLS. With ordinary wages that much lower, a modest legal minimum matters more to Guam workers than it would on much of the mainland.

On Guam, the legal floor is higher than the federal one, but no listed rule raises it on its own.

Hawaii's minimum wage caught up with several mainland states in a single step at the start of 2026. Employers in Hawaii must pay at least $16 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor, and that rate has applied only since Jan. 1, 2026. CNBC reports that the floor was $14 before that date, that the raise was the largest among the states that increased their minimums in January, and that Rhode Island and parts of New York reached the same $16 rate on the same day from higher starting points. Workers in those places had been guaranteed more than workers in Hawaii until the new year. Hawaii stands among the places well past the federal floor because of a fast catch-up, not a long lead. A worker or employer who still has last year's figure in mind is underestimating what the law now requires.

Hawaii's tipped workers are guaranteed nearly the full minimum wage, and that protection matters to servers and bartenders more than the headline figure does. In many places, the posted minimum can hide a much lower floor for people who earn tips. CNBC reports that the tipped minimum in Hawaii is $14.75 an hour, just under the standard $16 rate, while the tipped minimum in Missouri is $7.50 even after that state's own January increase. A tipped worker in Missouri can be guaranteed far less than the standard rate, while a tipped worker in Hawaii is owed almost all of it. In Hawaii, the legal floor for a server is close to the number on the poster, and few states can say the same.

Idaho has set no pay floor of its own above the one Congress set. The state's basic minimum rate is $7.25 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor, identical to the federal rate. Many American workers at the bottom of the pay scale earn more because their state set a floor above the federal one, but Idaho's basic rate gives its workers no such increase. Idaho's basic rate adds nothing above the federal figure. That places Idaho with the states pinned to the frozen federal number rather than with the states whose floors keep climbing past it. For Idaho's lowest-paid workers, a higher legal minimum would require action from Congress or from Idaho lawmakers.

The $7.25 rate is current, not an old figure left over from an earlier year. As of a Nov. 5, 2026, update, Idaho's basic minimum still stood at $7.25, late in a year when other states raised their own floors. On that date, the least an Idaho employer could pay under the state's basic rate was no more than what federal law requires. The state's rate stayed at the federal level while much of the country set higher ones. Workers in those other states began the next year with a larger guaranteed paycheck. Idaho workers on the minimum began it with the same floor they already had.

Crossing into Idaho brings a low-wage worker no higher floor than the federal minimum, and nothing in the state's basic rate is set to change that.

In Illinois, state law rather than Congress now decides the least an employer can pay. Illinois requires a minimum wage of $15 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. That puts the state's floor well clear of the federal minimum and places Illinois with the states that have moved past the national number instead of staying pinned to it. A covered worker in Illinois earns a floor set at the state level, and that floor sits far above anything Congress has guaranteed. For that worker, the state line matters more to the size of a paycheck than any decision made in Washington.

The $15 rate does not cover every worker in Illinois. The state applies its minimum wage only to employers with four or more employees, and family members do not count toward that total. A business with fewer than four employees falls below the line, and relatives who work for the owner do not help a business reach it. Workers at those businesses fall outside the state law that sets the $15 floor. Read that way, $15 is the standard for most Illinois workplaces, not a promise to every worker in the state.

Even on the side of the country where states have raised their floors well past the federal minimum, the headline rate comes with limits. A worker at one of the smallest businesses in Illinois should check the employer's size before counting on $15.

Indiana law guarantees the state's lowest-paid workers exactly the federal minimum wage. The state's basic rate is $7.25 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor, and Indiana has written no higher figure into its own law. That places the state with those whose floors match the federal rate, not with those that have raised theirs above it. For an Indiana worker at the bottom of the pay scale, the lowest legal wage is whatever Congress sets, and it goes up only when the federal rate does.

Indiana's own law matters for how many workers the minimum reaches, not for how much it pays. Any business with as few as two people on its payroll owes them at least $7.25, so the rule covers even the smallest shops. Only an employer with a single worker falls outside it. Indiana has spread its floor across nearly the full range of business sizes while keeping it at the federal rate.

The state also decides when premium pay begins, and it counts hours by the week rather than by the day. Indiana workers earn overtime after 40 hours in a week, so a single long shift brings no extra pay unless it pushes the week's total past 40. A worker earning $7.25 who puts in a few very long days in an otherwise short week can end up with no premium pay at all.

In Indiana, state law decides who must pay the minimum and when overtime starts, but it leaves the amount to Washington.

Iowa has written no wage floor of its own above the federal one, so its lowest-paid workers get exactly what Washington guarantees. The least an employer in Iowa can legally pay is $7.25 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. That is the same federal figure that applies across the country, and Iowa law adds nothing on top of it. In states with higher floors of their own, the federal rate is a fallback beneath a better state wage. In Iowa, the federal rate is the entire guarantee.

That places Iowa firmly with the states pinned to the federal minimum rather than with those that have set their own rates. The consequence for pay is direct. As long as Iowa's legal floor is the federal figure, it goes up only when Congress raises the national minimum. A worker doing the same job across the line in a state with its own higher floor would be owed more for each hour. Iowa lawmakers could change that by passing a separate state rate. Until they do, the state's lowest-paid workers wait on Congress, and the choice to stop waiting belongs to the Legislature.

For an Iowa worker earning the minimum, a higher legal wage can come from Congress or from the statehouse, and so far neither has acted.

Kansas workers at the bottom of the pay scale are owed exactly what federal law requires, with nothing added by the state. A covered Kansas worker must be paid at least $7.25 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. That is the same frozen figure that applies to anyone on the national minimum, and it was still the Kansas rate as of Nov. 5, 2026. Kansas has set no state rate above it, so the lowest-paid workers in the state have no higher state wage to fall back on. For them, a raise in the legal floor can come only from Congress or from a new law passed in Topeka.

State law in Kansas defers to federal law rather than going past it. Wherever federal law reaches a job, the state law steps aside. "The State law excludes from coverage any employment that is subject to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act," the Labor Department said in its listing of state minimum wage laws. The Kansas law therefore covers only the jobs that federal law leaves out, and it pays those workers the same $7.25 an hour. No premium pay after a set number of hours is listed for Kansas either, so those workers can find no state promise of a higher rate for a long week.

Whichever law covers a job in Kansas, the lowest legal wage comes out the same.

Kentucky has set no minimum wage above the federal one, and its lowest-paid workers are owed the same unchanged federal rate as workers in the other states that never moved. The state's basic minimum is $7.25 an hour, according to the U.S. Department of Labor. That is the federal figure exactly, with nothing added by state law. Kentucky has no state schedule or inflation formula to raise that pay. Its workers at the bottom of the pay scale get a higher floor only when Congress raises the federal one.

For one group of public employees, Kentucky law also changes how overtime is paid. Overtime is usually the most dependable way for a low-wage worker to earn more than the hourly minimum in a given week. In Kentucky, people who work for counties, charter counties, consolidated local governments and urban-county governments can receive time off instead of overtime pay, and that includes people employed by county-elected officials. The rule covers public employees, not the private workforce. The state allows the swap only when the employee asks for it in writing. The decision lies with the worker, and the worker has to put it on paper.

For a county worker earning $7.25, that decision carries real weight. Each overtime hour taken as time off is extra money that never appears in that week's paycheck. The posted rate covers only part of what such a worker earns. The rest depends on whether the worker takes the extra hours as pay or as time.
