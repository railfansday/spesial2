Drinking beer and spirits raises risk of acute kidney damage, study suggests

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Research also shows consuming wine could cut risk, reopening debate on whether beverage has health benefits

Drinking beer and spirits increases the risk of serious kidney damage, whereas consuming wine offers protection against it, a study suggests.

People who drink nine units of spirits a week are 26% more likely to suffer acute kidney injury than those who consume only one unit a week.

Beer lovers are also at higher risk. Those who drink 24 units of beer a week are 15% more likely to develop the condition than those who consume three units weekly.

In contrast, drinking fewer than 14 units a week of either red or white wine does not raise the risk of kidney damage. In fact it appears to reduce it.

The findings, published in the journal Scientific Reports, have reopened the longstanding debate about whether drinking wine offers health benefits.

The academics behind the research, from Anglia Ruskin University in Cambridge, acknowledge in their paper that “the relationship between alcohol consumption and acute kidney injury (AKI) is controversial”.

They analysed data detailing the health of 250,791 adults from the UK Biobank study who drank alcohol and followed them over a period of 14 years.

Although beer and spirits increase the risk of acute kidney injury, Dr Rudolph Schutte and colleagues also found that wine drinkers become exposed to the same risk if they consume more than 14 units a week, the maximum alcohol intake recommended by the UK’s chief medical officers.

They concluded that there is “a direct relationship between alcohol consumption and AKI”. However, they stressed that they had only found an association between them and could not prove that the relationship is causal.

“Acute kidney injury is a serious condition that can lead to chronic kidney disease, kidney failure and an increased risk of dying early,” said Schutte.

“While alcohol has traditionally been linked with kidney injury through its impact on the liver, our research suggests that spirits and beer may contribute directly to kidney damage through inflammatory pathways.”

They found that those with a higher risk of AKI had higher than usual levels of an inflammatory marker called C-reactive protein and a raised neutrophil count.

“Spirts and beer appear to drive these inflammatory markers while red wine and white wine showed inverse protective relationships,” they said.

“Even consuming less or equal to 14 units per week captured from spirits [was] associated with an increased risk for AKI.

“Conversely, no associations existed between red wine, white wine and AKI, except for a protective tendency of red wine consumption in men and when consuming less or equal to 14 units per week.”

Dr Peter Rice, the chair of the Institute of Alcohol Studies, urged caution about the interpretation of studies that find that red wine is healthier than other forms of alcohol.

“Studies which examine the health impacts of different alcoholic beverages are difficult to undertake due to a number of factors,” he said.

“In general, wine consumers tend to have fewer dietary and activity risk factors and are more affluent.

“The effect of the alcohol molecule on body tissues does not vary with the type of alcoholic product, therefore any difference in health outcomes is often due to other risk factors, in particular deprivation.”
