Nearly 15% of Americans expect prices to rise 15% or more next year, a pace the U.S. hasn’t seen in 79 years

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Even in 1979, the year of the Iranian revolution, gas lines and Paul Volcker, American inflation never got above 15%. It peaked just below that level in early 1980 before Volcker’s rate hikes broke it, according to the Federal Reserve’s own history. Prices haven’t risen faster than 15% a year since 1947.

A meaningful slice of Americans expects prices to rise faster than that over the next year anyway. “Almost 15% of consumers expect (or claim they expect) inflation of over 15%,” UBS chief economist Paul Donovan wrote in a Nov. 20 note titled “The benign inflation scenario.” In the University of Michigan’s October survey, 16% of consumers said prices would rise 15% or more over the next year, about the same share as a year earlier, according to the survey’s own tables.

The typical household is far calmer, and getting calmer. Median year-ahead inflation expectations “plunged from 4.7% last month to 3.7% this month,” University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers director Joanne Hsu said in the final November reading released Friday. That gap between the most frightened consumers and the data is what anyone setting prices or pay should watch. In the late 1970s, expectations that came loose from the numbers ended up feeding the inflation.

Since the government’s consumer price records begin in 1913, 12-month inflation has cleared it in only two stretches: on and off from 1917 to 1920, peaking at 23.7% in June 1920, and from November 1946 to June 1947, after World War II price controls came off, according to BLS data compiled by the St. Louis Fed and the BLS’s own history of the index.

To hit 15% next year, prices would have to rise about four times as fast as the 3.7% they rose in the 12 months through September.

Consumers “tend to expect future inflation that is higher than official estimates of current inflation,” Fed economists wrote in a 2016 note, in part because they remember price increases more readily than cuts and weight the things they buy most often. They also feel price levels, not rates: grocery prices were up more than 27% from five years earlier as of early 2025, the Minneapolis Fed noted, even as the inflation rate itself had cooled.

Partisanship matters, too, Michigan’s survey team has found. Independents’ inflation expectations “have consistently been closer to the political party not in the White House,” Michigan’s survey team found in 2024, and survey director Joanne Hsu said “the overwhelming size and persistence of the partisan gap has generated substantial economic uncertainty.”

Voters have already said what they think of prices. About 9 in 10 midterm voters called the cost of living a “crucial” or “major” factor in their vote for Congress, and about 6 in 10 of them backed Democratic House candidates, according to the AP Voter Poll of roughly 100,000 voters. Republicans took a blistering set of losses in House, Senate and governor’s races, AP reported. About two-thirds of voters said President Trump’s policies had fueled inflation; in 2022, only about half said the same of President Biden’s, AP reported.

Everyday costs weighed hardest. About 6 in 10 voters said they were “very concerned” about food and grocery costs, according to AP’s analysis of the poll, and about 1 in 5 said inflation had caused their family severe hardship, according to CBS News exit polls. Among voters earning less than $50,000, about 6 in 10 said they were worse off than two years earlier.

Americans have braced for price spikes before. In May 2008, with gas at records, Michigan’s year-ahead expectations jumped to 5.2%, the highest since February 1982, CNBC reported. Inflation did climb, to 5.6% in July 2008. Then the financial crisis hit, and by July 2009 prices were 2.1% lower than a year earlier, per BLS data.

The bigger miss came last year. Tariff announcements pushed Michigan’s year-ahead expectations to 6.5% in April 2025, the highest since 1981, with 60% of respondents volunteering comments about tariffs, the Boston Globe reported; the preliminary May reading hit 7.3%, before the final came in at 6.6%, per the University of Michigan. Actual inflation that April was 2.3%.

This time the median isn’t the outlier. Michigan’s typical household now expects 3.7%, the same rate as September’s actual inflation.

In 1979, the Iranian revolution cut output by 4.8 million barrels a day, about 7% of world production, and oil prices more than doubled between April 1979 and April 1980, per the Fed. This year it’s the Iran war. Brent crude is up more than 60% from before the fighting and briefly topped $117 a barrel in late October, Al Jazeera reported. U.S. diesel hit records in September. Gasoline was up 37.4% in the 12 months through October, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Those are the prices people see every week, and they drive what households expect. Donovan’s argument is that U.S. median inflation “has been falling to near pre-pandemic norms,” while the visible costs, airfares and diesel-driven shipping, come from the Iran war. Airline fares rose again in October, per BLS.

Fed researchers have tracked this pattern for decades. A 2016 Fed note found households hear more news about prices when gas prices swing hard; in 1979, a year when gas rose around 20% or more, more than 10% of households reported hearing bad news about rising prices.

Back then the inflation was everywhere. Consumer prices rose 13.3% in 1979 and 12.5% in 1980. This October, BLS said prices rose 0.4% for the month, with core prices up 0.3%. It couldn’t publish an annual rate, because it collected no prices in October 2025, when the government shut down. Bank of America estimates the rate at about 3.8% headline and 2.7% core.

Michigan’s median household expected 9.7% inflation in both 1979 and 1980, according to the 2016 Fed note. Boston Fed researchers Philippe Andrade and Michael Wicklein found in 2025 that the 1978–80 surge in expectations was “much larger than the increase that rising prices usually would imply.” More than 5 percentage points of it couldn’t be explained by actual prices at all; gasoline accounted for about one point. They call it the de-anchoring of expectations, and it’s what Volcker spent the early 1980s grinding out.

The central bank is the other difference. The fed funds rate went from 10% at the start of 1979 to 19% by the middle of 1981, per the St. Louis Fed, and unemployment topped 10%. This Fed raised rates a quarter point in September, then held them at 3.75% to 4% at its Oct. 28 meeting, with two officials voting for another hike.

In 1979, the typical household’s 9.7% guess was, if anything, too low; inflation came in at 13.3%. Donovan’s slice of consumers is expecting more than the Volcker era ever delivered, while the median household now expects 3.7%. The next read on who’s right is the November consumer price index, due Dec. 10.

Nick Lichtenberg is business editor and was formerly Fortune's executive editor of global news.
