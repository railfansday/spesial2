Tech stocks claim 17 of the S&P 500's 20 biggest gainers of 2026's first half, led by Sandisk's 858% climb.

Anyone who opened a brokerage statement this spring saw a split screen. Broad index funds delivered respectable returns, yet a narrow band of companies moved with a force that made the benchmark look sleepy. Owning the right handful of names in 2026 separated a decent year from a remarkable one, and missing them carried a real cost. A portfolio spread across hundreds of businesses captures the average. Knowing which companies drove those gains, and why analysts kept raising their expectations for them, helps investors judge whether the momentum rests on profits or on hope.

The S&P 500 has gained 9.5% through the first six months of 2026, a solid showing even though it trails the 12.8% yearly average the index has posted over the past decade. Profit forecasts have outrun prices along the way. The index's weighted forward price-to-earnings ratio slipped to 20.3 from 22.5 at the close of 2025, which means analysts lifted their earnings projections faster than buyers bid up shares. Advances spread across the market, with 62% of S&P 500 members trading higher this year. Information technology climbed 19.4%, second among the index's 11 sectors and a hair behind industrials at 19.5%. Price runs this steep among a select group of stocks raise a sharper question for anyone weighing them at midyear. Can earnings keep pace with share prices that sprinted ahead after January?

MarketWatch writers Philip van Doorn and Britney Nguyen ranked the 20 S&P 500 stocks with the biggest price increases in the first half of 2026, using LSEG data through June 30. The authors credit this year's bull run to upward revisions in earnings estimates, and their list measures price change alone, leaving out dividends. Sandisk climbed 858% in the first half of 2026, the largest gain in the S&P 500, yet the stock trades at a lower multiple of expected earnings than it did in December. Its forward P/E slipped to 12.0 from 13.6 at the end of 2025, a drop of 1.6 points. Analysts raised their rolling 12-month earnings-per-share estimates for the company by 987% over the same stretch. Projected profit outran a rally that left the stock price at more than nine times its year-end level. Investors paid more for each share in June, yet they paid less for each dollar of earnings analysts expect the company to produce.
