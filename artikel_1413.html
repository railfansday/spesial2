MCT1-dependent mitochondrial lactate import sustains cardiac metabolism

The heart possesses a dynamic capacity to oxidize a variety of fuels, including glucose and lactate, to meet its energetic demands under diverse dietary, exertion and injury stressors1. Oxidation of carbohydrate substrates is thought to converge on the cytosolic generation of pyruvate, which is transported by the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) for entry into the tricarboxylic acid cycle2. Here we find that glucose-derived carbon is oxidized in mouse myocardium even when the MPC is genetically ablated. Using genetic and pharmacological tools, we demonstrate that lactate monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1, encoded by Slc16a1) is required for the oxidation of glucose carbon by MPC-deficient myocardium. We further observe that MCT1, traditionally localized to the plasma membrane, also resides in the inner mitochondrial membrane of mouse and human cardiomyocytes. Together with matrix-localized LDHB, MCT1 enables mitochondrial lactate conversion to pyruvate and NADH, directly fueling the electron transport chain. Loss of MCT1 impairs lactate oxidation, disrupts metabolic flexibility and accelerates the development of heart failure with increased mortality after hyperactivation of the neurohormonal axis or ischemia–reperfusion injury. Thus, the existence of a parallel lactate-based pathway for carbohydrate oxidation can sustain cardiac energetics and maintain myocardial structure and function under diverse stress conditions.

The oxidation of carbon fuels within the mitochondria is responsible for more than 95% of human ATP production1,3. Dietary carbohydrates supply energy beginning with glycolysis, producing pyruvate that is either reduced to lactate in the cytosol by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which also regenerates NAD+, or is imported into the mitochondria for oxidation by the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle4. Circulating lactate turnover exceeds that of glucose in mammalian physiology5,6, establishing lactate production as the dominant mode of cytosolic NAD+ regeneration. Lactate has the same oxidation state as glucose, is a major circulating oxidative fuel source in both fasted and fed mammalian physiology, and is consumed primarily by the heart, skeletal muscle and kidney7,8,9.

Conventional models posit that lactate oxidation starts with the generation of cytosolic pyruvate by LDH, which is subsequently imported into the mitochondria by the obligate heterodimeric mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC, encoded by Mpc1 and Mpc2)2,10. This arrangement leads to competition between LDH and glycolytic enzymes for NAD+, restricting flux through these pathways when metabolic demand is high (Fig. 1a). However, the heart can oxidize both lactate and glucose simultaneously in response to energetic demand11,12,13, suggesting that there might be distinct routes for pyruvate and lactate oxidation14,15.

Here we reconcile the conflict between lactate, glucose and pyruvate oxidation by demonstrating that MCT1, which is canonically on the plasma membrane, is also present on the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) of cardiomyocytes. MCT1 is necessary for the uptake and oxidation of lactate, providing metabolic redundancy that sustains cardiac energetics. Genetic ablation of MCT1 abolishes mitochondrial lactate oxidation, disrupts adaptation to neurohormonal, hemodynamic or ischemic stress and accelerates the development of heart failure with enhanced mortality.

To test the prevailing model of lactate oxidation in heart, we utilized our previously described tamoxifen-inducible cardiac-specific Mpc1-knockout mice (Mpc1fl/fl; αMHCMerCreMer+/−, hereafter Mpc1iCKO) to quantify the extent to which the oxidation of glucose and lactate in the myocardium requires the MPC16. We implanted jugular vein catheters into 4 week post-tamoxifen injection (wpi) Mpc1iCKO mice and Mpc1fl/fl littermate controls with normal cardiac function and subsequently infused awake mice with [U-13C]glucose or [U-13C]lactate to reach stable serum enrichment (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Hearts and serum were then collected for isotopic labelling analysis of metabolites by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) (Fig. 1b). Similar to prior reports, we observed a rapid appearance of [1,2,3-13C]lactate in serum upon [U-13C]glucose infusion and labelled glucose with [U-13C]lactate infusion, reflecting high rates of interconversion between these metabolites6 (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). There was no difference in total serum isotopic enrichment and endogenous rates of glucose and lactate appearance between Mpc1fl/fl and Mpc1iCKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 1b–e). We anticipated that the contribution of glucose to TCA cycle metabolites and thus cardiac ATP production would decrease in Mpc1iCKO mice. Unexpectedly, we observed that the glucose carbon contribution to lactate, pyruvate and TCA cycle metabolites increased in Mpc1iCKO mice compared with controls, a finding also reported by others17,18 (Fig. 1c). 13C labelling in the M+1 and M+2 isotopomers of representative TCA metabolites succinate and malate from Mpc1iCKO hearts was increased, but no changes in M+3 isotopomer labelling were seen, indicating enhanced glucose contribution through acetyl-CoA entry to the TCA cycle19 (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). The contribution of lactate carbon to TCA cycle metabolites trended lower in Mpc1iCKO hearts but was not significantly different for any individual metabolite (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1h,i). Using a linear algebra-based approach6, our combined glucose and lactate infusion data showed that Mpc1iCKO hearts used the same total amount of carbohydrate-sourced fuel to supply the TCA cycle, but that it was paradoxically shifted towards glucose in MPC1-deficient hearts (Extended Data Fig. 1j). We performed additional infusions of other major cardiac fuels (palmitate, oleate and 3-hydroxybutyrate) to determine whether Mpc1iCKO mice displayed systemic alterations in cardiac fuel choice that may have confounded our glucose and lactate tracing results (Extended Data Fig. 1k–m). However, the contributions of these fuels to the cardiac TCA cycle were not statistically different from controls, suggesting that heart glucose metabolism is resilient to loss of the MPC in the short term.

Our data from young Mpc1iCKO mice suggests the presence of an MPC-independent mitochondrial import pathway for glucose and lactate carbons. To better understand the cardiomyocyte-intrinsic metabolism of glucose and lactate, we used primary cultured adult cardiomyocytes (ACMs). Control ACMs were competent to oxidize glucose as assessed by 13C incorporation into TCA cycle metabolites (Fig. 1e). However, when unlabelled lactate was added to standard lactate-free ACM culture medium (DMEM) containing [U-13C]glucose, labelling from glucose was attenuated, suggesting a strong preference for lactate over glucose oxidation (Fig. 1e). Lactate consumption was not affected by loss of MPC1 as ACMs from Mpc1iCKO and Mpc1fl/fl mice metabolized lactate into TCA metabolites equivalently (Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Mpc1iCKO mice develop heart failure16,17,20,21 and we investigated whether changes in glucose and lactate oxidation were now present. Mpc1iCKO mice at 16–20 wpi exhibited reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and hypertrophy (Fig. 1f,g). To successfully introduce stable isotopes into these mice with heart failure, we adopted our protocol to employ a tail vein catheter infusion administered under anaesthesia. Oxidation of glucose and lactate carbon was significantly reduced in failing Mpc1iCKO hearts, despite similar pyruvate and lactate labelling indicating that carbohydrate uptake was not altered (Fig. 1h,i and Extended Data Fig. 2b–f). Total cardiac glycogen was unchanged (Extended Data Fig. 2g). The observed reduction in the oxidation of infused lactate in failing Mpc1iCKO hearts support the hypothesis that there is an MPC1-independent path for lactate oxidation that supports cardiac function.

To identity a potential alternative mitochondrial transporter of lactate, we conducted immunoblot analysis of purified mitochondria from myocardium and observed expression of the lactate transporter MCT1 (encoded by Slc16a1) in the mitochondrial fraction of healthy and failing Mpc1iCKO hearts (Fig. 1j and Extended Data Fig. 2h,i). MCT1 has previously been reported in mitochondria from cardiac tissue22,23. Analysis of published single-nuclei RNA-sequencing data from human hearts showed that SLC16A1 transcripts are predominantly expressed in cardiomyocytes24 (Extended Data Fig. 2j). Notably, MCT1 expression was increased in the mitochondria of young healthy Mpc1iCKO hearts but not failing hearts (Extended Data Fig. 2i,k,l). The existence of mitochondrial lactate import and metabolism has been proposed for decades, but its physiological relevance and mechanistic basis remain actively contested within the field25,26.

To test whether MCT1 can provide an alternative mechanism for carbohydrate carbon entry and oxidation in mitochondria, we repeated our [U-13C]glucose tracing in young, healthy Mpc1iCKO mice cotreated with AZD3965 (AZD), a potent MCT1 inhibitor with selectivity over MCT227. AZD treatment in control mice had no effect on the oxidation of glucose carbon as measured by TCA cycle metabolite labelling (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3a–e). However, when AZD was administered to Mpc1iCKO mice, glucose labelling of TCA cycle substrates was ablated. Although MCT1 may have a role in the transport of lactate across both the plasma membrane and IMM, our tracing data support an essential mitochondrial role, as glucose carbon uptake is not dependent on MCT1 activity. Overall, our data suggest functional redundancy between MCT1 and the MPC for the uptake of three-carbon equivalents into the mitochondria.

To further explore the role of MCT1 as a mitochondrial lactate transporter, we developed a tamoxifen-inducible, cardiac-specific Mct1 knockout mouse: Mct1fl/fl; αMHCMerCreMer+/− (hereafter Mct1iCKO). We validated knockout by PCR and MCT1 immunoblots from whole-heart and mitochondrial preparations (Fig. 2b,c and Extended Data Fig. 3f). Across genotypes, a larger fraction of MCT1 signal was associated with mitochondria than MCT2 or MCT4 (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 3f–h). When comparing Mpc1iCKO, Mct1iCKO and control hearts from young mice, we observed an increase in cytosolic and total MCT1, MCT4 and LHDB protein expression in Mpc1iCKO hearts, suggesting a compensatory lactate metabolism axis (Extended Data Fig. 3f,h,i). No significant changes in the expression of these proteins or MPC1 and MCT2 were observed in Mct1iCKO hearts (Extended Data Fig. 3f–j).

We incubated ACMs from Mct1fl/fl (control) and Mct1iCKO mice with [U-13C]lactate to test whether cytosolic uptake of lactate is lost without MCT1. The rate of cytosolic uptake of lactate and production of labelled pyruvate was unaffected by loss of MCT1, suggesting that the observed oxidative phenotypes were unlikely to be the result of changes in cytosolic import (Fig. 2d,e). At steady state, lactate contributed robustly to citrate (M+2) labelling in control ACMs, but this was sharply reduced in Mct1iCKO ACMs. The overall lactate labelling fraction was also reduced, but this was owing to a sharp increase in the total levels of lactate present (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). To further investigate whether changes in mitochondrial uptake and oxidation of lactate were dependent on MCT1 and independent of its known roles on the plasma membrane, we treated isolated ACMs with saponin to selectively permeabilize the plasma membrane28 (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). Although plasma membrane integrity was disrupted, mitochondria membrane potential was maintained and cytochrome c was not released (Extended Data Fig. 4d–g). Upon incubation with [U-13C]lactate, we observed robust oxidation in Mct1fl/fl ACMs, as evidenced by TCA cycle labelling that was attenuated in Mct1iCKO cells and upon treatment with AZD3965 (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Fig. 4h,i).

We infused awake Mct1iCKO mice with [U-13C]glucose or [U-13C]lactate to determine whether MCT1 was necessary for the oxidation of lactate in vivo. Serum glucose and lactate metabolic parameters were not different by genotype (Extended Data Fig. 5a–d). In Mct1iCKO hearts, the entry of lactate carbon into TCA metabolites was significantly reduced, whereas labelling from glucose was unchanged (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 5a,b,e–h). Of note, loss of MCT1 did not alter tissue lactate or pyruvate enrichment, indicating that the observed TCA cycle defects were not due to a loss of plasma membrane lactate transport. Oxidation of 3-hydroxybutyrate was also not affected (Extended Data Fig. 5i). To isolate the effects of MCT1 on lactate oxidation alone, we used an ex vivo perfused heart model (Langendorff) with a non-recirculating solution to prevent isotopic exchange between substrates. We cannulated hearts and perfused them with a working buffer containing the major cardiac energy substrates (glucose, lactate, pyruvate and bovine serum albumin (BSA)-conjugated fatty acids)11 (Fig. 2i). Final heart rate was unchanged between Mct1iCKO and control mice (Fig. 2j and Extended Data Fig. 5j). We traced lactate utilization by replacing the unlabelled lactate with [U-13C]lactate (Fig. 2k). Loss of MCT1 prevented nearly all lactate oxidation, but had no effect on lactate enrichment within the tissue. In a second perfusion experiment, we observed that oxidation of [U-13C]glucose was increased in Mct1iCKO hearts, presumably compensating for the loss of lactate oxidation (Fig. 2l).

Paralleling our observations in mouse samples, we detected MCT1 in purified mitochondrial preparations obtained from myocardium of human donors and patients with heart failure (Fig. 3a). To confirm the identity of MCT1 within human mitochondria, we immunoprecipitated MCT1 from purified cardiac mitochondria followed by proteomic analysis. MCT1 peptides were identified together with a high representation of mitochondrial proteins, but not plasma membrane markers, supporting the existence of a mitochondrial MCT1 fraction (Extended Data Fig. 6a–d and Supplementary Table 3). To provide additional evidence of MCT1 and LDHB localization within the mitochondria, we performed proteinase K protection assays using purified mouse and human cardiac mitochondria. In isotonic buffer, intact membranes protect the IMM, intermembrane space and matrix proteins from proteinase K, whereas outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) proteins are digested. Hypotonic buffer disrupts the OMM, exposing OMM, intermembrane space and some IMM proteins. Detergent (Triton X-100) solubilizes all membranes and denatures protein, rendering all mitochondrial proteins sensitive to proteinase K. Mitochondria from mouse myocardium or isolated cardiomyocytes subjected to these conditions displayed an MCT1 protection pattern resembling that of an IMM protein (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). Across preparations, MCT1 was relatively resistant to proteinase K in isotonic buffer, unlike OMM proteins such as TOM20. In these preparations, we also observed a protected LDHB band that showed similar digestion patterns to citrate synthase, a matrix protein. Similar protection patterns were observed in mitochondria from a human cell line, failing human hearts and non-failing human donor hearts (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 6g,h). Samples from human hearts showed higher variability and more overall degradation due to sample handling differences including freeze–thaw cycles.

We next tested whether MCT1 was required for the mitochondrial uptake of lactate using [2-2H]lactate tracing. During LDH-mediated oxidation, the deuterium at C2 in [2-2H]lactate is transferred to NAD+, generating NAD2H (Fig. 3d). The presence of 2H-lactate in the mitochondria signifies that lactate entered the organelle before conversion to pyruvate29. However, because mitochondrial isolation from a mouse heart is slow compared with global LDH activity, this approach is unsuitable for in vivo studies. To overcome this limitation, we again employed our Langendorff ex vivo heart system in which we perfused [2-2H]lactate into Mct1iCKO hearts from mice also expressing a cardiomyocyte-specific mitochondria-localized affinity tag (MITO-Tag)30 (Extended Data Fig. 7a). We developed a protocol for the rapid isolation of cardiac mitochondria and quenching of lactate metabolism using inhibitor cocktails (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 7b). In perfused hearts, heart rate and whole-heart 2H-lactate labelling were similar across genotypes (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 7c). However, in the mitochondrial fraction, 2H-lactate was absent from Mct1iCKO samples (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 7d).

Proteinase K and immunoblot assays identified LDHB within the mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 3b,c). We quantified total matrix LDH activity by performing in vitro LDH assays on purified cardiac mitochondria with and without permeabilization and proteinase K treatment (Fig. 3g). Alamethicin treatment permeabilizes both mitochondrial membranes to small molecule transit (for example, NAD+ and ATP) while retaining normal matrix protein localization31. Cardiac LDH activity was both highly dependent on permeabilization and insensitive to proteinase K treatment, consistent with matrix localization (Fig. 3g,h and Extended Data Fig. 7e). Liver and kidney mitochondrial LDH activity showed different sensitivities to permeabilization and proteinase K treatment.

After identifying MCT1 in the IMM and LDH activity in mitochondrial preparations, we purified mitochondria from Mct1fl/fl (control), Mct1iCKO and Mpc1iCKO hearts and characterized their respiration and metabolism on different substrates. Mitochondria from control and Mct1iCKO hearts respired equivalently on pyruvate, but Mpc1iCKO mitochondria had reduced oxygen consumption rates (JO2), impaired 13C-citrate production and suppressed pyruvate uptake (Fig. 3i,j and Extended Data Fig. 7f). Conversely, respiration on lactate was similar in both control and Mpc1iCKO mitochondria but was reduced in mitochondria from Mct1iCKO hearts (Fig. 3k). [U-13C]lactate import and oxidation into TCA cycle metabolites was attenuated in Mct1iCKO mitochondria but unchanged in mitochondria isolated from the hearts of mice lacking the lactate exporter MCT4 (encoded by Slc16a3)16 (Fig. 3l and Extended Data Fig. 7g–k). Mitochondria from Mct1iCKO mice showed no gross defect in mitochondrial electron transport chain complex or supercomplex assembly (Extended Data Fig. 7l). Acute treatment of mitochondria isolated from control mice with an MCT1 inhibitor (7ACC2) or a pan-LDH inhibitor (GSK 2837808A) inhibited respiration on lactate (Fig. 3m). Finally, we treated purified mitochondria from non-failing donor hearts with [U-13C]lactate and observed impaired import and oxidation to pyruvate and citrate when treated with an MCT1 inhibitor but not with an MPC inhibitor (Fig. 3n).

The oxidation of one lactate molecule within the mitochondria by LDH to produce pyruvate generates one equivalent of NADH that can transfer electrons directly into the electron transport chain. To test whether this LDH activity could generate sufficient NADH for respiration, we inhibited the TCA cycle with the pyruvate- and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase inhibitor CPI-61332. As expected, mitochondrial respiration on pyruvate was sharply reduced by CPI-613 treatment, however lactate respiration was unaltered despite M+2 citrate production from 13C-lactate being suppressed (Extended Data Fig. 7m–o). NADH levels were unchanged (Extended Data Fig. 7p). Total oxygen consumption on lactate was consistently lower than on pyruvate under minimal assay conditions (buffer, mitochondria, substrate, malate and ADP). Because lactate is converted to pyruvate by LDH, equivalent respiration would be expected if oxidation proceeded identically, suggesting that lactate oxidation may be incomplete. We hypothesized that in the absence of exogenous pyruvate, lactate oxidation may be impaired through pyruvate efflux via the MPC. Pre-treatment of ACMs with UK5099, a potent MPC inhibitor, enhanced lactate-supported respiration (Extended Data Fig. 7q,r). Collectively, these data support a model in which mitochondrial LDH-dependent lactate metabolism supports respiratory flux.

Having established that MCT1 is required for lactate oxidation, we explored whether loss of this biochemical functionality affects cardiac physiology. Mct1iCKO mice did not show evidence of structural or functional cardiac abnormalities by 1 year of age as measured by echocardiography (Extended Data Fig. 8a–i). Consistent with this, Mct1iCKO mice exhibited unchanged maximal exercise capacity over time (Extended Data Fig. 8j,k). We hypothesized that the absence of a phenotype was a consequence of the functional redundancy between MCT1 and MPC in cardiomyocytes. Therefore, we turned to a neurohormonal agonist-induced model of cardiac hypertrophy that we previously reported to decrease Mpc1 activity16. We surgically implanted mice with osmotic minipumps delivering angiotensin II and phenylephrine (ATII/PE) to induce cardiac hypertrophy (Fig. 4a). After 6 weeks, all treated mice developed cardiac hypertrophy, which was more pronounced in Mct1iCKO mice (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9a). Body weight, heart rate, left ventricular end systolic diameter and left ventricular end diastolic diameter were not significantly different between genotypes and were only modestly affected by treatment (Extended Data Fig. 9b–e). By contrast, systolic function as measured by stroke volume was reduced in Mct1iCKO mice, leading to lower cardiac output, fractional shortening and impaired LVEF (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 9f–i). Consequently, ATII/PE-treated Mct1iCKO mice exhibited increased mortality (Fig. 4d). Similarly, loss of MCT1 accelerated the development of heart failure in mice when cardiac hypertrophy was induced by pressure overload caused by transverse aortic constriction (TAC) (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 10a–k).

To understand the accelerated development of heart failure in Mct1iCKO mice, we performed targeted gene expression analysis on ATII/PE and TAC-treated hearts. Neither loss of Mct1 nor ATII/PE treatment led to a reduction in Mpc1 gene or protein expression in myocardium extracts (Fig. 4f,g and Extended Data Fig. 9j). However, in purified adult cardiomyocytes treated with ATII/PE we observed a reduction in Mpc1 mRNA levels (Extended Data Fig. 9k). By contrast, TAC treatment led to a reduction in myocardial Mpc1 transcript levels and a trend towards reduced protein as well (Extended Data Fig. 10l,o,p). Slc16a1 and Slc16a3 transcripts were increased in control mice (Extended Data Fig. 10m,n). These data suggest that in some models of heart failure, MPC1 is suppressed and MCT1 may compensate.

To reconcile the severity of our cardiac phenotype across models with conflicting gene expression data, we performed metabolomic analysis on ATII/PE-treated myocardium. There were significant genotype-specific differences in metabolite abundances between Mct1iCKO and Mct1fl/fl hearts upon ATII/PE treatment (Fig. 4h,i and Extended Data Fig. 11a,b and Supplementary Table 4). Saline-treated Mct1iCKO hearts had increased levels of lactate, histamine, carnosine and glycine and reduced levels of citrate and isocitrate compared with Mct1fl/fl hearts. Upon ATII/PE treatment, metabolite changes in Mct1iCKO hearts were of greater magnitude and NADH was the most upregulated metabolite, indicative of an impairment in oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 4i). Consistent with a critical mitochondrial defect, all purine nucleotides were downregulated, with ADP being the most statistically significantly reduced metabolite in failing Mct1iCKO hearts (Fig. 4i). Metabolome differences were much less pronounced upon ATII/PE treatment in control hearts (Extended Data Fig. 11b).

We next performed [U-13C]lactate infusions on mice treated with ATII/PE to understand whether the observed steady-state metabolic changes between Mct1iCKO hearts before and after ATII/PE treatment reflected changes in cardiac substrate metabolism. Mice were treated with ATII/PE for 1 week prior to anaesthetized tail vein infusions to reduce procedure-induced mortality. ATII/PE treatment led to a rapid increase in the ratio of heart weight to body weight in Mct1iCKO mice (Extended Data Fig. 11c). Lactate turnover was systemically lower under anaesthesia in all conditions compared with awake mice and was further reduced in Mct1fl/fl mice subjected to ATII/PE (Extended Data Fig. 11d). Treatment did not affect the contribution of lactate to TCA labelling in Mct1fl/fl mice (Fig. 4j and Extended Data Fig. 11e). By contrast, the contribution of lactate to the TCA cycle (malate, succinate and glutamate) was attenuated in hearts from Mct1iCKO-treated mice with ATII/PE compared with saline-treated controls and Mct1fl/fl ATII/PE mice (Fig. 4j and Extended Data Fig. 11e).

We further investigated the nature of the impaired mitochondrial function in Mct1iCKO mice using electron microscopy (Fig. 4k and Extended Data Fig. 11f). Total mitochondria area was not different between saline conditions and increased upon ATII/PE treatment in the Mct1iCKO group (Extended Data Fig. 11g). Relatedly, the overall mitochondrial morphology differed significantly between saline and ATII/PE samples (Extended Data Fig. 11h–k). Cristae volume density was reduced in Mct1iCKO compared with Mct1fl/fl mitochondria and this difference was more pronounced under ATII/PE treatment (Fig. 4l). The high degree of circularity and reduced cristae volume density of mitochondria has been shown to correlate with oxidative stress33,34 and may indicate a role for MCT1 in cristae maintenance and contribute to the global impairment in oxidation seen in Mct1iCKO with ATII/PE treatment.

Increased MCT1 expression has been reported in myocardium during ischemia–reperfusion injury35,36. To study the consequences of MCT1 loss during ischemia–reperfusion injury and to explore the connection between MCT1 and susceptibility to mitochondrial oxidative stress, we studied Mct1iCKO mice subjected to ischemia–reperfusion37 (Extended Data Fig. 12a). Mct1iCKO mice had myocardial areas at risk for infarction of similar size, but larger necrotic areas and a trend towards higher mortality compared with control littermates (Fig. 4m and Extended Data Fig. 12b–d). To understand whether these effects were correlated with intrinsic defects in Mct1iCKO cardiomyocyte function upon hypoxia we utilized an in vitro hypoxia–reoxygenation setup that models ischemia–reperfusion injury37 (Extended Data Fig. 12e). At baseline, Mct1iCKO ACMs had increased mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨ), higher calcium (Ca2+) levels, and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) that were further increased after hypoxia–reoxygenation (Fig. 4n and Extended Data Fig. 12f–h). The MCT1-dependent changes in ROS are congruent with our mitochondrial ultrastructure data and suggest a role for MCT1 in the structure and function of mitochondria which become pathogenic upon cellular damage and energetic stress.

Collectively, our data indicate a key role for cardiac MCT1 in metabolic adaptation to models of both acute and chronic cardiac stress. To assess whether MCT1 was important for recovery from injury and cardiac remodelling, we allowed Mct1iCKO mice subjected to ischemia–reperfusion injury to recover (Extended Data Fig. 12i). In Mct1fl/fl and Mct1iCKO mice, LVEF decreased by a similar amount immediately after the procedure. However, in Mct1fl/fl mice systolic function returned to baseline by 7 weeks whereas Mct1iCKO mice showed no recovery of heart function over the duration of the study (Fig. 4o and Extended Data Fig. 12j–m). In the failing hearts of Mct1iCKO mice, expression of Lhda and Ldhb transcripts was significantly increased, and Mpc1 expression was decreased, indicating glucose and lactate metabolic pathway stress that was independent of Ppargc1a (encoding PGC1α; expression was unchanged; Extended Data Fig. 12n). Finally, analysis of mitochondria from patients with advanced heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and non-failing donor controls revealed increased mitochondrial MCT1 localization without a statistically significant change in total MCT1 or MPC protein levels (Fig. 4p and Extended Data Fig. 12o–s). Among all patients in our cohort, myocardial SLC16A1 transcript levels were reduced in heart failure, however the difference was not statistically significant (Extended Data Fig. 12t).

Our findings demonstrate that in the myocardium, glucose-derived carbons can enter the TCA cycle independently of the MPC through a pathway that is dependent on MCT1 (Fig. 2a). Using complementary approaches, we show that MCT1 resides in the IMM and its loss impairs lactate oxidation at the mitochondrial, cellular and tissue level. Loss of MCT1 compromises metabolic adaptation to cardiac stress, leading to structural and functional deterioration and ultimately heart failure. These results support a model in which mitochondrial lactate import enables glucose and lactate carbons to fuel the TCA cycle independent of the MPC while preserving cytosolic redox balance and sustaining glycolysis (Fig. 4q). Our model reconciles the long-standing observation of high systemic lactate fluxes in mammalian physiology6,7,38, mitochondrial lactate import independent of the MPC in purified systems39 and direct evidence from cellular lactate biosensors showing substantial lactate accumulation within mitochondria40,41.

Our data extend and refine the ‘lactate shuttle’ hypothesis developed by physiologists29,42,43,44,45,46. The rapid uptake and oxidation of lactate is a hallmark of exercise metabolism47,48,49. However, cytosolic lactate oxidation is inherently constrained by competition with glycolysis for NAD+, requiring the invocation of compartmentalized metabolism at the cellular or subcellular level8,25. Locating LDHB-driven lactate oxidation within the mitochondrial matrix resolves this conflict. The lower Michaelis constant (Km) of LDHB for pyruvate compared with LDHA50 favours forward lactate oxidation under non-equilibrium cardiac conditions, enhancing metabolic flexibility. In the mitochondria, lactate can shuttle an additional pair of cytosolic electrons into NADH for transfer into the mitochondrial quinone pool to fuel complex I activity. ATP production from lactate depends upon oxygen to serve as the terminal electron acceptor and drive the equilibrium of the reaction towards carbon oxidation. The lactate shuttle hypothesis, however, has been vigorously contested on the basis of conflicting data from isolated mitochondrial systems41 and on biochemical grounds25,26. Although our in vivo data cannot rigorously separate the dual roles of MCT1 in mitochondrial and cytoplasmic lactate transport, our work based on functional and tracing data from purified mitochondria, permeabilized cells, isolated organs and mice under physiological conditions establishes MCT1 as necessary for mitochondrial lactate oxidation in heart. We note that direct comparisons of in vivo cardiac metabolism between healthy and failing hearts are affected by systemic metabolic perturbations from anaesthesia.

Mitochondrial lactate transport provides crucial redundancy for carbohydrate entry into the mitochondria, enabling cardiomyocytes to maintain glucose oxidation even in the absence of the MPC17,18. We show that this redundancy is critical to support the heart’s need for a rapid and flexible energy supply in the face of cardiac stress and position mitochondrial lactate metabolism as a central determinant of cardiac structure, function and resilience.

Mass spectrometric data were collected on Orbitrap Fusion Lumos instruments coupled to a Proxeon NanoLC-1200 UHPLC. The 100 µm capillary column was packed with 35 cm of Accucore 150 resin (2.6 μm, 150 Å; ThermoFisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 360 nl min−1. The scan sequence began with an MS1 spectrum (Orbitrap analysis, resolution 60,000, 350–1,350 Th, automatic gain control (AGC) target 100%, maximum injection time of 118 ms). Data were acquired for 60 min per sample. The hrMS2 stage consisted of fragmentation by higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD, normalized collision energy 36%) and analysis using the Orbitrap (AGC 2 × 105, maximum injection time 60 ms, isolation window 1.2 Th, resolution 7.5 K). Data were acquired using the FAIMSpro interface the dispersion voltage set to 5,000 V, the compensation voltages were set at −40V, −60V, and −80V, and the TopSpeed parameter was set at 1 s per compensation voltage. Mass spectra were processed using a Comet-based in-house software pipeline. Mass spectra were converted to mzXML using a modified version of ReAdW.exe. Database searching included all entries from the human UniProt database (downloaded November 2021), which was concatenated with a reverse database composed of all protein sequences in reversed order. The digest was set to semi-tryptic. Searches were performed using a 50 ppm precursor ion tolerance and the product ion tolerance was set to 0.02 Th. Oxidation of methionine residues (+15.9949 Da) was set as a variable modification. PSM filtering was performed using a linear discriminant analysis, as described previously64, while considering the following parameters: XCorr (cross-correlation score), ΔCn (delta correction score), missed cleavages, peptide length, charge state, and precursor mass accuracy. Peptide-spectral matches were identified, quantified, and collapsed to a 1% false discovery rate and then further collapsed to a final protein-level false discovery rate of 1%.

Imaging was performed as described previously65. In brief, isolated cardiomyocytes were suspended in HPLM-FA medium and placed in an airtight chamber at 37 °C. The chamber was then sealed, and the cells left for 2 h at 37 °C. After 2 h, the cells were removed from the chamber and placed under normal cell culture conditions (37 °C, room air supplemented with 5% CO2) for an additional 2 h to reoxygenate and model reperfusion. Control cells were suspended in supplemented HPLM medium and kept at 37 °C, in room air with 5% CO2 for 4 h. For imaging, the cells were loaded with either 20 nM of TMRM (Thermo Fisher), 5 μM MitoSox (Thermo Fisher) or 5 μM X-Rhod1 (Thermo Fisher) to measure mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondrial ROS levels and mitochondrial Ca2+ levels, respectively. The cells were then placed onto glass-bottomed dishes (MatTek) and imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope. All three dyes were measured with an excitation and emission at 548 nm and 574 nm. All cells were imaged at the same power and gain settings and images were collected within 30 min after removal from incubator following simulated reperfusion. After euthanasia, the mouse coronary artery was retrogradely perfused through the aorta with 20 ml of ice-cold fixative (formaldehyde/glutaraldehyde 2.5% in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer). All fixatives and chemicals used for transmission electron microscopy are from Electron Microscopy Sciences. After perfusion, left ventricles were sliced into 1 mm3 tissue sections, stored at 4 °C in fixative, and sent to the Electron Microscopy Laboratory at University of Utah for further processing. Mitochondrial morphology and cristae content were analysed from transmission electron microscopy images using ImageJ software. The outer boundary of each mitochondrion, along with the contours of individual cristae within each mitochondrion, were manually traced using the freehand selection tool. Morphological parameters including mitochondrial area, circularity, roundness and the ratio of height/width were quantified to characterize mitochondrial shape. The surface area of each crista was measured, and the total cristae surface area per mitochondrion was calculated as the sum of the individual cristae areas. Cristae volume density was estimated by dividing the total cristae surface area by the corresponding mitochondrial area. Matrix volume density was subsequently derived by subtracting the cristae volume density from 100%.

We used GraphPad Prism software (v10.3.1) for all statistical analysis. To determine if our data were normally distributed, we used the Shapiro–Wilk and D’Agostino–Pearson omnibus tests. For in vivo experiments, we utilized Grubb’s method (extreme Studentized deviate (ESD) to detect one outlier and robust outlier detection (ROUT) to detect multiple outliers) to expose any significant outliers (P < 0.05) in our data. If outliers were detected, they were removed subsequent statistical analysis. For all our data we present it as the mean ± s.e.m. and applied two-tailed tests for all comparisons. When analysing two discrete groups (such as Mct1fl/fl versus Mct1iCKO), we used an unpaired Student’s t-test for normally distributed data, if the data were non-parametric, we used the Mann–Whitney U test. Multiple comparisons correction for unpaired t-tests was performed using the Holm–Sídák method for adjusted P values. For datasets with more than two variables one and two-way ANOVA was used with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. For evaluating serial echocardiographic data and multiple group comparisons, we used a repeated measures ANOVA (mixed-effects model) with a Geisser–Greenhouse correction, followed by a Tukey’s multiple comparison test for individual comparisons. We set an α level of P < 0.05 a priori and any value below this setpoint was considered statistically significant.
