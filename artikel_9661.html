How Surplus Is Created in a Zero-Sum Universe

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

The same lines of code run on a billion phones. A twenty-six-letter alphabet can generate every sentence anyone will ever write. An explanation outlives its author and reaches stars he never saw. Knowledge compounds, finite means generate infinite ends. David Deutsch, Naval Ravikant and Brett Hall will tell you the future is open, with a straight face, and they’re right.

“All markets are zero sum. Because the universe is zero sum. There is a fixed amount of energy in the universe. Matter cannot be created nor destroyed. All so-called ‘positive sum’ games are just zero sum ones where the loser doesn’t matter.”

Both sides are true. The dissonance is not a paradox to be lived with. It is the exact condition under which surplus can be created.

This essay examines and resolves the dissonance.

The resolution determines outcomes in people, markets, products, habits and systems.

You already know some of this by instinct because it is too ubiquitous. This essay compresses it into a rule you can state — hand to someone else and apply in a domain you’ve never worked in.

Let’s start with what the optimists miss.

Bugs getting genocided by pesticides don’t matter. Bacteria getting genocided by antibiotics don’t matter. Mountains getting blown into bits and turned into highways don’t matter. Iron getting ripped from its home and melted into steel doesn’t matter. The dirt that would have been heated by the sun that is now freezing because you placed a solar panel above it doesn’t matter. Because these things aren’t sentient beings who “deserve rights” or whatever. We see no “cost” because we are not impacted by it and it doesn’t trigger our empathy response.

But there is a cost, just not to “us”. Newton’s Third Law: “every action has an equal and opposite reaction”. You cannot delete the reaction. You can only try to make that reaction occur as far away and to the least “important” possible matter as you can.

This is what economic externalities are. Trying to push that “cost” off to someone or something else. It’s not a problem when you build solar panels and steal photons from the dirt that wasn’t “using” it anyway. But the whole bug genocide thing is not so clear cut. We thought the costs went elsewhere but they did not (the bee crisis and endocrine disruption just two well-known ways in which this “costless” benefit is costing us). Same with the bacteria. And obviously there are the popular ones like CO2, pollution, or plastic filling the oceans (which are also endocrine disrupting), and plenty of others.

“Market failures” occur when the “reaction” isn’t far away enough; when the external costs aren’t external enough. Far enough to not be impacting the producer, but not so far they don’t eventually come around and bite us in the ass later.

A cost can be moved. It cannot be deleted. Everything below follows from that, one step at a time.

1. Nothing is free. Every arrangement that exists right now is having its costs paid by something, right now.

2. An arrangement is a particular order of parts. Your body is molecules in an order. A company is people and capital in an order. A city is matter in an order.

3. For any arrangement, the ways it could come apart vastly outnumber the one way it holds together. Leave coffee on a table and its heat spreads through the room, because spread-out states outnumber the concentrated one by a factor too large to write down. That count is Boltzmann’s entropy (1877).

4. So every arrangement drifts toward disorder unless something maintains it. Houses fall down. Bodies come apart. The maintenance costs energy, and the moment you stop paying, the count wins. Staying here isn’t a state. It’s a rate.

Your savings, your fitness, your reputation, your customer base, a friendship: every one of them is a rate. Stop paying and the count wins, quietly, long before anything visibly breaks.

5. The energy comes from a supply that isn’t growing. Every draw is one another arrangement can’t make. Add a new draw and every neighbour’s ceiling drops. Nobody gets to sit the contest out.

6. That leaves one condition: take in at least what you leak, at every moment, not on average. Meet it and you hold. Miss it and you shrink toward zero. Exceed it and you can allocate towards growth.

7. Everything you can see met the condition. There is no moment where a thing exists and then, separately, survives. To be here is to have passed, continuously, up to now.

8. So in a zero-sum system, survival is the only outcome that persists.

The arrangement isn’t the stuff. It’s the order the stuff is in.

The stuff comes and goes. Most of the matter in your body was something else a few years ago. Your company isn’t staffed by the people it had five years back, and your city isn’t the same buildings. What you point at when you say “that’s still the same thing” is the order.

Which means the order can ride on different substrate. A recipe survives the meal that used it up. A song survives the performance that ended. A language survives every speaker who dies.

But a pattern doesn’t float. It exists only in matter, so to continue past its current instance it has to be realised again, in new matter, before the old matter gives out. That realisation is a copy.

In a zero-sum world, copying is the only move that doesn’t forfeit survival. Every other strategy ends when the instance does.

About 330 billion of your cells die and are replaced every day (Sender and Milo, 2021).

Franchises, precedents, birthing children, and printing presses are the same move at a larger scale. Whatever is still here is here because something copied it before the last carrier wore out. It’s what persistence looks like when nothing persists.

If this sounds like Darwin, good. Darwin reached it from beetles and finches. This reaches it from the ledger, with no biology assumed, which is why it fits firms, languages and ideas as well as animals.

Variation. No copy is perfect. Fidelity is bounded by the medium: molecules misplace, memories drift, code has bugs. Each cell division copies a genome of about 6.4 billion letters with roughly one error per billion, so a few billion copies make errors certain. Variation isn’t something life adds. It’s what imperfect copying produces.

Selection. Variants that meet the rate persist. Variants that miss it vanish. Nobody needs to introduce a theory of selection. It is the filter from the last section, run on varied copies.

The cost wall. Copying costs matter and energy out of a pool that doesn’t grow. Every copy is a draw someone else can’t make. That seems to shut the door: if all anything can do is copy inside a fixed pool, nothing new can ever appear.

Yet you are reading this on something that did not exist in 1950. Where did it come from?

The universe has a conservation law for energy. It has one for matter. It has none for arrangement.

Silicon in a quarry and silicon in a chip are the same atoms under the same laws. One is rock. The other runs a hospital. The universe charged nothing for the difference.

That gap is free in the one sense the ledger tracks. It isn’t free to hold: the rate still applies to every chip and every company. Searching for a better order of the same parts costs no more matter than any other thought, and once it’s found, it copies at the price of the copy, not the price of the discovery.

Information is physical, so this isn’t a metaphor: erasing a single bit costs a minimum amount of heat (Landauer, 1961). A farming method found once fed ten thousand generations. A proof derived once spares everyone after it. Libraries exist because civilisations learned that copying is cheaper than rediscovering, and bet on that gap for five thousand years.

That is the whole difference between selling your hours and selling an arrangement with cheaper cost of replication. Hours are the Nth copy of a skill, so they compete down to the price of the copy. An arrangement is a discovery: it buys time before it is copied, and once built it copies at almost no cost.

Take the part of your work that is a discovery and turn it into something that can be copied, a tool, software, hardware, template, a system, a piece of writing.

An arrangement earns its keep when it does more than it costs to build and hold.

The wheel: stone shaped to move weight for a fraction of the effort of carrying it.

The antibiotic: a molecule that ends the bacterium and spares you.

That is surplus. It isn’t more stuff. It’s more things stuff can do.

So what is anything you make worth? Exactly the surplus it creates for whoever pays: the cost of their alternative minus the cost of yours, counted in hours, calories, materials, attention and risk.

A car beats walking by hours and calories. A spreadsheet beats hand calculation by days. That difference is the only thing there is to price.

Alex Hormozi’s value equation is this ledger with labels: the dream outcome is what the saved substrate buys, perceived likelihood is the chance the arrangement holds, time delay is the discount, and effort and sacrifice is the cost still sitting on the buyer.

The game never ends, because an arrangement that works is an intervention, not a “solution”. It changes the world, and the changed world has problems it didn’t have before. Agriculture fed cities. Cities needed coordination, coordination needed institutions, institutions needed record-keeping, record-keeping needed information technology, and information technology produced overload, which now needs better filters. No step is the last one. The hotel fills up and infinitely more guests arrive.

But finding is half the job. The other half is making sure what you found survives the copy.

The reason a book lights your brain up isn’t that the author pulled knowledge from the air. Learning is reconstruction, you are not downloading my knowledge into your brain. A good explanation takes the scattered bits you already carry and hands you one handle that regenerates all of them, so you can let the scattered bits go. If you have no scattered bits beforehand, you will fail to learn. Like a toddler looking at SpaceX blueprints.

You don’t need to memorise every species of beetle to understand biology. You need a finite rule that stands in for the infinite cases. That is what knowledge is: compression other people can load.

What separates a compression that survives from one that rots is whether it is hard to vary

Compression is lossy. A rule that covers many cases must be shorter than the cases it covers, and to be shorter it has to drop distinctions. Which distinctions are safe to drop? The ones that don’t change whether the rule works — and the only thing that tells you that is the causal structure underneath the rule.

Everything alive does whatever its constraints make cheapest.

A system is the same arithmetic inherited by everyone inside it, and a system can only see what it can count. Freelance platforms rank on price, rating and completed jobs. A newcomer has none, so price is the only variable left, and the work competes down to the lowest cost of living among the copies. The proxy can’t see the cost pushed outside it, so whoever pushes more out of sight wins more.

Evil, lazy, stupid or selfish. Each is a word for a cost structure you haven’t looked at.

Coordination has no payer. A set of rules that lets everyone move faster together costs money to design and enforce. The benefit spreads across everyone who uses it; the cost lands on who funds it. Unless people pay, nobody builds it, and the individual solution appears in its place.

The Lagos danfo bus only earns when it’s full. So the driver waits at the stop, so the buses bunch, so the road carries more vehicle-miles per passenger than a schedule would, and a trip that should take forty minutes takes an hour. Every driver is locally rational, but this causes collective disaster. The collective solution was never built, so the individual solution was forced. The difference lands on lungs, hours, health and so on.

That’s why Lagos is dirty and Singapore is clean. Singapore paid for the coordination and priced what the driver couldn’t see: a lane, a timetable, a fare that pays for the schedule instead of for being full. Nobody changed character. The prices changed and the behaviour followed.

This happens with market behaviours, academia, dating apps, tragedy of the commons, arms races, bad habits and a lot of other problems in your life. And it’s hard to escape because the only way out is through personal sacrifice and short term disadvantages for the collective long-term or larger-scale benefit, which the system punishes.

This gives you a test for anything you’re about to build or join. What does this make cheap, and what does it make expensive, for the people inside it? At ten thousand copies, where does the cost land, and who is counting it? Does it make the decent move the cheap move?

If the third answer is no, you’ve built an incentive, and people will follow it.

“If it were 2,000 years ago, we would be sacrificing ourselves to the sun gods to save us from El Niño.”

Imagine you are a member of a tribe from five thousand years ago. Bears keep coming to the camp. You sing, you dance, you leave meat at the tree line, you paint your faces and fast. Eventually the bears stop.

Now what? Remove one step and see whether they come back? One failed test and your family is eaten. So you keep everything. When deleting a necessary step is fatal and keeping an unnecessary one is cheap, keeping the whole bundle is the best move available to a mind that can’t afford the experiment. “Superstition” is what rationality looks like under that budget.

Whatever you can’t afford to test, you also can’t afford to look past — which means the reach of your concern is set by the slack you have left after survival, not by how good a person you are.

Two thousand years ago, nobody cared about endangered species or global warming. That wasn’t a shortage of virtue. Nobody could measure carbon, and nobody had surplus to spend on a species they would never meet.

The line moved for the same reason the ritual did.

The ancestor who couldn’t afford to test one step of the bear dance couldn’t afford to extend care past the fire either. Same budget.

Self comes first, because nobody can pay your rate before you do. Then kin, because kin carry copies of your pattern (kin selection, Hamilton, 1964). Then the tribe, because tribes that could count on each other beat tribes that couldn’t (reciprocity, Trivers, 1971). Each step outward sat where the cost of including someone was something the era could pay and the bill from excluding them was something the era could see.

Then surplus arrived: farming, writing, trade, law. Each raised what a society could afford to include, and each time the bill for leaving someone out became collectable: the stranger with a weapon, the plague that crossed the wall, the trading partner who could walk away.

Your boundary is who you make eat the cost, not what you say.

You live in the margin between what matter is and what it can do. That margin has no conservation law. Every arrangement that earns its keep is a deposit into it. Every discovery that gets copied is a withdrawal the next person doesn’t have to make. Every explanation that carries its why keeps a civilisation compounding on the same finite pile.

So build arrangements that earn their keep. Make them hard to vary. Hand them forward with their why. And before you celebrate, find out who paid.

The game has no finish line. Every arrangement that works creates problems that call for new ones. That isn’t a bug — it’s what it looks like when arrangements succeed. And the arrangements that stop working don’t disappear; they break down into raw material the next ones get built from. The castrated calf, the flint axe, the guild rule: the technique is forgotten and the material stays in play.

That’s where every surplus in history came from. It’s where yours will.
