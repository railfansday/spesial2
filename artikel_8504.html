Studies linking wealth to selfishness have a mixed record, so more money won’t necessarily change you. To keep it that way, give a set share of your income automatically, keep a regular volunteer shift, and keep seeing the friends you had before the money.

A widely covered 2012 study found drivers of higher-status cars more likely to cut off other cars at a four-way stop and to cut off a pedestrian waiting at a crosswalk. In lab tests, its upper-class participants were also more likely to lie in a negotiation and cheat to win a prize.

Later tests have had mixed results. When a study in Austria repeated the crosswalk test with 374 drivers, more than twice the original study’s 152, how expensive a car looked made no measurable difference to whether its driver stopped. And in a Dutch experiment published in 2021, wealthy households returned 81% of letters delivered to them by mistake, against 38% for households with little wealth.

Research on paying people for help is shaky too. In Heyman and Ariely’s 2004 experiments, people sometimes worked harder for nothing than for a small payment. The journal has since flagged the paper with an expression of concern, citing statistics that didn’t match a recalculation. Two repeats of one of its studies, run with 3,302 people in all and published in 2022, backed only part of that study’s findings.

Three habits help keep you generous whichever way the research settles.

Three signs mean a habit is slipping: putting a price on every favor, seeing less of old friends, and feeling exempt from rules everyone else follows.

Set up the recurring gift in the first month of your higher income, through the charity’s website or a paycheck deduction at work.
