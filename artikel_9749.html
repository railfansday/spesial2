Manuj Mittal on Why “Most Tokens Aren’t Securities” Leaves Crypto With More Questions Than Answers, and How Administration Policy Fills the Gap

The Senate blocked the Clarity Act in September 2026. Within three weeks the CFTC opened its own rulemaking. The result is a framework that answers one question and relocates the rest.

On September 15, 2026, the Senate voted 49 to 50 against taking up the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, eleven votes short of the 60 needed under the chamber's agreement. Senator Thom Tillis switched his vote at the end so he could move to reconsider, which keeps a second vote possible. The bill had passed the House 294 to 134 the year before. It would have written into statute which federal agency oversees which digital asset.

Twenty days later, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission published an advance notice that it intends to write its own rules for crypto trading platforms. The notice appeared in the Federal Register on October 13. Comments are due December 14. Nothing in it has legal force yet.

Manuj Mittal, who holds an MBA and a master's in finance from the University of Rochester's Simon Business School, trained as a chartered accountant in India, and worked in risk advisory and risk assurance at Grant Thornton before serving fund managers at Morgan Stanley, has a short description for that sequence.

"Congress failed to pass a law, so the agencies are issuing guidance without regulations," he said. "From a policymaker's seat, guidance is not the same as a statute with institutional support behind it. From the recipient's seat, whether that is an investor or a business, you discount it differently. You hold less of it."

The foundation is a document the Securities and Exchange Commission issued on March 17, 2026, with the CFTC signing on. It sorts crypto assets into five groups. Digital commodities, whose value comes from a working network and supply and demand. Digital collectibles. Digital tools, like tickets or credentials. Stablecoins issued under the 2025 GENIUS Act. And digital securities, which are ordinary securities recorded on a blockchain.

The first three are not themselves securities, in the Commission's view. The release names bitcoin, ether, Solana, XRP, and Dogecoin as digital commodities. That part got the headlines.

The part that matters more for anyone building or buying is the second half. Under the interpretation, a token that is not a security on its own can still be sold as part of an investment contract that is one. The test for that comes from the Supreme Court's 1946 decision in SEC v. Howey. If a developer sells tokens while promising to build the network that will give them value, that promise can be treated as the security, even if the token itself is not. The interpretation works alongside Howey rather than replacing it, since Howey remains the governing precedent.

"The focus shifted from 'what kind of asset is this' to 'how was it marketed to buyers,'" Mittal said. "Any auditor will tell you the second question is harder. Classifying crypto as an asset class has one answer. Judging how it was sold depends on the paper trail, and the paper trail is whatever the marketing team wrote three years ago."

"Regulators stopped asking 'what is this token' and started asking 'what promises were made when it was sold,'" Mittal said. "Any auditor will tell you that the second question is far tougher. Labeling crypto as an asset class has a single answer. Evaluating how it was sold comes down to the documentation, and that documentation is whatever the marketing team put out three years ago."

The release also says the investment contract can end once buyers could no longer reasonably expect the developer's promises to stay attached to the token. It calls these "non-exclusive indicia," which is lawyer's language for a judgment call made on the facts. The Commission says it may revise the interpretation after public comment.

Chairman Paul Atkins described the interpretation at SEC Speaks as a beginning rather than an end. Commissioner Caroline Crenshaw, the agency's lone Democrat, dissented from the 2025 staff statements it built on, arguing that only a fact-specific Howey analysis can settle any particular case.

The SEC followed in August with a proposed rule, Regulation Crypto Assets, that would let projects raise up to $5 million over four years under a startup exemption or up to $75 million a year under a larger tiered exemption. Comments close October 20. It is a proposal, not a rule.

Total crypto market capitalization was about $2.8 trillion on October 14, according to CoinGecko. Bitcoin traded near $82,000, roughly a third below its October 2025 record of $126,198.

Institutional holdings are substantial but not growing quickly. U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs held about $106 billion in net assets as of October 12, with cumulative net inflows of roughly $57 billion since their January 2024 launch, according to SoSoValue. That is about 6% of bitcoin's market value.

Flows were negative for most of 2026 and turned positive for the year only in late September, at about $320 million a year to date by Bloomberg's count. Stablecoin supply stood at $304 billion at the end of September, per DefiLlama, about $16 billion below a May peak.

Mittal reads those numbers as a verdict on process, not price. "Six percent in ETFs after almost three years is not an institution waiting for a bull market. It is an institution that has sized the position to what it can defend to a board if the rules change," he said. "Clarity from an agency that can be reversed by the next commission is worth something. It is not worth a full allocation."

He is mindful about what the data cannot show. "Too many other things move crypto, from rates to leverage. Anyone who tells you the March interpretation moved the price is guessing. What the flows do tell you is that nobody treated it as the all-clear."

CFTC Chairman Michael Selig, writing in The Wall Street Journal on October 5, argued that enforcement without rules pushed firms offshore and produced FTX, which misappropriated about $8 billion in customer money while operating through a Bahamas entity. Rules before the fact, he wrote, would have been better than prosecution after it.

Lawyers who advise the industry are more measured. Dan Ullman and Sarah Wastler of Orrick wrote in an October 2 client analysis that agency rules are more exposed to court challenge and reversal than legislation, and that binding CFTC rules may still be more than a year away. Agency action also cannot preempt the state money-transmitter regimes that govern most of what retail customers actually do.

A Cahill Gordon & Reindel team led by Lewis Rinaudo Cohen called the SEC's August proposal the first real pathway for public funding of blockchain projects but asked whether the whole framework survives judicial review after the Supreme Court's 2024 Loper Bright decision narrowed the deference courts give agencies.

"Loper Bright is the part the market has not priced in yet," Mittal said. "Before 2024, when an agency interpreted a law, courts usually deferred to it, so that interpretation was close to the final word. Now a court can decide for itself what the law means, which makes an agency interpretation the starting point of an argument rather than the end of one. For crypto, that means every one of these SEC and CFTC documents carries a risk of being challenged and overturned in court. Investors and businesses should account for that risk, and so far nobody has put a number on it."

Three dates matter. October 20, when SEC comments close on Regulation Crypto Assets. December 14, when CFTC comments close on its advance notice. And November 3, the midterm election, which decides whether the Clarity Act gets a second attempt in a lame-duck session or waits for a new Congress.

Asked what single development would tell him the U.S. environment had become predictable, Mittal did not pick a date. "A federal court adopting the March taxonomy in a contested case. Until a judge has agreed with it, the taxonomy is the SEC's opinion of its own work. The day a court says the same thing is the day a pension fund can put it in a memo. Institutions run on process. Their decision-making depends on knowing the compliance requirements, because those requirements are what identify the key indicators, and the key indicators are how you correctly evaluate the crypto market. A court ruling gives them that footing. Guidance alone does not."

The March interpretation told the market what a token is. The next three years will show whether that was the question anyone needed answered.
