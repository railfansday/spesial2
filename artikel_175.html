Milburn calls for tax cuts for businesses hiring young people despite union concerns

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Youth unemployment review risks clash with unions and Labour left over workers’ rights and minimum wage

Milburn urges Burnham to spend billions on young people

Alan Milburn has called on ministers to cut business taxes and ease regulations to encourage employers to hire more young people, setting up a potential clash with trade unions and the Labour left over employment rights.

The former health secretary published the results of his year-long review into youth unemployment on Wednesday, in what the prime minister, Andy Burnham, called a “watershed moment” for young people.

But while ministers embraced his central recommendation of cutting benefit and sickness payments to pay for more concerted employment support, his recommendations on workers’ rights, national insurance and the minimum wage pose potentially awkward dilemmas for the government.

In the 278-page report, Milburn called for ministers to cut national insurance for companies that hire young workers, to apply a “cautionary principle” when abolishing exploitative zero-hours contracts and to slow the rise in the youth rates of the minimum wage.

“A new bargain needs to be struck between employers and government: employers should create more first chances; government should make it easier and less risky for employers to do so,” he wrote. “That is the give and the get.”

Trade unions warned against some of his proposals, despite broadly welcoming Milburn’s push for a drastic expansion in state support to help the almost 1 million young people not in education or work.

Paul Nowak, the general secretary of the Trades Union Congress, urged the government to “crack on and deliver its guaranteed hours rights in full” in the face of lobbying from business groups.

“The past decade and a half has tested to destruction the idea that low-paid insecure work is good for our economy and young people’s employment prospects. It’s for the birds,” he said.

Burnham welcomed Milburn’s report on Tuesday, telling cabinet colleagues it represented a “comprehensive vision” for how to change the welfare, health and education systems for young people to help them into work.

He then joined Pat McFadden in the Commons, where the work and pensions secretary told MPs the government would deliver “a new settlement for young people”.

Under Milburn’s plan, the government would spend nearly £2bn a year on a youth opportunity service, which would provide support and advice to give every young person a guaranteed route into education, training or work.

It would involve spending just over £2bn to create more vocational training courses and apprenticeships, and a further £1bn in direct payments and tax breaks for companies that hire young people.

The new system would be paid for largely by replacing young people’s disability and sickness benefits with a single youth allowance, which would be considerably less than some people can currently claim.

People aged 18-24 would no longer be able to claim personal independence payments, which would be replaced by a new allowance available only to those whose conditions are “well established, significant and ongoing”.

The benefit cuts would be expected to save £3.6bn by 2036-37, with an additional £2.4bn in higher tax receipts. That money would be almost entirely swallowed by the £5.8bn cost of the new system, Milburn said.

Ministers and Milburn have worked hard to avoid the response that greeted Keir Starmer’s attempts last year to cut the benefits bill by £5bn by restricting access to disability payments.

The former prime minister backed down in the face of opposition from his own MPs, in a humiliating retreat that arguably set in motion his eventual departure from office.

Early signs suggest the outreach efforts have been successful, with several of the architects of last year’s rebellion speaking in favour of the proposals.

Debbie Abrahams, the chair of the work and pensions select committee, called the review “an opportunity of a generation”, adding: “We must change the status quo.”

But while Burnham and McFadden expressed support for the idea of shifting money from benefits to employment support schemes, they did not explicitly back Milburn’s recommendations on either national insurance contributions or zero-hours contracts.

McFadden told the Commons on Tuesday: “We know that employment costs matter and we will consider all these things as we move forward.”

On the minimum wage, Milburn urged ministers to continue applying “caution” when judging how quickly to increase the youth rate, after Labour promised in 2024 to raise it to the same level as the adult rate.

And on banning zero-hours contracts, he warned: “There is a risk that in trying to address a legitimate challenge, the solution has unintended consequences. I believe this is likely to be the case.”

Business leaders welcomed the recommendations, in particular the call to backtrack on Labour’s increase in employer national insurance contributions with a tax cut for under-25s, starting in retail and hospitality.

Maxine Bligh, the interim chief executive of the Recruitment and Employment Confederation, said: “Tackling the Neet [not in education, employment or training] crisis gives ministers the perfect opportunity to reconsider a guaranteed hours gamble that risks costing jobs and damaging the chances of young people accessing the labour market.”

Industry chiefs have intensified their lobbying against the government’s Employment Rights Act, with bosses finding a warm reception from Kemi Badenoch’s Conservatives.

Andrew Griffith, the shadow chancellor, said Labour was pressing ahead with an “unemployment act” that would hit youth jobs. “You cannot solve a youth jobs emergency without jobs, and this government has spent two years destroying them.”

Government analysis published this year showed estimated costs to employers could range from £350m to £2.9bn a year, with a central estimate of £1.1bn.

But unions and charities urged Burnham not to water down the plans, arguing that low-paid and insecure work had contributed significantly to Britain’s youth jobs crisis.

Joanne Thomas, the general secretary of Usdaw, the shopworkers’ union, said “crucial policies” on workers’ rights needed to be retained. “We are clear that the government must ensure that employment is fairly paid and secure, with workers being treated with dignity and respect.”

Banning zero-hours contracts was a promise at the heart of Labour’s election manifesto. Under Starmer, however, elements of the party’s plan to “make work pay” were watered down amid opposition from business.

Clare McNeil, the chief executive of the charity Timewise, said: “Where we disagree with Alan Milburn is in any suggestion that the new responsibilities in the Employment Rights Bill will stop employers hiring.”

Allies of the housing secretary, Angela Rayner, who had championed the government’s zero-hours plan as Starmer’s deputy, insisted that her proposals would not discourage employers from hiring young people. However, she is no longer in charge of the policy, which must now be implemented by the business secretary, Jonathan Reynolds.
