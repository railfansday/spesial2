Black Monday 1987: 5 forces behind the stock market's worst day

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The Dow fell 22.6% on Oct. 19, 1987, with no major news to blame. Five linked forces explain how the selling snowballed.

The stock market's worst day arrived without a headline anyone could point to as the reason. The losses on Oct. 19, 1987, were not limited to a few famous companies. The broad S&P 500 fell about 20 percent in that single session, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Losses that wide and that fast, across nearly every part of the market, raise the question behind everything that follows: If no single piece of news pushed investors to sell, what did?

The answer is a set of conditions that fed one another, not one shock. Investors had been selling well before that Monday, and the S&P 500 lost over nine percent in the five sessions through Oct. 16, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Nothing of note happened in the two days before Monday's open to explain a fresh wave of selling. When selling resumed Monday morning, investors were continuing a retreat that was already underway rather than responding to anything new. The tools big investors relied on for protection against a falling market also operated outside regulation. The Federal Reserve Board reports that portfolio insurers did most of their trading in index futures. Those futures, the arbitrage strategies built around them and portfolio insurance were all new products that no rules covered, according to 2012 comments by Charles Geisst, a professor of finance at Manhattan College, reported by TIME. In a market that was already falling and tied to unregulated tools, ordinary selling could grow into something much larger. The slides that follow show how that happened.

Investopedia set out to explain what triggered the October 1987 market collapse. It looked at causes that ranged from the automated hedging known as portfolio insurance, to the expiration of several kinds of contracts on the same Friday, to plain fear. The Dow Jones Industrial Average lost 22.6% that Monday, a fall too large to pin on any single cause. These causes are best understood as connected parts of one event rather than as rivals for the blame, because each made the others more damaging. The turning points are worth watching for. Investors in an expensive market ran into trading programs that sold automatically whenever prices dropped, and then people joined that selling out of fear.

By the autumn of 1987, investors had grown used to rising stock prices, and that habit made the market dangerous. Stock prices had tripled since 1982, according to Investopedia, which means buyers were paying three times what the same shares had cost only a few years before. That was a long run-up rather than one hot year, so the gains had built a high base with a long way down. A bull market that had run that far for that long was due for a correction, and a major pullback was likely sooner or later. A pullback that is likely someday is not the same as a crash on a particular Monday, though, and the rest of the story begins in that gap.

Fear about what policymakers would do next turned that general fragility into an autumn sell-off. Investors who remembered punishing borrowing costs were quick to react to any hint that those costs might return. "The market had experienced very high interest rates between 1980 and 84, and was spooked by talk of a return to those rates," Charles Geisst, a professor of finance at Manhattan College, told TIME in 2012. The rate talk came during a change at the top of the Federal Reserve, where Alan Greenspan had become chairman only in August 1987. Investors had no record by which to judge how he would act, and that made rumors of higher rates harder to dismiss. The House Ways and Means Committee added a second worry with legislation that would end tax breaks on merger debt, which threatened the borrowing behind much of the era's deal-making. High prices explain why the market was vulnerable, and these policy fears explain why investors started selling in mid-October.

If overpricing had been the whole story, investors would have settled on a lower price and held there. Instead, they reversed most of the damage almost at once, recovering 57% of the Dow's losses, or 288 points, within two sessions. That quick rebound suggests that much of Monday's selling did not reflect a considered judgment that stocks cost too much. Something else pushed the selling well past what a correction needed, starting with trading programs that investors had set to sell on cue.

Program trading handed the decision to sell to preset rules instead of people. Investopedia reports that these automated orders carried no human judgment, and feedback loops turned falling prices into more selling. Once traders set their programs to treat a lower price as a signal to sell, a new order went out after each drop. The New York Stock Exchange routed program trades through its own automated system, called DOT, and the futures market took a severe loss, with S&P 500 futures down 29% on Oct. 19, 1987, according to SEC figures reported by the Federal Reserve Board.

Traders knew how these programs would behave, and that knowledge mattered as much as the programs did. A week before the crash, the Wall Street Journal warned on Oct. 12, 1987, that this kind of selling "could snowball into a stunning rout for stocks," as reported by the Federal Reserve Board. Anyone who expects automated sellers to unload into a drop has a reason to sell first, and that expectation added still more sellers to the rout.

Automated selling made the crash worse, but it did not account for most of the selling. Portfolio insurers, who sold by preset rules, made up roughly 40% of futures sales by traders other than market makers on Oct. 19, 1987, according to Brady Report findings reported by the Federal Reserve Board. Traders without such rules placed the majority of those sales. The programs magnified a fall that many other sellers were already making, and explaining Black Monday takes the rest of the chain.

The clearest sign that the feedback loop was real is that markets later built ways to break it. In 1987 the stop came only after the damage, when the Chicago Mercantile Exchange suspended futures trading at 12:15 p.m. on Oct. 20, the day after the crash, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Under rules in place as of 2022, trading pauses automatically at set levels of decline, according to Investopedia. Exchanges no longer have to improvise a halt in the middle of a crisis.

Program trading did not cause Black Monday on its own, but once the selling began, nobody had a rule in place to stop the programs.

Big investors bought portfolio insurance to protect themselves from a falling market, but the insurers provided that protection by selling as prices dropped. On Black Monday, insurers sold automatically through hedging programs once losses reached preset targets, Investopedia reports, and stop-loss selling cascaded while buyers vanished. Their sales made up a significant fraction of all the selling on Oct. 19, according to 1988 Brady Report findings reported by the Federal Reserve Board, which puts the fall that day in S&P 500 futures, the contracts the insurers traded, at 29%. A rule to sell when prices fall makes each drop the trigger for the next one, so a tool designed to limit one investor's losses spread losses across the market.

The selling did more damage because everyone could see it coming. Other large investors treated the insurers' planned sales as a signal to get out first. In 1988, the Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms, which investigated the crash, described "aggressive-oriented institutions" selling ahead of the insurers' trades, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Their early sales pushed prices lower and set off more insurance selling, which created a spiral driven by anticipation as well as by preset rules. Once the insurers' next move was obvious, traders had good reason to sell rather than buy.

How much of the crash portfolio insurance caused is still an open question. Adam Hayes, a financial writer and former derivatives trader who writes for Investopedia, treats it as apparently central to Black Monday but does not call it the only cause. In a 2012 working paper, Kyle and Obizhaeva estimated that the insurers' selling could have pushed prices down by 11.13% to 15.75% over the four days around Oct. 19, according to the Federal Reserve Board. That estimate points to a major force, though not a proven sole cause. The insurers acted as amplifiers, and they magnified a slide that had begun before Monday's opening bell.

The insurers had committed themselves to selling on a scale large enough to unsettle any market. Under Kyle and Obizhaeva's 2012 estimate, a 4% fall in the Dow would have obliged the insurers to unload $4 billion in stock, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Selling of that size has set off a crash on its own since then. Tugkan Tuzun, a Federal Reserve Board author who writes in his own name rather than for the Board, says that a single sell order of $4.1 billion triggered the Flash Crash in 2010.

The danger was never only the insurers of 1987, but the design of any large strategy that sells because prices are falling.

The last shock to the market before Black Monday came from the trading calendar rather than from any news. Several kinds of contracts expired on Oct. 16, 1987, the Friday before the crash, and Investopedia reports that all three types ran out at the same time. Traders had to handle every one of those expirations in a single session instead of over several days, so the strain arrived all at once. The result was wild volatility and heavy selling just before the crash. Investors went into the weekend with that turmoil still fresh.

The expiring contracts were tied directly to share prices. They included stock options, so their expiration affected the stocks themselves rather than some distant corner of the finance world. Still, one day of expirations could unsettle the market without causing a historic collapse on its own. Investors carried Friday's instability into Monday, and what happened next depended on conditions that were already in place. Stock prices had climbed high enough to fall a long way, and automated selling programs stood ready to answer falling prices with more selling. The expirations mattered because they handed those forces a shaken market at the opening bell.

A routine date on the trading calendar ended up shaping how the worst day in market history began.

Panic could not start the crash, but once prices began to fall, frightened investors kept the decline going. Most experts agree that fear made the decline escalate on Black Monday, as people kept selling after it began. Each round of sales gave the next investor another reason to sell. At that point the drop no longer depended on the futures market, the hedging programs or Friday's expiring contracts to continue. Automated trading and forced hedges started the heavy selling, and frightened people carried it forward.

Blaming the whole day on fear would overstate its role, even though fear is the most visible part of any crash. Several factors worked together that Monday. Investors panicked inside a market that was already overpriced and already falling, and the size of the day's losses belongs to all of those causes together. A crowd of scared sellers does the most damage when nothing in the market's structure slows them down.

The standard protection against panic selling is a forced pause, because frightened investors will not stop selling on their own. Under rules in force as of 2022, exchanges halt trading for 15 minutes after a 7% drop from the previous day's close, according to Investopedia. That first pause comes well before a slide reaches crash scale and makes sellers wait before they place more orders. A second 15-minute halt follows at a 13% drop in case selling resumes after the first break, and exchanges stop trading for the rest of the day after a 20% drop. By that last threshold, frightened investors have sat through two forced breaks, and they lose any further chance to sell until the next session.

The pauses cannot keep investors from being afraid, but they stop frightened selling from running nonstop, as it did in 1987.
