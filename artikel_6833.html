‘I’m Excited to F*ck People Who Read’: Inside the New Dating App for Book Lovers

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Readme wants to make modern dating more intellectually stimulating.

It was nearly 10:00 p.m. on a busy Thursday night in New York City when four young women brushed past a security guard at Funny Bar, a sexy late-night steakhouse, and crashed their way into the party. They made a beeline for the dance floor, knocking into me. Vodka splashed from my martini glass. This was the launch party for Readme, a dating app for readers.

Until the interlopers arrived, the room had been inundated with writers, publishers, podcasters, and other social intellectuals—people who loved to read or pretended they do. Now it was reaching full tilt. I squeezed between partygoers to ask Caroline Calloway—socialite, author of the memoir Scammer, and Readme creative director—about her involvement with the app. “I’m so ready for the dating-app space to not be such a hellscape,” she told me. “I’m more excited than ever to fuck people who are able to read.”

That is the promise of Readme. It aims to be an oasis among the swamp of dating apps where likeminded people can find someone intellectually stimulating that they want to fuck—or at least date a couple times. Dating apps like Hinge, Tinder, and the various off-shoots just aren’t cutting it anymore. A recent Match Group report found that 78 percent of users felt burned-out from the apps, and the number of people who pay for dating apps is declining. Nearly every single person I know who’s still on the apps tells me they never actually talk to anyone out there. That’s where Readme comes in.

“Everyone knows that the existing dating-app culture is deeply unsatisfying overall,” Emily Segal, the head of brand at Readme, says. “It often makes people feel overly performative, transactional, or disposable. We realized that when someone’s taste in books and literature resonates with you, you feel seen on a much deeper level.”

Or, as Calloway told me, “Out of my four major loves, the two that were built on intellectual respect were a lot deeper.”

The tagline for Readme is that it’s for “people whose love language is language,” and it comes at a precarious time for readers. We’re in what many have dubbed the postliterate age. Reading for pleasure has fallen from 28 percent in 2004 to 16 percent in 2023. Most of us would rather doomscroll than crack open a book.

Bypassing the superficiality of modern dating apps by making singles engage with their literary side seems like a novel (pun intended) solution. The app works by taking a detailed account of people’s compatibility. Readme creators Banu Gular and Segal were also behind Co-Star, the astrological-compatibility app with more than 30 million registered users worldwide. When you create an account on Readme, you have to fill out a lengthy quiz that will result in your taste profile. “You answer questions about the books and writing you like, and it gives you a portrait of your taste in language, which we think reflects how you approach love,” Segal says. “The app gives you a portrait of your literary taste and how you see the world, which we call an archetype. You’ll see a snippet of someone’s archetype when you match with them and some information about how you might get along—how you use the information is up to you.”

Back at the launch party, I met a guy who had just moved to New York City and came as a friend’s plus-one. As I quizzed him on his recent reads, Gloria Steinem came up, and to my utter horror, he didn’t know who she was. Desperate to redeem himself, he admitted he was a big Joan Didion fan; his ex introduced him to her writing. Maybe there is hope.
