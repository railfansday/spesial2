Danny Boyle on ‘Ink’ and Murdoch, the ‘Trainspotting’ Toilet Scene and How ‘Slumdog Millionaire’ Almost Went Straight to Video

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The Oscar-winning filmmaker spoke with The Hollywood Reporter in front of an audience at the SCAD Savannah Film Festival, where he was honored with the Maverick Director Award.

“Decades before clickbait, before Fox News, before OnlyFans, before Truth Social, before Google, before Facebook, before any of it, something started that we’re still living with the consequences of,” Danny Boyle said while recording an episode of The Hollywood Reporter’s Awards Chatter podcast in front of an audience at the recent SCAD Savannah Film Festival, where he was being honored with the fest’s Maverick Director Award. The Oscar-winning director was referring to the fateful 1969 meeting of Australian businessman Rupert Murdoch and British newspaperman Larry Lamb, and the tabloid journalism that it spawned, which is chronicled in Boyle’s latest film, Ink, which Netflix will release in theaters on Dec. 11 and on its streaming platform on Jan. 8. The 70-year-old Englishman has directed 15 features over the past 32 years, including 1994’s Shallow Grave, 1996’s Trainspotting, 2002’s 28 Days Later, 2008’s Slumdog Millionaire, 2010’s 127 Hours and 2015’s Steve Jobs. Described by the New York Times as “a cinematic hypnotist” and by The Guardian as “a film director in a class of his own,” he won best director Academy, Golden Globe and BAFTA awards for Slumdog, and an Emmy for his work on the London 2012 Olympic Games Opening Ceremony. And he also was presented with a Britannia Award for Artistic Excellence in 2009 and a BFI Fellowship in 2010.

On the scene in Trainspotting in which Ewan McGregor swims through “the worst toilet in Scotland”…

“CG was just beginning to emerge in cinemas. In fact, there was a Christmas film out at the time with Tim Allen playing Santa Clause, and they squeezed him into a chimney pot. That’s one of the very first uses of CG in film. It looked terrible, I thought. I thought, ‘We’re not going to do it like that,’ because somebody said, ‘Oh, you could use CG,’ as it was at the time. We wanted to do it live. So we used a theater trick, the trompe l’oeil: you cut the toilet in half and film it from the side. So, obviously, it isn’t a toilet on this side; it’s the slide that McGregor goes down. Then Brian [Tufano, the cinematographer] told Ewan [McGregor, the film’s star] to twist his feet ’round at the end, because then people will think it’s the U-bend that you’re going ’round, which curiously people do. It’s just that illusion that we buy into. If you believe the actors, you’ll believe anything! So people believed it was the worst toilet in Scotland, even though it smelled of chocolate, because all the shit was chocolate, and it was delicious! All the prop girls had candles burning so that everybody felt good about being there all day.”

On how Slumdog Millionaire almost went straight-to-video instead of to the Oscars…

“There was a change of regime at Warner Bros., and there was a change of emphasis. They decided that films that they put a bit of money up for would not be worth the cost of releasing, so they decided to put them straight out on video. And it wasn’t just my film, there was a Guy Ritchie film as well, Revolver, and a couple of others like that, as well. Fortunately, Peter Rice, [who was running Fox Searchlight at the time and] who’s just taken over Netflix, picked it up and bought it off Warners, which normally doesn’t happen. He knew somehow that when you played it with an audience, it would prove itself.”

“The film’s really about truth, so you have to tell the truth about Murdoch and what Murdoch was like. Even though people would prefer you to paint him as a villain, he, in fact, had many reservations about what was going on. But he didn’t act on them, which is as much condemnation as you need, I think, rather than just portraying him as a villain.”

“He is the kind of actor like [Al] Pacino is, who can take you to an impossibly dark place — in Pacino’s case, the ordering of the execution of his brother [in The Godfather, Part II] — and yet you still lean into the character. Jack has that. If you think about he stuff he does in Sinners and in 28 Years Later: The Bone Temple, he’s a monster, and yet you’re drawn into him. That’s a weird duality he has, which not many actors do. He’s a wonderful, wonderful actor.”
