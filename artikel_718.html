‘The Gospel of Christmas’ Review: Tyler Perry’s Churchy Musical Drama Does a Threadbare Job of Getting Into the Spirit

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Perry cuts out the soap-opera extravagance in a Christmas musical so earnest but rickety it's like a Sunday sermon stretched out to movie form.

As an intermittent Tyler Perry fan, I was eager to see what a Tyler Perry Christmas movie would look like. That’s partly because I love Christmas movies, but also because it seemed a natural for his wheelhouse. Did I want to hear Madea, in her sinfully irreverent but pious way, go off on Christmas? Hell, yes. I also wanted to see Tyler Perry’s idea of making merry.

But Perry’s “The Gospel of Christmas” turns out to be as earnest and generically reverential as its title. There is no Madea in it; there is no Tyler Perry performance as any other nasty relative; and there is very little of his shameless but diverting soap-opera extravagance. “The Gospel of Christmas” is a straight-up drama of faith (losing it, testing it, regaining it), which would be fine if the film weren’t wrapped around a family saga so rickety and threadbare it’s like watching a Sunday sermon padded out to movie form. One reason “The Gospel of Christmas” has such a weirdly abstract flavor is that it’s a musical, filled with churchy soul and gospel numbers designed to carry the action along. Some of the songs are quite lovely, and a couple of them are pop-gospel rousers, yet their mood is so at odds with the rest of the film that they almost seem part of another movie. The songs don’t make “The Gospel of Christmas” any less sodden — they just break up the soddenness like commercials.

The film’s star, Durrell “Tank” Babbs, is a good actor, but the role Perry has written for him calls on him to mope, wallow in self-pity, get angry and irritated, and then mope some more. It’s as if we were watching George Bailey from “It’s a Wonderful Life” at his most hopeless moment, only stretched out to 90 minutes. Babbs’ Isaac Jefferson is a former bad-boy pop singer who was once famous (his biggest hit: “Gimme Them Panties”), but his career hit the skids 20 years ago. How did that happen? We’re not sure. Nothing in “The Gospel of Christmas” is filled in in a convincing way. The film plays like a Christmas “parable” because it’s so stripped of detail.

On his deathbed, Isaac’s clergyman father asks Isaac to take over the Baptist church of his great-grandfather, which is located in New York City. Isaac, the prodigal son, has no desire to become a pastor. But he feels like he can’t turn his father down. So he and his wife, Rebecca (Fantasia Barrino), and their two sons — Jacob (Mason Douglas), surly and tall, and Esau (Eric Wiltz), who’s about 10 and does not speak — move from Georgia to Perry’s studio-set version of the big bad city, which looks even stagier than the New York of “Eyes Wide Shut.” The church, a stone edifice, is an impressive chunk of real estate, but when Isaac gets into the office he sees a pile of foreclosure letters. The church is five months behind on rent, and with penalties and taxes they’ll need to come up with $58,000 to keep it going.

That’s quite a demand, but Isaac’s real burden is that he’s forgotten how to pray; he’s forgotten how to believe. The movie is all about his coming to see the light, but you wish it happened in a more flowing, less contrived way. “The Gospel of Christmas” has a chorus of angels, who give the movie its only hint of levity. It’s got a villain, Mr. Ehtami Lived, a pawn-shop purveyor who represents Temptation — though Ricco Ross, who plays him, is a subtle enough actor to give him more dimension than anyone else in the movie. There’s a plot twist that’s a real whopper, as Isaac, out of nowhere, gets offered $3 million to do a concert tour — an offer, his whole family realizes, that’s coming straight from the devil, though the audience for his kick-off concert is so pumped that all I could think was, “Why did his career jump the track?” Isaac, of course, gets his faith groove back, and I suppose you could say that “The Gospel of Christmas” is more of a Christmas movie than most Christmas movies, since it’s really about…believing in Jesus. If only it were easier to believe the story it’s telling.
