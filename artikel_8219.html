4 Backcountry Field Repairs That Can Save Your Trip (When Duct Tape Isn’t Enough)

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

No matter how well you’ve prepared or how careful you are with your gear, at some point, whether by cactus spine or sheer volume of trail miles, you’re going to tear a piece of clothing. Or, if you’re me, you’re going to flop down on your sleeping pad, which is on top of your toiletry bag, which holds a pair of impossibly vertical tweezers, and punch an irreparable hole in your mat on the first night of a 225-mile backpacking trip.

You can’t fix every gear problem in the field—a melted down jacket is going to need professional attention or replacement—but with a few simple supplies and some know-how you can handle more of them than you think. Here are four field repairs beyond taping your down jacket you should know, along with a minimal repair kit to get you to the next road crossing.

The glue line that attaches the bottom package—the midsole and outsole—to the shoe upper is the most common structural failure in footwear. While you can manage a few days with a blown-out upper or worn-down outsole tread, the bottom of your shoe flapping in the breeze is a problem.

If you’re close enough to town or have a sense of humor, you can wear your Crocs as hiking shoes. For the rest of us, use a combination of lacing, zip ties, and duct tape to keep your kicks together long enough to get back to civilization. The most secure way to reattach the bottom of the shoe to the top is with an X pattern, similar to how you’d wrap a sprained ankle. If you have a spare set of laces or shock cord, begin at the front of the shoe and criss-cross over the bottom and top in a series of figure-eights until you reach the top of the laces. From here, you can cover the lacing with duct tape in the same pattern. If you brought zip ties, you can punch through the outsole/midsole (be sure to punch far enough in that it doesn’t wear through the side) and the edge of the upper where it won’t rub or cause blisters. Secure it tightly, then finish with the duct tape in a figure-8 pattern.

I typically don’t carry single-use items in my pack aside from the core components of my kit, but a tent pole splint is an exception. Tent poles carry high amounts of tension from taut pitches and are under even more strain in high winds, and the tape-a-stake-to-the-pole simply does not work. A tent pole splint (or sleeve) is a tube made from the same material as the pole, and simply slides over the bent or broken section to create an unbroken line.

Before you do anything else, straighten the bent pole as much as possible or line up the two broken sections as close as they can get. Once the sleeve is centered over the break, secure it with plenty of duct tape. The pole splints are typically not much wider than the pole itself, so it should hold in place fairly well until you can get a replacement pole section.

I’ve slapped plenty of patches onto my down jacket and sleeping pads, but tearing your fly is trickier because you’re relying on the material for waterproofing. I ripped a 10-inch hole in my Big Agnes Copper Spur tent a few years ago, and my attempt at a duct-tape repair held up for about six minutes before condensation separated it again. This is where the Tenacious Tape comes in handy, with its flexible nylon, vinyl, or ripstop material and aggressive adhesive backing. It sticks better than duct tape and can fend off a decent amount of moisture for a surprisingly long time.

Be sure to clean the area around the tear as thoroughly as possible (alcohol wipes come in handy here) and dry it thoroughly before applying the tape. GearAid also makes an incredibly waterproof Aquaseal adhesive compound that can act as a seam sealer or smooth on over raincoat tears.

I must be cursed with backpacking stoves, because I feel like my ignitor stops working after my first few trips. I try to remember a lighter for this very reason, but if you didn’t and the finicky ignitor stopped sparking, you can try repositioning the electrode tip. This works if you can hear the igniter clicking but not lighting the gas—the delicate metal electrode wire likely bent away from the port.

Use your multitool pliers here and position the tip five millimeters (just under 1/4 inch) from the center ring, which should be enough for the spark to jump. The wire on the popular MSR PocketRocket tends to slip out of its guide tube, and if you know what to look for, you can slide it back into the alignment sleeve to restore contact. This video demonstrates replacing the ignitor on a similar stove. (Alternatively, just remember your lighter.)

Some items, like load-bearing pack straps or buckles, will need to be tied together until you get to town, but there are a few things that are handy to take into the backcountry.

The short answer is tape, tape, tape, tape. I wrap a healthy dose of duct tape (bonus points for weird patterns) around my trekking poles or a prescription bottle to cobble together many repairs. I also bring a few inches of Tenacious Tape, designed to patch waterproof fabrics like rain jackets and tent flies, and a NoSo Patch, which works wonders for keeping the feathers inside a torn jacket or sleeping bag.

For thru-hikes or longer trips where I’m going to be way in the backcountry, I bring a tiny sewing kit, a lightweight knife with grippy pliers and a screwdriver end, a few zip ties for holding a backpack together, a tent pole splint, and several safety pins. Finally, your inflatable sleeping pad likely came with its own tiny repair kit, including an alcohol wipe and a matching repair patch with adhesive optimized for the pad material. This whole kit weighs three ounces and can help prevent some serious headaches.
