Paul Bettany and Ruaridh Mollica Can Finally Talk About Those ‘VisionQuest’ Spoilers

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

A week on from that buzzy finale, the co-stars, who play synthezoid Vision and his superpowered son Tommy Maximoff, tell all to THR about filming the final chapter of the ‘WandaVision’ trilogy and what that heartfelt family reunion (minus one key character) means for their futures in the MCU. "As long as there are interesting and fun stories for Vision, I’ll be putting on my tights," quips Bettany.

[This story contains MAJOR spoilers from the finale of VisionQuest, “Forever.”]

For all its existential theatrics, Marvel’s VisionQuest was always going to tell a profoundly human love story between a synthezoid father, Vision (Paul Bettany), and the long-lost son he finally claims as his own, Tommy Maximoff (Ruaridh Mollica).

Created by Terry Matalas, the new Disney+ series picks up the threads left dangling after WandaVision and Agatha All Along, where White Vision vanished into the sky with his restored memories and Tommy’s soul was reincarnated into the body of teenager Thomas Shepherd. Over the course of eight episodes, VisionQuest finds both characters wrestling with their fractured sense of identity. Vision works to piece together his humanity while keeping his paternal instincts at arm’s length — fearing that acknowledging Tommy means accepting the loss of Wanda Maximoff (Elizabeth Olsen) — even as Tommy struggles to reconcile his new life with his latent super-speed and a deep longing for the family he barely remembers. Vision and Tommy’s parallel journeys collide in the finale, when grief pushes a newly unleashed Ultron (James Spader) — Vision’s own mercurial A.I. father figure — to launch a deadly nanobot swarm on London after Jocasta (T’Nia Miller), the companion he unwittingly falls for, is killed. Rather than defeating his creator through brute force, Vision pulls Ultron into a shared sitcom mindscape — a poignant callback to WandaVision — to talk him through his pain and convince him to stand down. The gambit saves the city at the cost of Vision’s own power core, leaving Tommy and his newly arrived twin brother Billy (Joe Locke) to combine their powers and revive their father, cementing the family bond Vision spent all season running from.

Having finally reclaimed his humanity (and transformed back to his original red self), Vision decides to finally release the various A.I.s from his mindscape, dismantling the internal sanctuary he once used to shut out the world so he can build a real life with his now-grown sons.

By the end of VisionQuest, Vision is “fully ready to engage with planet Earth [and] the people in it, rather than this space that he has created where he can just hide and ruminate in his own mind,” Bettany tells The Hollywood Reporter. Still, the English actor — who first played the synthezoid in 2015 — insists that he doesn’t know what the future holds for his character: “As long as there are interesting and fun stories for Vision, I’ll be putting on my tights.”

Similarly, Mollica claims that he does not know when he will play Tommy again, but he knows how his character fits into the MCU canon. “I loved the Young Avengers comics,” says the Scottish-Italian actor. “I really started reading them all as I was going through the audition process for character research. Those stories are so cool, and it’d be cool to explore that one day.”

In separate conversations last week, which have been edited into the extended Q&A below, Bettany and Mollica spoke to THR about playing out Vision and Tommy’s parallel trajectories, sharing the screen with Locke’s Billy for the first time, and their hopes for a full Maximoff family reunion.

Jac Schaeffer initially conceived WandaVision as the first show of an MCU trilogy, but she reportedly stepped away from this Vision sequel to focus on Agatha All Along, and Terry Matalas quickly stepped in to redevelop the show. Paul, what exactly was your collective vision — no pun intended — for this final installment, and how involved were you in crafting the story as an executive producer?

PAUL BETTANY It was actually a great thing that we had five years to figure this out. We were able to sit with ideas about what the show could be. It’s easy to think of Marvel as a kind of infernal machine churning out endless product, and actually, it’s very boutique in that you can count the people on one hand that you’re actually speaking to about a project. When Terry came on, he and I went and had a delicious lunch, and he was very collaborative, which is in keeping with how all of my experiences with Marvel have been. He was asking what I wanted to do, and I knew that we were going to have Tommy in it and eventually Billy — it feels so weird to say because I’ve been keeping that so secretive for so long! (Laughs.)

To that end, I wanted to make it really about fathers and sons. I thought it would be a really cool counterprogramming to WandaVision, which was a lot of female energy, to end it with something that was a lot of positive male energy and see how different characters deal with their grief. So when Terry said, “I have an idea. What about we get the band back together and we get James Spader?” I was like, “That’s it! Because now we can do pain and trauma being passed down from father to son, but do it with robots and lasers and fighting and all of that great Marvel stuff.”

Ruaridh, you didn’t even know what you were auditioning for until you met Paul and Terry for a screen test in Los Angeles. How did those early conversations inform your approach to playing Tommy, given that he is also grappling with dual identities like Vision?

RUARIDH MOLLICA The hardest part was figuring out the inner conflict within Tommy right at the start in the first few episodes, where he is denying his existence as Tommy Maximoff, and he’s very much holding onto his life as Thomas Shepherd. He grew up an orphaned kid, he’s not had a lot of support and love in his life, and all he really wants to be is seen. So when you throw something else into the mix that he others himself with, it makes that harder to accept. He’s really struggling with the impossibility of these nightmares that he’s having.

Through those conversations, we really wanted to lean into his utter fear. In that first scene where Tommy meets Vision, I’m strapped in the chair in St. Mayben’s [the psychiatric facility where Thomas Shepherd was held after Tommy’s consciousness was moved into his body], and White Vision goes underneath the red light, and it makes up the red face of all of these nightmares that my character had been having. I put a picture of Paul’s face as Red Vision on my wall and wrote “Devil! Nightmare!” around it, because it was so important to be afraid of him at the start.

And then it meant a really natural progression into slowly trusting and that back and forth of slowly accepting the truth. I guess we’re both doing that simultaneously. To see them on such a parallel journey is really funny sometimes because they’re both wanting the same thing and both unable to accept it. But that’s what’s so beautiful about that relationship, because they need each other to do that.

For most of the season, Vision acts like a father to Tommy while refusing to claim the title, fearing that acknowledging his son means accepting the loss of Wanda and the destruction of their family. It isn’t until he confronts that grief and embraces his humanity that he can finally step wholeheartedly back into fatherhood.

BETTANY You’ve identified what the whole show really is about, which is an acceptance of who you are and being cool with your identity. There is a moment where I’m with Tommy at the pool in episode three, and I tell him, “This isn’t you. I just haven’t been listening well enough, and you are who you think you are.” I think that’s a really powerful thing to say to young people.

I’ve had the fortune — because I’ve been playing this character so long — of meeting the people who are fans of that character. If you listen and you are open, you will hear what feels important about Vision to them — and I really did listen. There was a lot of similarity in the people who were fans of Vision, and I realized, “Oh, that was me when I was young. I was the bullied kid. I was the kid who didn’t feel like he fit in anywhere, who was so concerned that there wasn’t going to be a place for him in the adult world.” And then you understand what that contract is between the audience and Vision.

So that moment foreshadows the moment where Vision finally accepts who he is and what has happened to him, which Terry and I wanted to be the moment that Vision becomes his most powerful. It’s true of humans: [The moment] you really accept who you are and what has happened to you and are okay with that is when you become the most powerful version of yourself. So because we’re in a superhero show with robots, that’s where he gets his powers back. The initial remit from Kevin Feige was: How do we get cool White Vision back to warm Red Vision? We thought it’d be so great and a nice bit of clockwork storytelling if that is the reason that he becomes whole again.

Vision’s personal breakthrough comes in episode seven, when he finally confronts the grief of losing his family — allowing him to transform back into his red self. He only reaches that point after an encounter in his mindscape with two figures whose own complicated father-son dynamic forces him to reflect on his role as a parent: Dr. Phineas Horton, the WWII-era scientist who built the first synthetic man, and J.I.M., his emotionally conflicted creation. Considering you had to play all three characters in that scene, what was the biggest challenge of acting opposite yourself multiple times?

BETTANY I’m always up for a challenge, and it was the most challenging three days mentally, but not really for the reasons one might think. I had a brilliant stunt man called John who was up for learning all of the lines, and he would play the scenes with me. But the problem is when you’re working on your script, you know the story and the lines, but you don’t know how the other actor is going to say those lines. It made me really relish working with actual other actors who are not me, because, for instance, [when] you’re working with James Spader, there’s never a scene where he doesn’t do something to surprise you, and that’s gold dust as an actor working opposite them. Whilst I am working opposite John, I basically know what I’m going to do when I’m the other person.

So it was a tricky three days, but it was such a laugh to know what we were trying to accomplish. There was some pressure on that scene, because it’s really where you are landing the plane in terms of Vision coming to terms with who he is. The last thing we shot was White Vision’s coverage.

Terry, who was directing episodes seven and eight, did a fantastic job with me. He’s an actor’s director, and he pushed me. It’s a silly thing, right? You think of sad things and make yourself upset, and unless you’re an insane person, it’s hard to want to stay in that place. You get in there and you want out; you want to get the shot. I said, “I don’t know, Terry. I think we’ve got it.” He walked me around the corner and we sat down together, and he went, “I think we got it, but I was wondering: We do still have a little bit of time left, and if you have one [more]…” He was, in that very gentle way, pushing me to dig deeper — and that’s the take [of Vision’s breakdown] he used.

As Vision rediscovers his own powers, Tommy — whose superhero name is Speed — is also just beginning to realize that he has powers he can’t control yet. Ruaridh, how did you figure out what those powers would actually look like in live action?

MOLLICA One of my favorite parts about superhero films and shows is when they’re figuring out their powers and don’t quite know how they work yet. At the start, it had to manifest as smaller things. So exploring how that would exist in the transference of energy from the electrical cattle prod in episode two felt like a good way to let that out first, where even Tommy’s not sure what happened. It planted seeds of the truth in his psyche, but it wasn’t a big enough event for him to be like, “Oh wow, I have superpowers,” because it could still be played off as a freak accident.

But it was really cool doing the one in the swimming pool with Paul when I bring him back to life, exploring how speed manifests in being able to change the molecular structure and put that atomic speed into the water to create enough electrical charge to bring him back. We did things multiple times so they’d be stacked over each other in post and used a lot of flickering light and the camera at a certain frame rate [to create that effect].

The running was great, though. When I say, “I’m Tommy Maximoff,” [in the finale], that was the first time I actually sprinted. It was my favorite moment to shoot the whole show because it felt so badass, and Terry was just like, “Just fucking leg it.” And I just ran! I was like, “This is my big sprint moment. I’ve got to sprint.” And only Terry could take the epic superhero moment and make it hilarious by having a father-son argument while running around the table.

Did you have any particular conversations about Tommy’s green Adidas jacket, given that it is the closest he gets to wearing a super suit this season?

MOLLICA At the start of the show, we actually did plan to fit a superhero suit, and it was only later on as we got into the show that [we realized] Billy could create his suit because of his powers, but there was actually no way for Tommy to find a speed suit. I guess we leaned more into how Spider-Man made his first suit. We wanted to do a really strong nod to the suit and have that essence of the comic book green. Obviously, we did get our speed goggles, which are such a signature part of Speed. I think the direction of the track suit and that kind of vibe goes hand in hand with his superpower and the nod to running. It’s kind of funny — of course, Tommy was like, “Wow, I’m super Speed. What would I wear? Oh yeah, running gear.”

Paul, you mentioned that the plan was always to have Billy reunite with Vision and Tommy by the end of VisionQuest. Ruaridh, at what point did Terry clue you in that the twins would be reuniting and using their combined powers to save their father?

MOLLICA I didn’t know before we’d started shooting or anything because I don’t think those scripts had been written yet. I’m sure the ideas were already there, but it must have been almost halfway through when I found out Joe was coming back. I actually think I knew Joe was coming back before he did! (Laughs.)

It was absolutely epic to read that in the script, but doing those scenes in real life and bringing that off of the page was just so magical — pardon the pun — because it’s really a different visual language that gets brought in with Joe’s character that is his signature blues and purples and witchy hexes. It was like stepping into a whole different world, which was really quite exciting. There was that really tough day where we meet each other in the hex for the first time [at the start of the finale]. It was lit by all real candles, so that was the hottest room in the world, so much so that I think me and Joe spent a lot of time laughing.

Tommy initially believes that Billy is too far away to help him, but Tommy could not save Vision alone. Can you give voice to what is going through Tommy’s mind when he sees Billy come through that portal?

MOLLICA I think he just feels held and united in a way that I don’t think he ever has felt before. Tommy really goes into that moment believing that he can bring Vision back. And when he can’t, I think he starts to doubt his strength: “How could I possibly lose the father that I only just found?” Terry and I really agreed that Tommy should be crying in this moment. For him, he has just lost the most important person in his life that he only just found. And having Billy come in that moment to help save the day really solidifies this theme of what WandaVision was, which is that strength of family to tackle things. Obviously, for Wanda, it was to tackle her grief; in this situation, it’s to resurrect. There’s such beauty in that theme.

What else do you each remember from shooting that emotional father-sons reunion?

BETTANY It was really uncomfortable. (Laughs.) I was wildly overacting, and my eye had been blown out from the day before or something from overacting again. [Vision’s] being sort of electric shocked, I guess, to try and bring him back to life, and I was on top of that goddamn nest. (Laughs.) But, actually, it was a lot of fun and it felt like a moment of, the sun is shining, and [imitates a warm hug] God is in His heaven. I wanted it to be, as I did a lot of moments, redolent of things that people know. I think that every parent knows what it is when you’ve been away from your children to just be able to pull your children into you and hold onto them. So, yeah, it was a lovely moment for me.

MOLLICA That finale scene was actually my last day on the whole job. So to have Joe there for that as well was actually really beautiful, and it was quite a momentous way to end everything — and it was really powerful.

Making the show was the single most incredible and biggest moment of my whole life so far. So to have had Paul in there with me at the screen test, where I was learning about everything for the first time and finding out I got the job, and then to be with him at the end — it’s just that funny thing of art imitating life. Our relationship on the show, I feel like, really reflects our relationship in real life. I am always asking Paul for advice, and I feel very supported by him as a titan of the MCU.

Paul, you recently joked that you blew a bunch of blood vessels in your eye while shooting the show, but I didn’t realize that it happened toward the end of filming the finale. Were you really overacting?

BETTANY Well, you can imagine the pressure of thinking, “Okay, so I’ve got a device on my head that’s sucking out my brain somehow. What’s that going to look like?” So you do it and that’s all fine, and then you realize, “Fuck! I’ve got to do this for six hours, or whatever.” (Laughs.) And you’re kind of going [imitates grimacing] for six hours. At some point, [I began] making a self-deprecating joke about wildly overacting and blowing out my eye and it being a lesson in “less is more,” because I don’t know how many thousands and thousands of dollars it must have cost to paint my [bloodied] eye out.

In their final scene together, Vision and Tommy talk about having dinner with Billy in Eastview. What do you imagine their lives together will look like now? How does Vision feel about having to parent his boys now as young men?

MOLLICA I think they’ll just make up for so much lost time; they’ll just want to be together and do regular father-son things. I imagine all the memories they had of their time in WandaVision — I think their first Halloween again together will be funny. I imagine it will still sometimes be awkward and clunky, like that scene in episode six when we’re both alive [after the shootout] and I get out the back of Paladin’s van, and Vision’s like, “Well… I’m glad you’re alright.” And I’m like, “I’m glad you’re alright.” There’s those silly, awkward moments that will always exist in that relationship because that is what family is.

BETTANY I think right now [Vision’s] thrilled and high on it all, and I’m sure the reality of all of that will cause complications. I have a similar thing that happened in my actual life when I married Jennifer [Connelly]. She already had a son. I become a sort of instant father, and that is incredibly exciting. And then the reality of it is there is a lot of work really to build that connection.

I don’t know how to play a robot. I never have. I’ve never tried to. I always try to find human paradigms because I don’t know what else to do. Vision has the problem that he seems to have a biological son in the world, but that’s impossible — and yet, somehow, it is true. He has biological children. [My stepson] Kai is not mine biologically, but somehow he feels part of me — and it’s just true. I feel no differently toward him than I do my actual biological children. They feel exactly the same. Some impossible osmosis has happened that we have become a part of each other. So I think I’ve relied very heavily on that experience for doing this fun, ridiculous notion of a robot having biological children in the world.

In their final scene together, Tommy asks Vision if he thinks there is any chance, with the two of them and Billy still around, that they may see Wanda again. Did you and the creative team ever consider bringing Wanda back for the finale? [Writer’s note: The finale features a post-credits scene — not included in press screeners — of Robert Downey Jr’s Doctor Doom awakening Wanda, who had seemingly been crushed under a mountain at the end of Multiverse of Madness.]

BETTANY Yes, there were conversations, and in the end, I think it was the right decision. It would seem that we have managed to make the fans care about [Vision’s] journey, and for the moment he becomes most powerful to be an acceptance of what he has lost, and then to just immediately give him back [what he lost], I think could have destroyed the end of the quest for him. Because the quest in this, as it turns out, is not to find Wanda — it’s to find himself. He has always been for Wanda. He’s always sort of existed for her… and [a decision] was made at a high level that, “No, this is Vision’s story, and it needs to finish as Vision’s story.” I’m (a) very flattered by that, and (b) I think it was probably the right decision. Who knows what the future [will hold]? If you tie too many of those things up, what are you going to do next?

Do you think Wanda and Vision would still work as a couple? Would they find a way back to each other?

BETTANY Well, wouldn’t that be fascinating to find out? My instinct is that whilst there can be tumultuous moments, rather like Romeo and Juliet, you just have to imagine that they would. I do!

Of course, besides wanting to see Vision and Wanda back together, a lot of fans are waiting with bated breath for her to reunite with her sons. Ruaridh, you’ve affectionately referred to Tommy and Billy as the “queer twin brothers” of the MCU. What has it meant to you to be an openly bisexual actor playing a bisexual character on a project of this scale?

MOLLICA I feel so lucky (a) to be in this world of the MCU, but (b) to get to portray a character that champions the sexuality that I am as well. It’s so important to have that on such a big platform that people can watch and hopefully feel seen themselves. I do think bisexual representation in big media is confusing. People don’t really know how to tackle it or address it, so I feel really, really happy to be hopefully a good example for that, which is why I want to be vocal about it. Hopefully, in the future, we explore some of Tommy’s other relationships that exist in the comics.

Have you talked to Joe at all about the pressure and privilege of bringing that kind of queer visibility and representation to the MCU? Does it come up in conversation often between the two of you?

MOLLICA Yeah, it totally does. He’s such an important figure in queer media [because of Heartstopper], and to be talking to him who is openly gay and has done Agatha and is in the MCU, and to have someone to ask what that is like was really important.

As if this role could not be more tailor made for you, Ruaridh, you actually earned an undergraduate degree in computer science before deciding to become an actor. Were you thinking at all about the accuracy of the coding or artificial intelligence terminology while filming the show?

MOLLICA [Giddily] I actually really did think about it. I would be reading the scripts trying to be like, “What have they said there? Does that make sense?” Obviously, I wanted all the code talk and things like that to make sense. For me, it was actually really reassuring because when I read it for the first time, almost all of it is based in so much truth. I’m watching it again as it’s coming out and noticing all these other things and turning to my friend and going, “Yeah, that’s actually how we would do that in real life.” I really geeked out being in Vision’s mindspace. Obviously, I didn’t have a lot of scenes in there, but sometimes I would go over and have a little poke around.

I just loved that imagining of an A.I. trying to make things look normal. [Costume designer] Sarah Young is posting all the details of the costumes on her Instagram at the moment, and one of Vision’s shirts has three collars and three cuffs all stacked on top of each other. It’s just things like that that I think are brilliant and so true. If an A.I. was to make a shirt, it probably would have three collars.
