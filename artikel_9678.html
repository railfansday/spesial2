Energy-Based Reasoners: Giving Looped Reasoning Models a Built-In Progress Signal

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Looped reasoning models reuse one small block many times to get depth without adding parameters. They work well on puzzles, but they have a blind spot: nothing in the loop says whether the current latent state is any good, when to stop iterating, or which of several candidate answers to trust. A new paper from Duke, UIUC and Harvard, Energy-Based Reasoners: Your Recurrent Reasoner Should Be an Energy-Based Model, adds that missing signal. Its 1.8M-parameter model reaches 99.9% on Sudoku-Extreme with 345x less inference compute than the next-best model the authors compare against. This post walks through how the mechanism works and why it matters.

The Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) showed that a 27M-parameter recurrent network with two modules running at different frequencies could solve hard Sudoku and maze tasks from about 1,000 training examples. Tiny Recursive Models (TRM) then simplified the design to a single 2-layer network with 7M parameters and reported 45% test accuracy on ARC-AGI-1.

Both share a structure: a latent state is updated again and again, and the final state is decoded into an answer. The EBR paper's abstract names the gap directly. These models are "not inherently guided by an explicit measure of progress," so it is hard to decide when to stop looping and how to improve the current state. In practice this means a fixed number of iterations for every input. Easy puzzles waste compute, hard ones may be cut short, and there is no built-in way to rank several sampled answers without an external verifier.

The refinement step is what makes this different from simply adding a value head to a recurrent model. The same network that defines the score also defines the direction of improvement, so the score and the update cannot drift apart. The authors report that correct solutions settle at low-energy stationary states with small gradients, while wrong trajectories keep moving. That behavior is what makes the gradient usable as a stopping criterion.

An energy function is only useful if its landscape is shaped well away from the training solutions. If the model only ever sees correct answers, it has no reason to assign high energy to plausible wrong ones, and descent from a bad start could stall in spurious minima. The paper addresses this with what it calls exploration-based training. According to the abstract, the goal is to discover additional modes and shape the energy landscape over a broader set of states.

This also bears on puzzles with many valid answers. N-Queens and graph coloring can have multiple solutions per input. A model trained to emit one fixed answer collapses to a single mode. The authors describe EBR as behaving like stochastic gradient descent, so different starting points can fall into different valid minima. That is why they measure coverage: the share of an input's distinct valid solutions found by 20 samples.

The reported results come from algorithmic reasoning benchmarks, not language tasks, so read them in that context.

On single-sample accuracy, the project page lists EBR at 99.4% on Sudoku-Extreme and 87.4% on Maze-Hard. The listed baselines include TRM at 87.4% and 85.3%, and looped flows at 97.9% and 86.7%. With multiple samples, EBR reaches 99.9% on Sudoku-Extreme and 88.3% on Maze-Hard using 5 samples. EqR needs 128 samples to reach 99.8% on Sudoku, and the page says EBR reaches EqR's best accuracy with 7x fewer iterations and 43x fewer samples.

On multi-solution tasks, EBR gets 100% pass@1 on 8x8 N-Queens and 99.5% on 10x10, against 99.5% and 89.7% for GRAM. On graph coloring it reports zero conflicts with one sample. Coverage at 20 samples is 98.1% and 92.0% on the two N-Queens sizes, compared with 89.7% and 57.5% for GRAM. On graph coloring the coverage margin is smaller: 88.6% against 85.8% on 8-vertex graphs, and 54.5% against 51.3% on 10-vertex graphs.

The efficiency claim comes mostly from adaptive stopping. The page states that stopping each trajectory once its energy gradient falls below a threshold keeps 99.9% accuracy with 10.9x fewer FLOPs than a fixed-depth EBR. Combined with the sample efficiency, that is how the authors arrive at the 345x figure against EqR. Easy puzzles finish in a few steps, and hard ones keep iterating.

Several caveats apply. The benchmarks are structured puzzles with checkable answers, where an energy function over a small discrete state is tractable to learn. Nothing in the source material shows this approach working on open-ended language reasoning, and the paper's title makes a claim about recurrent reasoners rather than LLMs. The multi-solution baseline numbers are taken from GRAM's paper according to the project page, so comparisons depend on matching experimental setups. The headline 345x figure is also relative to one specific baseline and assumes comparable training compute, as the abstract notes.

Still, the design choice is worth attention from anyone building small reasoning models. Verifier-free selection is a practical benefit. Many test-time scaling pipelines need a separate reward model or checker to choose among samples. Here the model's own energy does that job, and the gradient norm gives a principled halting rule without a learned halting head.

For developers, the takeaway is a pattern rather than a library: if your recurrent model iterates, give it a scalar that measures progress and derive the update from it. The authors link code from the project page for anyone who wants to reproduce the Sudoku and N-Queens results.

Energy-Based Reasoners take the recurrent puzzle solvers popularized by HRM and TRM and make their loop accountable: one learned energy decides how to refine, when to stop and which answer to keep. The evidence so far is limited to algorithmic benchmarks, but the efficiency gains from adaptive stopping and verifier-free selection are large enough on those tasks to make energy-based latent reasoning worth following as researchers test it on harder, more varied problems.
