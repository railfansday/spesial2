In the 80s, there was an ad that asked, "Is it live or is it Memorex?" These days, the more appropriate question to ask ourselves is, "Is it real or is it AI?"

More and more of what we're seeing, especially on social media, is fake news and AI-generated slop. This has been going on for a while now—I've been writing about fake news for years—but earlier this fall, I noted that the modern media landscape seems to have devolved into "the pageant of the unreal."

Examples of false content being mistaken for facts are everywhere, ranging from the trivial to the outright dangerous.

Back in September, it was reported that there was a venomous snake on the loose in Orange County—an African Gaboon viper, no less—based on the testimony of a man who reported the snake to the police based on what he claimed was photographic evidence.1 Although the report was initially treated as an emergency and with a warning to the public carried by reputable news outlets, it was soon discovered that the photo was AI-generated. Police were considering charging the man with false reporting.

Around the same time, three hikers used Google's Gemini to plot a route to reach the summit of Mount Shasta. The chatbot provided directions as well as requested advice about what provisions to bring for the journey. Following the chatbot's instructions, the hikers ended up stranded in a deep canyon without enough food and water. Fortunately, they were able to call for help and ended up getting rescued by the US Forest Service.2Last month, a woman with a severe seizure disorder sought advice from OpenAI's ChatGPT about tapering off her seizure medications and the chatbot reassured her that she could "start microdosing as you see fit."2 It advised her to hide her "self-initiated dosage adjustment" from her doctor, calling it a "solid avoidance strategy." When she began having seizures again and expressed concerns, ChatGPT reassured her that she had "absolutely no need to worry." Even when she implored the chatbot to restart her medications as she felt increasingly ill, it told her not to, telling her that her brain was merely "working hard to rebuild itself."

Sadly, the woman wasn't as lucky as the Mount Shasta hikers—she died, presumably from an intractable seizure. Her family has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI.

One of the best—that is, most evidence-based—ways to avoid succumbing to fake news is through analytical thinking. In my book, FALSE: How Mistrust, Disinformation, and Motivated Reasoning Make Us Believe Things That Aren't True, I describe analytical thinking as one of the three pillars of the "Holy Trinity of Truth Detection." Analytical thinking doesn't mean being especially smart or even analytical; it just means taking the time to slow down when we encounter information and consider that it might not be true—especially when it reinforces what we already believe or want to believe. More than ever, this is a vital task that we all need to master lest we get duped by the AI slop that's all around us.

With this caution juxtaposed with increasing evidence that the use of AI chatbots can impede learning and lead to "cognitive atrophy," I recently wrote enthusiastically about new-Luddism, a revivalist movement that's gaining steam based on old concerns about the perils of new technology. I'm encouraged to see that some are taking such concerns to heart.

Although an increasing number of students and teachers alike are starting to question the point of going to college due to rampant use of AI to complete assignments and pass tests, it was recently reported that undergraduates at Harvard have flocked to enroll in a notoriously "brutal" humanities course with a heavy reading burden of classic literature and philosophical texts.4 Undaunted, one student noted that "the only way to get anything out of this class is not to use AI."

It seems that some young people really do want to learn instead of succumbing to the temptation of "cognitive offloading," whereby we let chatbots do the work for us.

Sweden has already been aligning with such Luddite values for the past few years now by moving away from "digital learning," banning cellphones from classrooms and phasing out tablets and laptops in favor of "textbook-only teaching" and "pencil and paper learning."5 While critics—and predictably, technology companies—have argued that this could set students back by creating a "digital divide" that leaves them ill-prepared for the modern-day workforce, the backlash was sparked by the fact that test scores previously declined when digital learning was embraced.

Accordingly, some US schools have begun to follow suit. Relying on computers and AI chatbots may be useful for certain jobs in the future, but there's no good justification to focus on that at the expense of actual learning. Paraphrasing Yale computer science professor Lin Zhong, if we let AI do our jobs for us, then we set ourselves up for AI to take our jobs.But maybe there's hope for some of us yet. Ironically, it may turn out that it's those who embrace the old ways who will actually come out ahead.
