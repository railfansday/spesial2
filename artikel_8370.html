Highways In the Sky

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

At 8:00 am we started our hike today after a great night of rest. Ahead of us was a 13-mile gradual climb to Herrerias. Even though it is well into May, the morning air was still brisk and cold.

Leaving Villafranca del Bierzo, we crossed two bridges over distinct rivers tucked into a narrow mountain gap. Here, the trail splits into two paths:

Just past the two bridges, we stopped for breakfast at a cafe with great online reviews—a new habit I picked up after a disappointing grocery store run back in Molinaseca!

While enjoying a wonderful breakfast, we struck up a conversation with Xavier, a fellow pilgrim from San Francisco who is now living a nomadic life. As travelers often do, we spent time swapping stories and notes on life on the road before setting off again at 9:30 AM.

The trail started as a sidewalk along a quiet road. With light traffic, the walk was peaceful, accompanied by the loud, rushing waters of the river below. Though I was still dealing with a bloody nose—which was a bit scary—having seen the doctor earlier gave me peace of mind.

Looking back into the valley offered stunning final views of Villafranca del Bierzo. As we walked up the canyon, a short concrete barrier separated us from the road. Eventually, the main road entered a tunnel through the mountain, leaving our path on a quiet, barely-trafficked roadside directly beneath a soaring interstate viaduct.

With the interstate running on massive pillars far overhead, all we could hear was bird song and the rushing river below. It made for a surprisingly serene walk.

Entering the first town of Pereje, we passed a hillside cemetery with crypts built directly into the steep slope—there simply was no flat ground here. The town itself was tucked tight against the mountain. A small church retained its original bell tower despite being largely destroyed, and while a few buildings were occupied, most stood vacant and in disrepair.

A few miles back, I had picked a small bouquet of white flowers for Knockerz. As we walked, I passed an elderly Chinese woman who looked like she was having a tough time. We had shared a kitchen with her and her daughters several days prior, and I was so impressed to see her out here tackling the Camino. On impulse, I gave the flowers to her.

When we paused in Pereje to adjust Knockerz’s pack, the woman and her daughters caught up with us. The mother was still clutching the flowers. One of her daughters stopped to share how much that small gesture had brightened her mother’s Day.

She then translated a traditional Chinese idiom to describe Knockerz and me walking together:

When you travel and without understanding the local language, it’s easy to tune out written words and ambient chatter, missing so much around you. Walking down the smooth road today, I started using Google translate to read road signs and graffiti along the way—it opened up a whole new window into the local culture.

By 11:00 AM, a side road led us toward Trabadelo. The entrance was guarded by a magnificent grove of massive chestnut trees, some with trunks nearly 15 feet in diameter.

The town itself was another picturesque medieval mountain village. We hoped to grab a bite, but the local cafe was closed for Monday. On the Camino, you learn to never expect fixed schedules—places open and close for seemingly random reasons!

Leaving Trabadelo, we walked for a mile down an abandoned paved road beneath the shade of large trees merging back near the main highway. The canyon eventually opened up along the river near an interstate exit. The sudden drone of traffic, truck stops, and cars was a bit of a rude awakening, but it offered a welcome opportunity to grab food and caffeine.

Continuing up the canyon, we noticed the elevated interstate featured avalanche protection chutes—a stark reminder of how different this landscape must look in the dead of winter.

Arriving in Vega de Valcarce, the interstate viaduct soared hundreds of feet above us. I couldn’t help but look at a 400-year-old old stone house and wonder what its original builder would think of modern “highways in the sky.”

Vega del Valcarce was large enough to feature a small grocery store—our only shot at getting supplies for the day. We cobbled together a quick lunch of fruits, nuts, and yogurt and sat on a bench outside to eat.

While we ate, a lively local woman rode up on a bicycle with music blaring from a portable speaker. She stopped to offer us some gum, and thanks to Google Translate, we shared a brief, fun conversation. Before riding off, she struck up a quick dance right in front of uswith a huge smile on her face!

With just 2.5 miles left, the trail wound down a quite paved road through Ruitelan, running parallel to the rushing river while the bi-level interstate viaduct towered overhead.

To reach Herrerias, we dropped down off the main road into a narrow, peaceful valley. Down here the highway noise vanished entirely, replaced by the sound of a bubbling creek flowing through town.

While not a major tourist hub, Herrerias features a few welcoming  Albergues, restaurants, and a bar. We found an outdoor table facing the street to enjoy dinner while pilgrims and locals strolled by.

As the day ended, local life provided the evening’s entertainment: a young boy herding a dozen cows down the street was so absorbed in his smartphone that he didn’t notice a calf wander off into an open field. An older Spanish man spotted the escapee, gave the boy a gentle scolding in Spanish, and then looked over at us with a chuckle and a shake of his head.

It was the perfect ending to a truly unique day on the Camino.
