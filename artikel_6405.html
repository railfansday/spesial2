Ancient Indigenous practice may help clams survive ocean acidification

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Scientists found that adding crushed shells to clam beds may help young clams cope with ocean acidification, supporting a traditional Indigenous practice.

For a young clam, growing a shell takes energy. As the ocean absorbs more carbon dioxide and becomes more acidic, that job gets harder.

A new lab experiment suggests that mixing crushed shells into the sand can ease some of that strain. The practice has long been part of how some Indigenous communities care for clam beds.

Young clams in acidified seawater showed substantial changes in gene activity.

When researchers added crushed shells to their sand, those changes largely disappeared, and their gene activity looked much like that of clams in regular seawater.

Hannah Kempf led the study as a doctoral student in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences at the University of California, Davis. Her team raised the clams in buckets at Bodega Marine Laboratory for 90 days.

The ocean’s surface has become about 30% more acidic since the industrial era began.

As seawater absorbs carbon dioxide released by people, it loses some of the chemical building blocks that animals need to make shells.

That means clams, oysters and mussels must spend more energy building their shells. Acidic water can also dissolve them.

In the early 2000s, coastal acidification contributed to widespread losses at Pacific Northwest oyster hatcheries.

Scientists and hatchery workers tested ways to help, including adding soda ash to tanks holding oyster larvae.

Burrowing clams face the problem beneath the sand.

Pacific littleneck clams live about two to six inches (five to 15 centimeters) below the surface, where water between the sand grains, called pore water, is usually more acidic than the seawater above.

Along North America’s Northwest coast, some Indigenous communities have long cared for beaches as clam gardens.

That care can include mixing crushed shells, known as shell hash, into the sediment.

These gardens reflect traditional ecological knowledge built through generations of tending coastal habitats.

Near Bodega Bay, about 60 miles (97 kilometers) north of San Francisco, archaeological sites show that Coast Miwok communities managed clam beds.

Coast Miwok clam gardening ended in the 1930s under a federal policy based on the belief that harvesting would reduce clam numbers.

The restrictions instead led to smaller clam populations across the region, with clams nearly disappearing from some places.

The experiment began with 19 adult Pacific littleneck clams from Bodega Harbor.

They spawned overnight in the lab, and their offspring grew for 200 days before the test began.

Researchers divided the young clams between regular seawater and water made more acidic by adding carbon dioxide.

Within each water treatment, some clams lived in plain sand and others lived in sand mixed with crushed shells.

The team broke the shells with a mortar and pestle. Shell pieces made up 10% of the sand mixture by weight.

The acidified seawater averaged a pH of 7.3, compared with 7.8 in regular seawater. A lower pH means more acidic water.

Each week, researchers collected pore water from about three inches (eight centimeters) below the sand’s surface. In plain sand beneath regular seawater, this water became more acidic as the experiment continued.

Adding crushed shells helped keep the pore water’s acidity steadier. It also left that water less acidic than pore water in plain sand under the same seawater conditions.

The team also calculated how favorable the water was for forming aragonite, a mineral that clams use to build their shells.

Under acidified seawater, this measure stayed below 0.5 for most of the experiment in plain sand, while crushed shells kept it close to one.

Before the experiment, the clams spent a day in a dye that marked their shell edges and glowed under a special microscope.

At the end, researchers measured how much shell had grown beyond that line in 130 clams.

In acidified seawater, clams in plain sand added new shell equal to 4.7% of their length. Those living with crushed shells added 6.4%.

Under regular seawater, the two groups grew by similar amounts: 7.7% in plain sand and 7.9% with crushed shells.

Clams generally grew less in acidified water. Although those living with crushed shells showed greater growth, the difference was not statistically significant.

The genetic results revealed a clearer pattern than the growth measurements.

The team examined tissue from 24 clams and found 284 genes whose activity was at least four times higher or lower in one group than in another.

Clams raised in acidified water over plain sand had gene activity that stood apart from every other group.

Among the other three groups, no more than 11 of those 284 genes differed between any two of them.

So the clams living in acidified water over crushed shells had gene activity much like that of clams in regular seawater.

Researchers couldn’t match most of the 284 genes to a known job.

One that they could match resembles a gene for a protein that helps some mollusks control shell formation, and earlier research found that oysters need that protein to grow proper shells in acidified seawater.

The experiment used only two buckets for each condition, making differences harder to detect. The clams also began at different sizes, which the analysis did not account for.

The buckets contained clean quartz sand. Real beaches have microbes, mixed sediments and changing tides, and field studies have produced mixed results.

On a mudflat in the northeastern United States, an earlier study found that a shell-mineral reading around 0.4 killed many young hard clams. Adding crushed shells tripled their survival over 30 days.

But on a mudflat in New Brunswick, Canada, crushed shells raised the pH only slightly, and the effect disappeared after two months. The authors suggested that the approach may work best in small, local patches of habitat.

Study co-author Tsim Schneider, an associate professor of anthropology at UC Santa Cruz, described how this research could support communities.

“If there’s a history of adding shell hash to clam beds in California, for example, western science might be able to better support communities interested in reawakening dormant cultural knowledge and coastal stewardship practices,” said Schneider.

The team did not test the thin surface layer of sand where baby clams first settle.

The authors called for field studies of clam bed tending, designed for individual sites in partnership with Indigenous communities.

The study is published in the journal Biogeosciences.
