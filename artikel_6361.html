Scientists discover hidden genetic clues that may help explain autism

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Scientists have discovered overlooked DNA sequences that may help explain autism, revealing genetic changes missed by conventional testing.

Scientists have linked hundreds of genetic changes to autism, yet genetic testing can identify a cause in only about 15 percent of cases.

A new study suggests that some of the missing answers may be hiding in overlooked stretches of DNA.

Benjamin Blencowe of the University of Toronto‘s Donnelly Centre co-led the work with Quaid Morris of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. Blencowe also works at King’s College London and the Francis Crick Institute.

The results are computer predictions checked against thousands of family genomes, and there’s no clinical test yet.

A gene isn’t read straight through. Cells copy it into a working message, then cut out some pieces and keep others, so one gene can make slightly different proteins in different tissues.

Some of these retained pieces, called microexons, are only three to 27 DNA letters long. Despite their tiny size, they can influence how proteins function in the brain.

Neurons use microexons much more than other cells do, and the same ones show up across vertebrates. Blencowe’s lab had already found that the brains of autistic people often use them incorrectly.

Mice with only one working copy of the main switch for them show autism-like behavior.

What nobody had was a full list. Most earlier searches used whole brain tissue, which can miss a fragment that only certain kinds of neurons use.

Guillermo Parada, a postdoctoral fellow in Blencowe’s London group and co-lead author, built a tool that scans RNA data, the working copies of genes, for microexon-shaped pieces.

He ran it over more than 100 terabytes of public data. He also included data from single cell nuclei, so a piece that only one kind of neuron uses could still show up.

The scan covered 4,790 human tissue samples and 47,380 single nuclei from the cortex.

It turned up 1,514 human microexons that no catalog had listed, and 292 of them are used differently in nerve cells than elsewhere. A matching search in mice found another 1,115.

Many of the new pieces, more often than known ones, insert a stop signal that makes the cell destroy the message instead of altering a protein. That gives neurons a way to turn a gene down.

The next question was what tells a neuron to keep a microexon included when editing the message for making a protein.

Parada and co-lead author Hannes Bretschneider worked with Morris to train a machine learning model on about 1,800 known neuronal microexons and 91,920 look-alike stretches that cells don’t use.

It reads 300 letters on each side of a fragment and scores how likely a neuron is to use it.

The team then changed every letter in those windows on the computer and recorded how the score changed.

The positions that mattered most matched the DNA that evolution has kept the same across vertebrates.

“What surprised me was that the sequences the model identified as important closely matched those conserved throughout vertebrate evolution,” Parada said.

The model also flagged a short pattern just upstream of some fragments as a signal to leave the piece out. In mouse cells, lowering the protein that binds there, QKI, made those microexons show up more often.

With their model in place, the researchers could investigate how genetic changes found in autistic people might disrupt the way neurons use microexons.

Working with Stephen Scherer and Brett Trost of The Hospital for Sick Children, they applied it to whole genomes from three large collections.

Two of them together held 2,666 pairs of siblings in which one is autistic and the other isn’t.

Rare changes, those seen in under 1 percent of reference genomes, had larger predicted effects than common ones. The same subset of microexons turned up as affected in all three collections.

“Using our model, we were able to show that rare genetic variants generally have the largest predicted effects on the splicing of neuronal microexons,” Blencowe said.

The results showed that autistic siblings carried more predicted genetic disruptions than their non-autistic siblings.

The three collections together held 25,132 people from 7,391 families, and in that group 72 of the genetic variants were tied to an autism diagnosis.

They turned up in 2.5 percent of the autistic participants and in none of their unaffected siblings.

Although these variants appeared in only a small percentage of autistic participants, they offered potential explanations for cases in which previous genetic testing had found no cause.

Of the people carrying those variants, 82 percent had no previously identified genetic cause.

The affected genes cluster at synapses, the junctions where neurons pass signals, and several were already on autism gene lists.

Nearly all of this is prediction. The team tested a few of the flagged variants in lab-grown human neurons, and those did change how the microexon was used.

A flagged variant isn’t a diagnosis, either. More than a third of everyone in the study, autistic or not, carries at least one change predicted to affect a microexon. The ones tied to autism were the rare few with large predicted effects.

The model learned its rules from known microexons, and only 59 of the new human pieces followed them, so others may obey a code it doesn’t yet have.

And only 12 percent of the new brain-specific human pieces have a clear match in mice, which limits what mouse experiments can check.

How a disrupted microexon changes the proteins a neuron builds is the open question.

“In terms of future work, it will be important to understand how disrupted microexons affect interactions between proteins that are important for nervous system development and function,” Blencowe said.

The team has published its prediction for every variant it scored, so other groups can run new genomes against it.

That includes genomes from people with schizophrenia or epilepsy, where microexons may also be disrupted.

The findings suggest that some of autism’s missing genetic explanations may lie not within genes themselves, but in overlooked DNA sequences that help determine how those genes are used.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
