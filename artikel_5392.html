Measles symptoms to know, from the incubation period to the rash

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Measles symptoms follow a typical order, and these 10 signs and stages, from the first fever and cough to Koplik spots and the rash, show what to watch for

Measles hides in plain sight for its first few days. The opening signs — fever, a dry cough, a runny nose and red eyes — look a lot like a bad cold or the flu. The clues that point clearly to measles arrive later. By the time the rash shows up, an infected person may already have spent days around family, classmates or co-workers.

That timing is the main reason it helps to know measles symptoms in the order they tend to appear. The illness follows a fairly consistent sequence. Recognizing where someone sits in that sequence can help a parent or patient decide when to call a clinician and when to keep a sick person away from others.

The sequence begins with a silent stretch. Symptoms usually appear seven to 14 days after exposure, and the person feels fine while the virus replicates. Then comes the prodrome, the early phase before any rash. High fever, cough, congestion and irritated eyes show up together during this phase. Two to three days in, tiny white spots can form inside the mouth. Those spots, called Koplik spots, are one of the clearest signals that the illness is measles rather than a routine virus. The rash follows. It starts at the hairline and face, then works its way down the body before fading.

The 10 slides below walk through that progression one stage at a time. They cover the incubation period, the early respiratory and eye symptoms and the mouth spots. They also cover how the rash starts and spreads, how long a person can pass the virus on and which complications call for urgent care. Each slide stands on its own. A reader can jump straight to the stage that matters right now.

There is no specific antiviral cure for measles. Care is supportive.

Two doses of the MMR vaccine are about 97% effective against measles, according to the CDC. Children usually receive the first dose at 12 to 15 months of age and the second at four to six years.

The measles incubation period — the stretch between exposure to the virus and the first symptom — usually lasts about seven to 14 days. The full range runs from about seven to 21 days, and the person feels well throughout.

Nothing about this phase signals an infection. There is no fever, no cough and no rash. Daily life carries on as normal.

The virus is already replicating during these symptom-free days. A lack of symptoms during this stretch reflects timing, not an absence of infection.

The length of this window helps when someone tries to work out where an infection came from. A person who falls ill can count back across the range to estimate when the exposure likely happened. In most cases, that points to a contact one to two weeks earlier. At the outer edges of the range, the exposure could have happened as recently as one week before or as long as three weeks before.

That backward count has a practical limit. Three weeks is long enough for a brief encounter to slip from memory. A short visit, a shared ride or a crowded shop may not stand out by the time illness begins. Tracing the source can mean reviewing every place a person went across that whole stretch.

Fever is usually the first symptom of measles to appear. It often climbs above 101 degrees Fahrenheit (38.3 degrees Celsius) and can reach 104 F (40 C) or higher.

The fever signals that the incubation period is over. Before it arrives, a person who has caught measles generally feels well. Its arrival opens the prodrome, the early phase of the illness before any rash develops.

The fever does not break after a day or two. It tends to peak around the time the rash appears. The rash usually shows up three to five days after the first symptoms. The highest temperatures therefore often come several days into the illness rather than at its start.

That timing makes the fever a useful marker. A temperature still rising on the third, fourth or fifth day of illness fits the usual course of measles. So does a high point that arrives together with the rash.

A runny nose and nasal congestion often join the fever and cough in the first days of measles. Together, these symptoms can make early measles look like a bad cold or the flu.

The nasal symptoms belong to the prodrome. That is the early phase of the illness, before any rash appears.

At this point, a stuffy or dripping nose does not point to measles in particular. Plenty of common respiratory viruses cause the same complaints.

That overlap makes the first days of measles hard to identify. A person with congestion, a fever and a cough has no visible marker that separates measles from a seasonal cold or influenza.

The features that set measles apart arrive later. They include Koplik spots, which are tiny white or bluish-white spots inside the cheeks, and a red rash that begins at the hairline and face.

Koplik spots are tiny white or bluish-white spots on a red background, found on the inside of the cheeks near the molars. They are a hallmark sign of measles and usually show up one to two days before the rash.

The spots tend to appear two to three days after the first symptoms begin. That places them in the prodrome, the early phase of measles before any rash. By then, fever, a dry cough, a runny nose and red, watery eyes have usually started. The skin, however, still looks clear.

Their location makes them easy to miss. They sit on the lining of the cheeks next to the back teeth, not on the face or body. Seeing them requires looking directly inside the mouth. A quick glance at someone's face or throat will not reveal them.

The pale white or bluish-white color stands out against the reddened tissue around it. That contrast between pale spots and a red background is their defining look.

The measles rash moves in one direction, from the head toward the feet. It spreads from the hairline and face over the neck, trunk, arms, legs and feet, and it lasts about five to six days. It then fades in the same order it appeared.

The spots do not erupt across the whole body at once. They advance in stages. The face and neck are covered before the rash reaches the legs. The feet are among the last areas to show it.

The rash changes in appearance as it moves. Its first spots are flat and red. As more spots appear, neighboring ones can merge into larger, blotchy patches. Some spots also become raised rather than staying flat against the skin.

Fading follows the same route. The areas that broke out first are the first to clear. The face can lighten while the legs and feet still show spots. By the end, the rash has retreated from head to toe along the path it took on the way in.

Measles can lead to ear infections, diarrhea, pneumonia and, in rare cases, encephalitis, which is swelling of the brain. Anyone with measles who has trouble breathing, becomes confused, has a seizure or runs a very high fever that will not come down needs urgent medical care.

These complications sit on top of the usual course of the illness. The fever, cough, red eyes and rash can follow their typical pattern while a complication develops alongside them. Watching for warning signs does not end once the rash appears.

Pneumonia, an infection of the lungs, is the most common cause of measles death in young children. Breathing that becomes labored or difficult in a child with measles is a reason to seek care right away rather than wait to see whether it improves. Trouble breathing is a warning sign at any age, not only in children.

The same range applies in the other direction. Someone who knows they were near a person with measles cannot assume they escaped infection after a few symptom-free days. Feeling fine a week after the contact still falls inside the incubation period. Symptoms can begin as late as about 21 days after exposure.

The range also means that two people exposed on the same day may not fall ill together. One could develop symptoms after about a week, while the other stays well for close to three weeks.

The incubation period ends when the first symptoms appear. Those early signs mark the start of a separate phase of the illness. Until then, the infection gives no outward sign of its presence.

The fever also overlaps with Koplik spots, the tiny white spots that form inside the cheeks two to three days after the first symptoms. A rising fever paired with those spots points toward measles before any rash has begun.

A dry, persistent cough is one of the early signs of measles. It is part of the prodrome, the stretch of illness before the rash appears. The cough develops because the measles virus infects the respiratory tract. That makes the cough a direct signal of where the virus is active in the body during this early phase.

Prodrome is the medical term for the opening phase of a disease, when symptoms have started but the defining feature has not yet shown up. In measles, that feature is the rash. The rash follows the first symptoms by several days. Until then, nothing on the skin marks the illness as measles.

A dry cough brings up little or no mucus. That sets it apart from a wet, productive cough that brings up phlegm. In measles, the cough is also persistent, continuing through the prodrome rather than clearing quickly.

The cough does not arrive alone. It develops alongside a fever and a runny nose, and red, watery eyes often join them in the same phase. On its own, a cough reveals little about which virus is behind it. The combination is what starts to suggest measles.

Exposure history adds weight. A dry cough with a high fever and red eyes, in someone who may have been around a person with measles, fits the pattern of the measles prodrome.

Koplik spots can form inside the cheeks during this same phase, before the rash. A cough alongside those spots points more clearly to measles than the cough does alone. The spots are a hallmark sign of the disease.

The cough also matters for spread. Measles is airborne, and a person can pass it on before the rash appears. Someone with a cough and no rash yet may already be contagious.

A cough that turns into trouble breathing calls for urgent care. Pneumonia is one of the complications of measles.

Measles often turns the eyes red and watery through conjunctivitis. Many people with the infection also find bright light uncomfortable, a symptom called photophobia.

These eye changes belong to the prodrome, the early stretch of illness before any rash appears. They show up in the same period as the fever, the dry cough and the runny nose. Together, that cluster can pass for a heavy cold or a case of flu.

Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva. This is the thin, clear membrane that covers the white of the eye and lines the inside of the eyelids. When it becomes inflamed, the white of the eye turns red and the eyes produce extra tears.

Photophobia means light causes discomfort or pain. A person with measles may squint, keep their eyes partly closed or prefer a dim room. Daylight and bright screens can feel harsh during this phase.

Red eyes alone do not point to measles. What sets measles apart is the timing and the company the eye symptoms keep. Red, watery eyes that arrive alongside a high fever, a persistent cough and a runny nose form part of the early picture of the illness.

In the measles sequence, the eye symptoms come before the rash. That makes them one of the earlier clues that an illness resembling a cold may be measles.

Their value lies in timing. Koplik spots are a distinct feature of measles, and they come before the rash. They can point toward the illness while the skin still shows no change. For a parent watching a feverish child with a cough, they can be the first sign that sets measles apart from an ordinary respiratory bug.

The timing also carries a practical warning. A person with Koplik spots is already contagious, because measles spreads for several days before the rash appears. Spotting them is a cue to limit the sick person's contact with others.

The spots are short-lived. They fade once the rash is established. By the time the rash has fully developed, they may already be gone. A person whose illness is first noticed only after the rash has spread may never see them at all. Their absence at that stage does not rule out measles.

The measles rash usually appears three to five days after the first symptoms begin. It starts as flat red spots along the hairline and on the face.

The rash has a fixed point of origin. It shows up first where the hair meets the forehead and across the face. Only after that does it reach the neck, trunk, arms and legs.

At this early stage, the spots lie flat against the skin rather than rising above it. Their texture can change later. As the rash moves down the body, some spots merge into patches and some become raised.

Measles spreads through the air. It does not need direct contact to pass from one person to another. The virus can stay in a room for up to two hours after an infected person leaves.

That means someone who walks into a waiting room, classroom or office after a contagious person has left can still be exposed. The two people never need to share the space at the same time.

The risk to people nearby is high. About 90% of unvaccinated close contacts of an infected person become infected, according to the CDC. That figure describes people without vaccine protection, not the population as a whole.

The contagious window closes about four days after the rash first appears. The rash itself lasts about five to six days. A person can therefore still have visible spots after they are no longer able to spread the virus.

Ear infections and diarrhea also appear on the list of measles complications. Either one can develop in a person who is already coping with fever and rash.

Encephalitis does not happen often. Confusion and seizures, though, are both among the signs that need urgent care in a person with measles.

A high fever is part of a normal measles course. A raised temperature on its own is not the warning sign. The red flag is a very high fever that stays up and will not come down.

Some people face a higher risk of complications than others. The higher-risk groups are children under five, adults over 20, pregnant women and people with weakened immune systems. For a person in one of these groups, the warning signs above deserve an especially close watch.

Anyone with suspected measles should call a clinician before going in. A call ahead lets staff prepare and take steps to limit exposure to others in the waiting room or clinic.
