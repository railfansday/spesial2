Fed minutes, the biggest IPO ever, and new 401(k) limits: talking points for client calls.

By Amanda Kish, CFA, CFP® – Nov 20, 2026 at 12:55PM EST

This week the market ran into the AI trade's awkward phase, the part where the story is still great but nobody agrees on the price: the S&P 500 logged a sixth straight loss (its longest streak in two and a half years), Nvidia posted a blowout quarter and still sold off, and the Fed's October minutes made a December rate hike look like a formality. Then, on Thursday, the largest IPO in history hit the Nasdaq, and stocks sold off anyway.

Inside: what the hawkish Fed minutes mean for client conversations, equal-weight versus cap-weight S&P 500 funds after the Nvidia wobble, why Nvidia's strong quarter and higher outlook didn’t lift the stock, the IRS's 2027 retirement-plan limits, and a Thanksgiving-week calendar worth bookmarking.

Last week's inflation report raised the question; this week's FOMC minutes mostly answered it. The Fed is no longer asking whether to keep turning the thermostat down (it is raising rates), only how many degrees at a time. Here is the short version for the call.

The honest caveat: Not everyone expects a long campaign. Nuveen's fixed-income team, for one, still looks for just one more hike this year followed by an extended hold through 2027, and says markets are pricing a steeper path than that. Translation: the direction is clear, the destination is not, so plan for a range.

Client-ready line: "The Fed is still raising rates because the economy is holding up and prices are stubborn. We can't predict how far it goes, so we're planning for a range, not a forecast."

Tech's slide has put losses on the books in some taxable accounts, and there are six weeks left to use them. Realized losses offset realized gains dollar for dollar, then up to $3,000 of ordinary income, with the rest carried forward. The catch is the wash-sale rule: buying the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale disallows the loss, and that includes purchases in a spouse's account or an IRA. Pair this with last week's year-end distribution estimates, since a harvested loss can absorb a fund's capital gain payout. Try: "The recent drop gives us a chance to turn paper losses into a lower tax bill, and I'll make sure the replacement holding keeps you invested."

Nvidia answered last week's question and walked straight into a new one. In its first report since Jensen Huang's resignation, the company beat on revenue, earnings and guidance, then watched its shares jump after hours and flip to a loss. The stock closed down about 3.3% Thursday and is down more than a quarter this month, about $2.2 trillion below its October peak in market value.

Our read: the numbers back up last week's argument that the business is intact. Kress also said Nvidia's additional share repurchases now top $150 billion, and told CNBC the board has no plans to bring Huang back. But the stock was never trading on this quarter. It is trading on three open questions: who runs Nvidia permanently, how much margin gives way as volume climbs, and how long customers keep spending on AI at this pace. One analyst told Reuters the quarter was the kind Nvidia has delivered for years, which is exactly the point: a beat is the price of admission, not a catalyst. What to do with Nvidia shares still depends on position size and why a client owns them: a large position bought on conviction in one leader deserves a risk check, while a smaller position held for the products and AI demand has a thesis that this quarter supported.

Last week we noted that the IRS's 2027 inflation adjustments did not cover retirement-plan limits. On Nov. 12, they arrived in IRS Notice 2026-66: the 401(k) limit rises $1,000 to $25,500, while the IRA limit stays put at $7,500.

Sources: IRS news release IR-2026-136 and Notice 2026-66 (retirement limits); IRS Rev. Proc. 2026-24 as summarized by NFP and PLANSPONSOR (HSA).

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share on Nov. 18, above its $70-$78 target range, and began trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker ANT. The company and selling stockholders sold 980 million shares for $81.3 billion, topping SpaceX's $75 billion offering from June as the largest on record, at a fully diluted valuation of about $1.5 trillion. The stock opened at $111.50, 34% above the offer, and touched $115 before fading to close up roughly 15%-17% from the offer, around $97, even as the broader market fell.

Clients will ask, and some will have been tempted to chase it. A few points worth having ready:

The most a 401(k) participant aged 60 to 63 can defer in 2027: the new $25,500 limit plus the $11,750 SECURE 2.0 “super” catch-up.

Why it matters to advisors: Clients in the final stretch before retirement can use this window to catch up quickly, and many don't know it exists. For clients in this age range, check in to see if their plan allows the “super catch-up” and confirm how higher earners' catch-up dollars will be taxed. Try: "Between ages 60 and 63, you can save more in your 401(k) than at any other point in your career. Let's see what that would do for your retirement date."
