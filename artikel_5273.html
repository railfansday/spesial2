DeSantis says a Florida city misread his anti-DEI law. Now a nonprofit needs $130,000 to save its St. Patrick’s Day parade

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Delray Beach, Florida, had set aside about $77,000 for its St. Patrick’s Day parade, according to local reports. Now a local nonprofit says it will take $130,000 to run the event without the city, and its fundraiser has raised nearly $2,900.

The city has celebrated St. Patrick’s Day for more than 50 years with a parade of floats and marching bands that draw an average of 42,000 spectators, according to the city’s report for sponsors. But on Nov. 10, Delray Beach announced it’s canceling the 2027 parade along with its Pride event because of a new state law officials argue makes it illegal to celebrate a specific group of people—in this case, the Irish.

“Following a review of the legislation and its requirements, the City has determined that it is legally prohibited from organizing, funding, sponsoring, promoting, or marketing these two events in 2027,” a City of Delray Beach spokesperson told Fortune. “This decision is not budgetary in nature, nor is it the result of a local policy decision. It is a matter of compliance with state law, which applies to municipalities throughout Florida.”

The law is part of Florida’s broader crackdown on DEI—which has included restricting discussions of gender in public schools and ordering the removal of rainbow crosswalks—but its effect on Delray Beach shows how far its reach could extend: a measure targeting DEI is now being interpreted as a reason to cancel a celebration of Irish heritage that predates the DEI political debate. Now, the governor who signed it says Delray Beach has it wrong.

“Delray Beach is engaging in ‘malicious compliance,’” Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis posted on X. “What they are doing is not mandated by Florida law. It’s just an attempt to make a political point.”

Signed by DeSantis in April, the bill prohibits local governments from organizing or promoting “training, programming, or activities designed or implemented with reference to race, color, sex, ethnicity, gender identity, or sexual orientation,” and it will go into effect on Jan. 1, 2027. St. Patrick’s Day isn’t among the observances the law exempts.

“St. Patrick’s Day is not a federal holiday and, in this context, does not qualify for an applicable exemption,” the Delray Beach spokesperson said. “As a result, the annual parade is subject to the law’s restrictions.”

But the bill’s sponsor says that’s not what the bill was intended to do. “When the bill was on the Senate floor, the specific question was asked related to whether St. Patricks [sic] Day observances would be prohibited,” State Sen. Clay Yarborough wrote on X. “The answer was and still is no.”

Despite the political criticism, some local officials are open to other ways to make the parade happen. Republican Mayor Tom Carney told NPR, “It’s engrained in the DNA of Delray” and he would be open to private sponsors taking over to host the festivities. City Manager Terrence R. Moore is also open to this, per the Delray Beach spokesperson. She said the city has received an application from Delray Beach-based events company Festival Management Group to host the parade, but it’s still in the “very early stages of the city’s special events process.” Festival Management Group did not respond to Fortune’s requests for comment.

Going private will cost more than the city spent. Delray Beach had budgeted $77,000 for the parade, according to local reports. Now a nonprofit is trying to raise nearly twice that. Delray Beach Arts Inc. has applied to the city to produce the 59th Annual St. Patrick’s Day Parade on Saturday, March 13, joining Festival Management Group as a would-be host.

The group puts its projected production budget at approximately $130,000, covering public safety, traffic management, barricades, insurance, permits, entertainment, equipment, staffing, and professional event management. A GoFundMe for the effort had raised $2,885 as of Monday afternoon.

“While the City can no longer legally provide municipal funding or promotional support for these events, the legislation does not prohibit private organizations or individuals from independently sponsoring and organizing them,” the Delray Beach spokesperson said.

The city’s decision comes as Florida cities brace for a property tax cut that is already forcing some to drop events and services, Fortune reported.

The law also reaches private companies. Starting Jan. 1, any business seeking a contract or grant from a Florida city or county must certify that it does not and will not use county or municipal funds in requiring its employees, contractors, volunteers, vendors, or agents to ascribe to, study, or be instructed using materials relating to diversity, equity, and inclusion.

Delray Beach’s decision mirrors a retreat corporate America has been making for two years, as companies cut DEI programs and language under legal and political pressure. The stakes for the city’s officials are steep: under the law, an official who violates it commits misfeasance or malfeasance in office, and residents can sue their city.

Walmart became the largest company to pull back its DEI policies in late 2024, after Ford, Harley-Davidson, Lowe’s and Tractor Supply had already done so. Meta followed in January 2025, terminating its DEI programs, including those involved in hiring, training and choosing suppliers. After Target pulled back its DEI programs in January 2025, a boycott followed, and the company missed analysts’ sales expectations by nearly half a billion dollars, though analysts also blamed problems in its stores, and it seemed to recover foot traffic this past quarter.
