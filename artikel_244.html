Experts say ‘trusted messengers’ critical as Amish grapple with measles outbreak

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Unfolding crisis shows importance of ‘human connections’ over the long term – especially in under-vaccinated areas

Tristan Sharratt, a family nurse practitioner and public health researcher, began serving an Amish community in central New York almost inadvertently. A friend told an Amish neighbor that Sharratt was accepting new patients and was a good, solid guy. Word spread quickly, and now Sharratt sees many members of the community, which numbers in the hundreds.

He is what is called a “trusted messenger” – someone who understands a community and has earned their respect. Other trusted messengers can be neighbors, co-workers, customers, midwives, or people who work as long-distance drivers for the Amish. Connecting with such people will be crucial for ending the measles outbreak and preventing future crises.

“We can’t build trust with these communities just dropping in and being like, ‘Hey, there’s a measles outbreak. You should get vaccinated.’ Trust-building with these communities takes months to years,” Sharratt said. “This is a hard lesson and a tremendous opportunity to start building connections with these communities, because that is what it’s going to take to prevent the next situation like this.”

The unfolding measles outbreak, which has killed at least eight people in Pennsylvania, two in Wisconsin, one in New York, and one in Virginia, also demonstrates the importance of funding public health long-term – for everything from water fluoridation to road safety, said Sharratt, who is also a clinical lecturer of nursing and public health at Binghamton University.

“There’s just this enormous amount of work to be done,” he said.

Measles has raged through communities of Plain Anabaptists – including Amish and Mennonites – in this and previous outbreaks, and they are “emblematic” of many rural communities that have not been reached well by public health, Sharratt said.

“We haven’t invested in rural communities in a long time from a public health perspective,” Sharratt said, “and we’re really seeing the consequences with this outbreak.”

A central belief for the Amish is Gelassenheit, which essentially means “God’s will” – the idea that one’s life belongs to God; both joy and suffering are God’s will. “Part of my conversations with them is trying to determine why scientific advancements don’t fit into the perspective of God’s will,” Sharratt said. “Why is it not God’s will that scientists have developed the technology to [prevent measles]?”

Being unvaccinated in Plain Anabaptist communities is usually “an outcome of this whole idea of plain living”, Sharratt said. But the work of anti-vaccine activists accelerated alongside the Covid pandemic, and “unfortunately it has started to creep in” to Amish communities as well, Sharratt said. Non-Amish anti-vaccine groups such as Children’s Health Defense – then led by Robert F Kennedy Jr, now the secretary of the US Department of Health and Human Services – held events and wrote articles for Plain Anabaptist publications, overtly targeting the communities with vaccine misinformation.

Amish life centers around community, and so should efforts to reach them.

“Human connection really should be at the center,” Sharratt said. “You really just have to approach these people as people, build relationships as peers first, and then be there when they’re ready to talk to you or ask your opinion about these health issues.”

Inroads to Amish communities are frequently informal. “Maybe the associate public health director just happens to drive by the Amish farm every day and buy something from the vegetable stand – it can be as simple as that,” Sharratt said. Business owners and contractors can be a good way to connect.

“The Amish will work as subcontractors, and then all of a sudden you’re working next to Amos on the build site, and you’re there for eight hours together doing rough carpentry, and you have the chance to talk,” he said. “Maybe you go to an Amish family’s bake stand every Saturday to get the pie, and you just happen to run into them and build a relationship.”

There are local health departments that have done a great job of building connections – Sharratt pointed to the Otsego county health department, which developed a strong relationship with two Amish communities two decades ago during a pertussis outbreak; they continue to run vaccine clinics three to four times a year with those communities.

Often, however, health departments might know where a Plain Anabaptist community is, but they might not know how many people live there, how many children there are, or what the vaccination rate is.

“We have a huge data problem with these communities,” Sharratt said. “That requires going back to old methods of epidemiology, of community public health.” And public health at the national and state level hasn’t been as focused on community-building, he said.

Many Amish people want access to modern medicine when they need it – but access is frequently a challenge. Most Amish communities participate in some version of community insurance, where everyone chips in to cover healthcare costs. But typically providers accept conventional insurance; even those who negotiate lower prices with uninsured patients may be cost-prohibitive – especially as medicine is more corporatized and profit-driven, Sharratt said. That means communities often end up with providers who may not be as locked into conventional medicine.

It’s important to note that one Amish community is one Amish community; each makes unique decisions about healthcare, he said. “It’s not as simple as saying, ‘OK, here’s the strategy for all Amish communities in New York state.’ You need to approach every single one as a unique entity.” And there may be variability even within the communities, with some families seeking out vaccines despite anti-vax perspectives from community leaders.

A strong focus on community can also be an asset. “Once you convince [Amish] communities to adopt a certain intervention or vaccination or something, it’s likely that you will get buy-in from more than just a couple of families. The reward for the work is, I think, relatively higher than [other] rural communities,” Sharratt said. Infectious diseases may spread rapidly, but so does trust once it takes root.

“We can change it,” Sharratt said of the course of the outbreak. “We definitely can. It’s just going to take a change in how we’re approaching these things.”
