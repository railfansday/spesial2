8 steps the National Institute on Aging says may protect your brain as you age

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The National Institute on Aging lists 8 steps for brain health. See which have firm evidence and which are still only promising.

Many people think of memory loss in old age as fate, something that simply happens to them. Much of that danger can be changed, however. The 2024 update of the Lancet Commission counts 14 risk factors for dementia that people can change, according to TIME. With so many factors open to change, anyone who wants to protect their memory has real room to act. That room has limits, and even the researchers who study these risk factors avoid overselling them. Kristine Yaffe, director of the Center for Population Brain Health at the University of California, San Francisco, cautions that no one should believe the future of their brain rests on their own choices alone. The realistic goal of any habit is lower odds, not immunity. A person who develops dementia after years of careful living has not failed.

The habits that lower those odds do not carry equal weight, and the ones that feel most virtuous do not always have the strongest evidence behind them. Researchers learned much of what links daily habits to a sharper mind by following people over many years, not by testing a single habit directly. People who are healthier in general may stay sharper for reasons that have little to do with any one habit. The firmer evidence tends to involve conditions a doctor can measure and treat. Part of the work therefore happens in a doctor's office, not through willpower alone. For a reader with limited time and energy, it helps to know which habits have a proven payoff and which are only promising.

The National Institute on Aging, the federal agency focused on aging, set out to identify the habits older adults can adopt to protect their thinking skills and memory. These are steps an ordinary person can take at home or with a doctor. They range from the clinical, such as keeping blood pressure in check and understanding how medicines affect the brain, to the social, such as staying connected with other people. Diet, exercise and mental challenges round out the rest. Each slide that follows weighs one habit by how solid its evidence is and what a reader can realistically expect from it, sorting the proven from the merely promising.

Looking after the body is really several separate jobs, such as regular screenings, enough sleep and an end to cigarettes. The National Institute on Aging says that general body care, such as screenings, sleep and quitting smoking, may also benefit thinking. That word "may" offers a plausible benefit rather than a promise. The agency presents the advice as what people should generally do, not as a target tested in trials or fitted to any one patient. A reader still has to decide which of these jobs deserve limited time and energy first.

A hearing test may matter more for the brain than the habits people are most often lectured about. The National Institute on Aging names hearing loss, along with vision loss, among the conditions to look after. Hearing loss makes up 7% of the dementia risk that people can change, according to Lancet Commission figures reported by TIME. That makes a hearing check one of the more consequential of these appointments. Anyone who has put off a test because it felt cosmetic or inconvenient should move it near the top of the list.

Smoking and drinking matter less for brain risk in particular than their reputation suggests. The agency tells people to give up cigarettes and stay away from chewing tobacco, yet smoking accounts for only 2% of the dementia risk people can change, according to Lancet Commission figures reported by TIME. Heavy alcohol use makes up 1%, and that makes the advice to limit drinking sensible but not a major defense against dementia. None of this is a reason to keep smoking, which is estimated to kill about 480,000 Americans a year. Quitting pays off mainly by keeping a person alive and well, and any benefit to the brain comes on top of that.

Sleep is part of the agency's advice, but the major official reviews have not backed it as a way to prevent dementia. Sleep does not appear among the modifiable risk factors in the latest Lancet Commission update, and World Health Organization guidelines judge the evidence too thin to support recommending sleep programs to protect thinking, according to TIME. Some researchers read the evidence more warmly. "I think the evidence for sleep is actually quite strong," Dr. Kristine Yaffe, a professor and director of the Center for Population Brain Health at the University of California, San Francisco, told TIME. In her own 2026 paper, the tie to sleep is an association, not proof of cause, and that gap helps explain why the official bodies hold back.

Sleeping well is plausible and costs nothing, but a hearing test is the surer step.

Most advice about keeping a mind sharp stays vague, but managing blood pressure comes with a reading a person can check at every appointment. Adults 50 and older in the SPRINT MIND study who brought their systolic pressure, the top number, below 120 had a lower risk of mild cognitive impairment over five years, according to the National Institute on Aging. That benefit came within a few years rather than decades. The people who gained it were not young, and a reader past 50 has not missed the chance to benefit.

A person does not have to hit the strictest target to gain something, because ordinary treatment also appears to help. People treated for high blood pressure had a 12% lower risk of dementia when researchers combined six earlier studies, according to NIA, and those studies looked at blood pressure medicine of any kind, not the strict goal. A strict target tested directly and everyday care measured across many patients point the same way, which makes the case firmer than either line of evidence alone. The type of drug made no difference to the drop in risk, and the benefit appears to come from lowering the pressure itself. A patient and doctor can choose whichever medicine the patient tolerates and will keep taking.

Medicine also appears to close the gap with people who never had high pressure. Those who kept their high pressure under control with medication had the same dementia risk as people whose pressure was normal without any drugs, according to NIA. That result is strongly encouraging, though it shows an association rather than a guarantee.

The danger builds long before memory becomes a worry. High pressure in a person's 40s to early 60s is tied to later decline, which makes midlife the best time to act. The first step takes little effort: a yearly blood pressure check at a doctor's office. The SPRINT MIND benefit itself covers five years of treatment. Researchers do not yet know what years of rising or falling pressure mean for a person's odds of dementia, according to NIA. Blood pressure control works best as ongoing upkeep rather than a one-time fix.

Even with that question open, blood pressure is the rare brain habit a person can track with a number, and that makes it one of the easiest to start on.

The strongest reason to eat healthy foods is what they do for the rest of the body. The National Institute on Aging says that eating healthy foods can help lower the risk of chronic disease. Anyone who changes their meals for the sake of their brain is likely helping the rest of the body too. The diet designed for the brain also grew out of heart and blood vessel care. MIND blends two existing eating plans, the Mediterranean diet and DASH, whose initials stand for Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension. Researchers named the result the Mediterranean-Dash Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay, according to Loughborough University. The brain diet, in other words, is built on an eating plan meant to control blood pressure. A person who eats this way is working on blood pressure at the same time, so the effort counts even if the brain benefit is never proved.

Most of the support for the brain comes from watching people who already eat this way. The National Institute on Aging reports that observational studies link a Mediterranean diet to a lower risk of dementia. People who stick closely to such a diet may also live healthier lives in other ways, so part of that lower risk may have nothing to do with food. Results look smaller when researchers test a diet directly, and people in a trial of the MIND diet improved their thinking skills only slightly. A reader should expect a small benefit for the brain, not protection from dementia.

The useful advice is to follow the whole pattern rather than search for single "brain foods." Results for whole grains were surprisingly weak in a Framingham analysis, according to Loughborough University. The National Institute on Aging does not endorse any vitamin or supplement as a way to prevent mental decline, and its diet advice centers on meals rather than pills. "Still, despite these promising findings, results are not conclusive," the National Institute on Aging said.

Eat this way for the lower risk of chronic disease, which has the stronger support, and count any gain for the brain as possible but unproven.

Researchers have tested exercise directly, and they have not relied only on comparing active people with inactive ones. The National Institute on Aging reports that people who exercised in a trial ended up with a larger brain structure tied to memory. That result is evidence that the brain itself responds to movement. A larger memory region is not the same as protection from decline, however, and that step has not been shown. "Although these results are encouraging, more research is needed to determine what role exercise may play in preventing cognitive decline," the agency said. None of the trials of exercise and thinking skills in older adults enrolled people with diagnosed dementia, according to Frontiers in Public Health. Those trials cannot yet speak to stopping the disease. Exercise is a promising habit with a plausible way of working, not a proven defense against dementia.

The gains in older adults' thinking scores lean positive, but they rest on weak evidence. The certainty of the evidence is low on most of the thinking tests reviewed and moderate on just one, according to Frontiers in Public Health. Most of these findings could shift as more trials come in. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment, a widely used screening test, shows the same doubt. The average improvement on it looked sizable, but the range of likely results still included no benefit at all. A person who starts exercising for their brain can hope for modest gains but should not count on them.

No single kind of exercise has been shown to do the most for the brain, and the sensible choice is the one a person will keep doing. The best type changes with the test, Frontiers in Public Health reports. Mixed training ranked best on the Mini-Mental State Examination and stretching on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, though on the Mini-Mental State Examination no type clearly outperformed the others, so these rankings call for great caution. Aerobic training was the only type tested on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, a measure of flexible thinking. The researchers suggest sessions of 60 to 90 minutes, at least three times a week, for at least 12 weeks. They drew that schedule from trials set up in different ways and did not test it against other schedules. A reader should treat it as a starting target rather than a proven dose. The National Institute on Aging points to an activity guideline written for all adults, and that guideline is general health advice rather than a prescription for the brain.

The plainest brain benefit of staying active has nothing to do with test scores. The National Institute on Aging recommends fall-prevention programs, since falls can cause brain injuries.

Exercise is worth doing for the whole body, and any benefit to the brain is a bonus.

Keeping the mind busy is backed by an experiment, not only by observing people who happen to stay sharp. Older adults who trained their reasoning or their processing speed lost thinking ability more slowly across a decade in ACTIVE, a randomized, controlled trial, according to the National Institute on Aging. A third group in the trial practiced memory, AHRQ reports, but the slower decline came from the reasoning and speed exercises. "Keep your mind engaged" points toward certain kinds of mental effort more than it sets a blanket rule.

The decade-long result is the most impressive part of this evidence, and it is also the weakest. AHRQ reports that 57 percent of participants had left the trial by year 10, and it rates the evidence for the long-term results as low in strength. The people who stayed in the trial may differ from those who left, so the late comparison covers a much smaller group. A reader can hope for a lasting benefit from this training, but the trial does not settle that question.

The gains that did appear faded with time and stayed narrow. At five years, people who did speed training scored 240 points higher on a 1,500-point scale, and by year 10 the gain was 24 points, according to AHRQ. Some benefit may remain, but little is left of the earlier effect. Getting faster at a speed task also rarely made people better at other mental tasks, so the training improved one skill rather than the whole mind. The trial did not show whether the training keeps dementia away either. The evidence on dementia is rated insufficient, which leaves that question open.

Games that promise a sharper mind across the board are promising more than the training delivered. "Beware of claims that playing certain computer and online games can improve your memory and thinking," the National Institute on Aging warned. A better choice is an activity worth getting better at for its own sake. In one study the agency describes, people who did quilting or digital photography improved their memory more than people whose activities asked less of their minds.

Stay mentally busy because it costs little and may help, and treat any sharper thinking as a bonus rather than a guarantee.

Older adults who stay connected with other people tend to think more clearly, and those who feel lonely face a higher risk of dementia. Social engagement was linked to better thinking skills in more than 7,000 older adults, according to the National Institute on Aging. In research led by Florida State University, lonely people had a 31% higher risk of dementia, the National Institute on Aging reports, and that increase is close in size to the danger from an inactive life or from cigarettes. A person who would never shrug off a smoking habit has good reason to take loneliness just as seriously, rather than treating a social life as a pleasant extra in old age.

What seems to matter is feeling close to people, not keeping a full calendar. The agency suggests neighborly visits and volunteering as ways to stay engaged. The National Institute on Aging reports that being lonely differs from being isolated, and a person with plenty of company can still feel alone. Outings and meetings work best as a way to build real closeness, and someone who goes out every day but still feels distant from everyone may miss the purpose of the habit. Seen this way, staying connected is open to people who can't easily leave home. Social groups that meet online also count, so someone with limited mobility can still find close contact without a busy schedule in person.

The case for connection shows a link, not proof. The higher dementia risk among lonely people is an association rather than proof of cause, according to the National Institute on Aging. No one can yet promise that easing loneliness will keep dementia away, and the advice remains general rather than a prescription for a particular kind or amount of contact.

Staying connected is a promising habit, worth the effort for the odds it may improve, but not a guarantee against decline.

No one can practice this habit alone. It means getting treatment for illnesses that can harm the brain, so the work happens with a doctor. The National Institute on Aging identifies stroke, depression and delirium as conditions that endanger thinking skills. A loss of memory can therefore come from an illness that has a name and a treatment, not only from aging itself. Depression reaches beyond mood. People with depression can become confused or struggle to pay attention, and the agency links the condition to dementia. Low mood in an older relative is something to raise with a doctor, not a feeling to wait out. The practical habit is to stop dismissing new confusion or sadness as a normal part of getting older.

Delirium is a common event during a hospital stay, affecting around one in four older adults who are admitted, according to the University of Edinburgh. That makes one of the conditions the federal government names as a threat to the brain an ordinary event on hospital wards. Any family with an elderly parent in the hospital may face it, and that family can prepare before the parent is next admitted.

Whether treating these conditions protects the brain is still a hope, at least for delirium. The National Institute on Aging holds out the possibility that keeping such illnesses under control could slow or put off a loss of thinking skills. University of Edinburgh researchers, whose study appeared in The Lancet Healthy Longevity in 2026, say that treatments to reduce the long-term risk to thinking from delirium still need to be developed. Their study ties delirium to later dementia without showing that it causes the disease. No one can yet promise that preventing an episode spares the brain. Families can still act on an episode when it happens. "Delirium is an important warning sign for future brain health, underlining the need for routine delirium assessment in hospital and clear communication and follow-up after discharge," said Dr. Rose Penfold, a geriatric medicine doctor and research fellow at the University of Edinburgh's Usher Institute.

The step within a family's reach is to ask whether a hospitalized parent is being checked for delirium and to make sure any episode gets follow-up after the parent goes home. It is a firm step because it does not depend on a treatment that has not yet been developed.

One of the clearest risks to an aging brain is the medicine an older adult already takes. "Some medicines and combinations of medicines can cause confusion, memory loss, hallucinations, and delusions in older adults," the National Institute on Aging said. A side effect from a pill can therefore look like dementia itself. Several of the drugs the agency warns about are everyday purchases, such as antihistamines that people take to calm allergies and pills meant to help them fall asleep. Its warning also covers medicine for bladder control problems. An older adult who treats allergies, poor sleep and bladder trouble at the same time could be taking several of these drugs together without knowing it. Understanding these medicines starts with knowing exactly what is in the cabinet and why.

A prescription does not guarantee that anyone weighed its effect on a patient's thinking. NIH reports that in a study led by Dr. John N. Mafi of UCLA, 25% of dementia patients held a prescription for one or more risky drugs that act on the central nervous system, against 17% of people whose thinking was unimpaired. The patients whose thinking is most fragile are the ones most often given the kinds of drugs that can bring on confusion and memory loss. The check has to come from patients or caregivers asking questions, not from an assumption that the prescriber already did it.

Doctors prescribed these drugs less often over time, but the decline was slow. The share of older adults in the study who were prescribed risky drugs fell from about 20% to 16% between 2013 and 2021, according to NIH. The progress was also uneven, because other drug classes in the study were prescribed at lower rates that changed little over those years. A falling national rate gives no one reason to assume their own prescriptions have been reviewed.

The answer is a review with a doctor, not an emptied medicine cabinet. NIH reports that Dr. Annie Yang, the study's lead author, sees a place for these drugs in some cases and urges older adults and their caregivers to go over each one with their physicians. The hazard here is named and documented, and checking for it takes one conversation with a doctor. That makes it one of the most practical steps an older adult can take to protect the brain.
