NFL guarantees explained, from skill and injury protection to dead money

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Guaranteed money in NFL contracts comes in several forms, from signing bonuses to vesting guarantees, and this guide explains how each works and when teams can void it

A signing bonus is guaranteed money that an NFL player is owed for agreeing to a contract. For salary-cap purposes, the league does not count it all at once. The bonus is spread evenly over the years of the deal, up to a maximum of five years.

That even split is called proration. Each season covered by the deal carries an equal share of the bonus on the team's books. The share does not change based on how the player performs or how many games he plays. Because the shares are fixed at signing, a team knows the bonus charge for every season of the deal in advance.

The five-year ceiling shapes how longer contracts are counted. On a three-year deal, the bonus is divided into three equal parts. On a five-year deal, it is divided into five. On a deal that runs longer than five years, the bonus is still split over just five seasons. The seasons beyond that carry none of it.

A fully guaranteed contract protects a player's money against every standard reason a team might stop paying. Those reasons are release for performance, an injury suffered on the job and a cut made for salary-cap purposes.

When that protection is in place at signing, the team is committed to the full amount from the first day. No later date or condition has to pass before the money is secure.

Most NFL deals work differently. A typical contract guarantees only part of its value. The team can release the player and owe nothing beyond that part. Some contracts also carry one type of guarantee without the others. A player might be covered if he is hurt on the job but not if he is cut for performance.

A fully guaranteed contract closes those gaps. Releasing the player does not erase what he is owed. Poor play, an injury or a tight salary cap does not change the total either.

Dead money is the salary cap charge a team still carries for a player who is no longer on its roster. It is made up of guaranteed money and unamortized bonus money that remain on the cap after a release or trade.

Bonus money turns into dead money because of a gap between when it is paid and when it is counted. The player collects the bonus, but the team books its cap charge in yearly installments. If he leaves before every installment has been counted, the remaining ones do not vanish. They still have to land on the cap.

Guaranteed salary follows the same logic. If a contract protects a player's pay for a future season and the team cuts him, the club still owes that money. The amount counts against the cap even though the player will never take another snap for the team.

Void years are placeholder seasons added to the end of an NFL contract for one reason: to stretch how a bonus is counted against the salary cap. The player is not expected to play them.

The tool builds on signing bonus accounting. A bonus is charged to the cap in equal shares across a contract's years, with proration capped at five years.

Void years work inside that rule. Each added season counts as a contract year for proration purposes. More years on paper means the bonus is divided into more shares. Each share is smaller. The bonus charge in every season the player actually plays shrinks as a result.

That is the point of the structure. A team carries a lower cap figure while the player is on its roster. The rest of the cost is pushed into seasons when he will not be there.

An NFL team can generally seek to void guarantees or recover bonus money when a player breaches his contract. Failing to report, retiring and being suspended are among the breaches that can open that door.

A guarantee is a promise from the team, but it rests on the player meeting his own obligations under the deal. When a player stops meeting them, the team can argue that its side of the promise no longer holds.

That is why a guarantee is not unconditional. Its protections cover a release for performance, injury or salary-cap reasons. A breach by the player is a separate matter.

Failing to report is one example. A player who holds out from training camp or skips mandatory team activities is not providing the services his contract calls for. That absence can give the team grounds to challenge money it agreed to guarantee.

An NFL contract has two numbers that matter. One is the total value announced on signing day. The other is the portion a player is certain to collect. Those figures are often far apart. The gap between them shapes how teams build rosters, manage the salary cap and decide when to move on from a player.

Most NFL contracts are not guaranteed in full. A team can release a player and owe him nothing beyond the guaranteed portion of his deal. That makes guaranteed money in NFL contracts the clearer measure of what a deal is worth to the player. It is also the clearer measure of how much risk a team has taken on.

Guarantees are not a single category. Some money is protected only if a player is injured on the job. Some is protected against a release for performance reasons or for salary-cap reasons. Some is protected against all of those at once. That is what "fully guaranteed" means. Money can also change status over time. It can start with limited protection and become fully guaranteed on a set date.

The signing bonus sits at the center of many deals. It is guaranteed to the player. The team, however, spreads its cap charge evenly across the contract, up to a maximum of five years. That accounting choice shapes much of what happens later. It feeds into dead money, the cap charges that remain after a player is released or traded. It also explains why teams use the post-June 1 designation. And it explains why some contracts include void years that a player is not expected to play.

Guarantees also have limits. Offset language can reduce what a former team owes once a released player signs elsewhere. A team can also try to void guarantees or recover bonus money when a player breaches his contract. Failing to report, retiring and being suspended are examples. Whether that effort succeeds depends on the contract's language and the collective bargaining agreement between the league and the players' union. Sometimes it depends on an arbitrator.

Most of the money in an NFL contract is not guaranteed. A team can release a player and owe him nothing beyond the guaranteed portion of his deal, however many years or dollars remain on paper.

That makes the announced value a best-case figure rather than a promise. A deal is usually reported with a length and a total. That total assumes the player stays on the roster for every season of the contract.

If the team cuts him early, the unguaranteed salary in the remaining years is never paid. The player keeps only what the team committed to in advance. Everything above that amount depends on the club's ongoing decision to keep employing him.

The guaranteed figure is the part of the deal a player can count on. It is owed whether or not he finishes the contract. The unguaranteed remainder works more like a series of future choices the team can make or decline.

Guaranteed money can take more than one form. A signing bonus is guaranteed to the player. Salary for a given season can also be guaranteed, either fully or only against specific risks such as injury.

This explains why two contracts with similar totals can mean very different things for the people who sign them. One deal may protect a large share of its value. Another may protect only a small slice, leaving most of the reported total tied to future roster decisions.

The split matters to teams as well. Unguaranteed money disappears when a player is released. The guaranteed portion is the commitment a club cannot walk away from. Releasing a player saves a team only the unguaranteed part of his remaining pay.

The guaranteed amount, not the total, shows how secure a player's pay is. How much is guaranteed, in what form and from which date are the details that decide what a player collects.

Proration is an accounting rule, not a payment schedule. It governs how the bonus counts against the cap. It does not determine when the money reaches the player. The timing of the actual payments is a separate matter set by the contract.

That separation lets a team commit a large sum to a player while counting only a fraction of it against the cap in any single season. The bonus can keep appearing on the cap sheet in seasons after the cash has already been paid.

For the player, the value lies in the guarantee. A signing bonus belongs to the protected part of a deal, not the part a team can walk away from by releasing him.

That sets it apart from much of a contract's headline value. Salary scheduled for later seasons may never be paid if the player is released first. A release does not cancel the signing bonus.

The size of the bonus also affects how much of a deal is protected. A contract built around a large signing bonus and modest yearly salaries puts more of its value into guaranteed money. A deal with a small bonus leaves more of its value exposed to an early release.

NFL guarantees come in three main types, and each covers a different reason a team might cut a player. A skill guarantee protects against release for performance, an injury guarantee protects a player hurt on the job, and a cap guarantee protects a player released for salary-cap reasons.

The skill guarantee covers a judgment about ability. If a club releases a player because it decides he is no longer good enough, a skill guarantee means it still owes the protected amount. The decision to move on does not cancel the obligation.

The injury guarantee is tied to a specific event. It applies when a player is hurt while performing his job for the team. If that injury leads to his release, the guaranteed salary remains payable. It does not shield him from a release driven by declining play or by budget pressure.

The cap guarantee covers a third scenario. Teams operate under a salary cap, and a club sometimes cuts a player to clear room for other spending. A cap guarantee means that reason alone does not let the team walk away from the protected money.

These categories are separate promises. A contract can include any combination of the three. Some deals protect money against only one risk, while others stack two or all three on the same salary.

A single guaranteed total does not show which protection applies to each dollar. Money shielded only against injury gives a healthy player little security if his performance slips. Money with only a cap guarantee does not help him if the team cuts him for poor play.

Each protection is defined by the reason for ending the deal. That makes the reason for a release central to what the player collects. Two players cut on the same day for the same reason can leave with very different sums, depending on which protections their contracts carry.

Reading guarantee language comes down to two questions: how much money is protected, and against which of the three risks.

Kirk Cousins signed one of these deals with the Minnesota Vikings in 2018. It was a three-year contract worth $84 million, and the entire amount was fully guaranteed.

Deshaun Watson's 2022 contract with the Cleveland Browns followed the same structure on a larger scale. It covered five years and $230 million, all of it fully guaranteed.

Full guarantees also appear at the start of some careers. First-round rookie contracts are typically fully guaranteed. A team that drafts a player in the first round is usually committed to his whole rookie deal from the day he signs it.

Players drafted in later rounds face a different arrangement. Their contracts more often guarantee only the signing bonus. The rest of their salary is not protected. A team can release them and owe nothing beyond that bonus.

The split between those two groups ties financial security to draft position. A first-round pick usually holds a fully guaranteed deal from signing. A later-round pick often keeps each season's salary only by staying on the roster.

A vesting date is the point at which a limited guarantee becomes a full one. Some contract guarantees protect a salary against injury only at first, then become fully guaranteed on a set date, usually tied to the start of the league year.

Before that date, the protection is narrow. An injury-only guarantee covers a player who gets hurt on the job. It does not cover a release for performance or for salary-cap reasons. A healthy player cut ahead of the vesting date can leave without that money.

After the date passes, the same salary is protected against all three of those risks. The team owes it whether the player is cut for skill, for injury or to clear cap space. The dollar amount does not change on the vesting date. What changes is the set of circumstances in which the team must pay it.

That structure turns the vesting date into a decision point. A team that wants to move on from a player has a reason to act before the guarantee locks in. A team that keeps the player past that date has, in effect, committed to the salary. For the player, the date marks when an expected payment becomes one he can count on.

The fifth-year option on a first-round pick follows this pattern. When a team exercises the option, the salary for that extra season is guaranteed for injury only. It becomes fully guaranteed on the first day of the league year. Until then, the team keeps some flexibility over a player it has already chosen to retain.

The franchise tag works differently. There is no waiting period tied to the calendar. Franchise-tag salary becomes fully guaranteed once the player signs the tender. The trigger is the player's signature, not a date on the league schedule.

Vesting language also explains why a guaranteed total announced at signing can overstate what is secure on day one. That figure may include money protected only against injury. Checking when each portion vests shows how much is secure immediately and how much becomes secure later.

Non-guaranteed salary works differently. A released player's unprotected base salary comes off the books entirely, and it never becomes dead money. Only protected or already-paid money can leave a charge behind.

A trade can leave dead money behind as well. The original team has already paid the signing bonus. The share of that bonus not yet counted stays on its cap after the player moves to his new club.

This math shapes roster decisions. A release frees only the difference between what the player would have counted and the dead money he leaves behind. When the dead charge is large, a cut can save little cap room that season. In some cases it uses more cap space than keeping the player would have.

Dead money cannot be shifted to another team or wiped off the books. It occupies cap space the team cannot spend on anyone else. A club carrying heavy dead charges has less room to sign free agents or extend its own players.

Timing matters too. A separate tool, the post-June 1 designation, changes how the charge is divided across seasons.

An NFL team can designate up to two player releases as post-June 1 cuts. That label spreads the dead money from each of those releases across two seasons instead of loading it into one.

Dead money is the cap charge a team still carries after a player leaves through a release or trade. It can include guaranteed salary the team still owes and bonus money it has not yet counted against the cap. The player is off the roster, but the charge remains.

The designation changes timing, not size. A released player is still owed every guaranteed dollar his contract promises. The label does not cut his pay, cancel a bonus or reduce what the team must pay him. It moves part of the cap accounting from one season into the next.

The salary cap limits what a team can spend on player pay in a season. A large dead-money charge in one year uses cap space that could otherwise go to other players. Splitting the charge across two seasons lowers the burden in the first one.

The cost is deferred, not avoided. Whatever leaves the first season lands in the second. A team that uses the designation accepts a smaller dead-money charge now in exchange for an added charge the following year.

Bonus proration explains why the split can be large. A signing bonus is guaranteed money for the player, but its cap cost is counted in pieces over the life of the deal. A player released early in a long contract can leave several seasons of uncounted bonus behind. The designation lets a team divide that leftover amount between two seasons rather than absorbing it at once.

The limit of two designations forces a choice. A team weighing several releases must decide which ones gain the most from the split. Releases that leave behind the largest dead-money charges free the most room in the first season.

The five-year ceiling still limits the math. Void years can stretch proration only as far as that ceiling allows. A deal that already runs five real seasons gains no extra bonus spreading from added years.

The deferred charge comes due when the contract voids. At that point, the bonus shares assigned to the placeholder seasons have not yet counted. They accelerate onto the cap together rather than spreading over the years they were assigned to.

The same acceleration applies if the team releases or trades the player before the contract reaches its void date. Any bonus shares not yet counted, including those tied to void years, stay on the cap as dead money.

Void years do not change what the player is owed. The signing bonus is guaranteed money either way. The added seasons alter only when its cap charge is recorded, not whether the player collects it.

A void year also differs from an ordinary final season, which a player is under contract to play. It exists on paper for cap accounting, not for games.

Offset language lets a team that releases a player subtract whatever he earns from a new team from the guaranteed money it still owes him. The clause turns the old team's obligation into a backstop rather than a separate paycheck.

The clause only matters after a release. Offset language applies to the guaranteed money a team still owes at that point. It does not create a guarantee, and it does not erase one. It changes how much of the remaining guarantee the former team ends up paying.

The mechanism is subtraction. The former team starts with the guaranteed amount left on the contract. It then deducts what the player earns from his new club. The former team pays whatever is left over.

The effect depends on the size of the new contract. A player who signs a small deal elsewhere reduces the old team's bill only slightly. A player whose new earnings match or exceed the remaining guarantee can reduce that bill to nothing. In the first case, his combined pay from the two clubs equals the guarantee he was already owed. In the second, it equals his new salary.

Without an offset clause, the arithmetic runs the other way. The former team pays the full remaining guarantee regardless of what happens next. The player keeps that money and also collects whatever his new team pays him. That outcome is commonly called double-dipping.

The presence or absence of an offset therefore changes what a guarantee is worth in practice. Two contracts with identical guaranteed totals can produce different payouts after a release. The deciding factor is whether the former club is allowed to count the player's new earnings against what it owes.

Offsets also shape the money a released player can expect from his next deal. With an offset in place, the guarantee from his old team already sets a floor on his income. A new contract below that floor shifts who pays him, not how much he receives.

Retirement is a different kind of breach. Here the player chooses to stop playing before his contract runs out. A team in that position can seek to void the guarantees that remain. It can also try to recover signing bonus money it has already paid.

Suspension is a third trigger. A suspended player is unavailable to his team for the games he misses. That absence can give the team grounds to seek to void guarantees.

None of these outcomes is automatic. Whether a team can actually void a guarantee or recoup a bonus turns on three factors. The first is the specific language in the player's contract. The second is the collective bargaining agreement between the NFL and the NFL Players Association. The third, in some disputes, is the ruling of an arbitrator.

Contract language matters because guarantees are negotiated deal by deal. One contract may spell out exactly which conduct cancels a guarantee. Another may be written more narrowly, leaving the team fewer grounds to act.

The collective bargaining agreement sets rules that individual contracts must follow. It shapes what a team may pursue against a player and how disagreements over money are handled.

When the team and the player read those documents differently, the dispute can go to arbitration. An arbitrator then decides whether a breach occurred. The arbitrator also decides what money, if any, the team can withhold or claim back.
