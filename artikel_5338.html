Nvidia degrades U.S. national security as Chinese military snags its chips

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

When Super Micro compliance auditors visited a Southeast Asian customer in August 2025 to inspect thousands of servers packed with Nvidia chips, the warehouse floor looked in order. According to federal prosecutors, the scene was pure theater. The servers on hand were nonworking dummies staged for the audit; the real ones had already gone to China. When Commerce Department inspectors came later, conspirators set up the same fakes again, using a hair dryer to swap labels and serial stickers. Prosecutors say at least $510 million in servers went to China over a few weeks in spring 2025 alone.

Fifteen months later, the country is once again being asked to take tidy paperwork at face value. In less than two weeks, Nvidia’s leadership turmoil swung through an astonishing reversal. Jensen Huang abruptly resigned as chief executive on November 12, and five days later the company’s chairman declared that the board “does not intend to reinstate” him. Come Monday, directors brought him back anyway, citing assurances from senior U.S. officials and concluding that his return “poses no national security risk.” President Donald Trump claimed credit for talking them into it, telling CNBC, “It’s a threat to lose him.”

Official endorsements cannot wish away the serious security record piling up in the public view. Set aside, for a moment, the scrutiny swirling around Megaspeed, the firm tied to Huang’s paramour Alice Huang, as well as the question of whether she acted as a spy. Even apart from that controversy, a largely overlooked trove of documentation proves beyond dispute that restricted and banned Nvidia chips have been illegally shipped into China, where the Chinese military has openly recorded buying elite processors to bolster capabilities aimed against the United States.

Congressional investigators, state attorneys general, and the broader national security community will inevitably unravel what transpired with Megaspeed and Alice Huang. As they pursue that thread, however, they cannot lose sight of the larger pipeline operating right under Washington’s nose. Smuggled American chips are already strengthening the Chinese Communist Party’s military power and domestic repression, fueling weapons research, design, and manufacturing alongside electronic warfare and intelligence collection. Before anyone treats Huang’s return as case closed, investigators must confront the pressing, unanswered questions still hanging over Nvidia.

Consider some of the military-linked recipients already plainly visible in public records. A Reuters investigation published November 11 found that Northwestern Polytechnical University, a military-linked institution on the U.S. export blacklist since 2001 over alleged missile proliferation, recorded an October purchase of two H200 chips through a Chinese supplier. Its cybersecurity school also contracted in January for access to two servers containing eight H200s apiece.

Some advocates point out that the H200 is an older and less sensitive chip, but the administration’s January H200 licensing relaxation explicitly did not erase restrictions on blacklisted entities. Indeed, its safeguards expressly excluded military and military-intelligence users and prohibited remote access for specified restricted parties. The university’s disturbing procurement records therefore demand a detailed explanation: what authorization, if any, covered these supplies, and which intermediary checked the actual beneficiary? Did the U.S. government authorize these sales (it would be ludicrously stupid if so), or did Nvidia fail in its responsibility to monitor its own supply chain and maintain awareness of end customers?

The Harbin Institute of Technology offers another disturbing example. When Commerce blacklisted it in 2020, the department specifically cited efforts to acquire American technology for Chinese missile programs. Yet Reuters reported this March that Harbin’s July 2025 procurement notice described a Super Micro system containing eight Nvidia A100s. Beihang University, another blacklisted institution with military ties, recorded purchasing a workstation with four A100s in March 2026.

At a minimum, all of this points to persistent demand from the Chinese military and institutions linked to it — the very institutions the controls were designed to exclude and the very institutions that want to hurt U.S. national security the most. Who is dropping the ball here?

If the Chinese military has its way, more banned Nvidia chips will soon be on their way. WireScreen reviewed more than 3,800 Chinese military and government procurement records. The units seeking Nvidia hardware include the Rocket Force, which operates China’s land-based nuclear missiles, the Cyberspace Force, and the equipment arm of the Communist Party’s Central Military Commission. One Cyberspace Force tender for A100-class servers named a password-cracking tool as the workload. Georgetown researchers found a PLA lab requesting 16 H100s and another unit seeking a DeepSeek-linked cyber range for “intelligent attack.”  That should scare any and every American.

It is not just the Chinese military obtaining older, still-restricted Nvidia chips. There is also ample evidence that the most cutting-edge, most sensitive chips—such as the Blackwell chip, which is entirely banned from being sold into China—are still making their way into China anyway, and worse, directly into the hands of the CCP. Last month, the AI security firm Lava reported finding 32 NVIDIA Blackwell Ultra B300 GPUs operating on Chinese networks—specifically linked to state-owned China Telecom—during a study of more than 12,000 GPUs, despite a ban on Blackwell chips going to China. That comes on top of the fact that even a senior administration official says DeepSeek trained a model on banned Blackwell chips, likely in Inner Mongolia. Congressional investigators should demand answers about how these Blackwell chips are getting into China and whether Nvidia is asleep at the switch on the most basic KYC duties—know your customer.

Nvidia surely must know about the transshipment of its sensitive chips into China – as has already been proven in the public record, with a long trail of criminal indictments. Alan Hao Hsu and his company, Hao Global, pleaded guilty in October 2025. According to the Justice Department, the scheme involved at least $160 million in exported and attempted exports of restricted Nvidia H100 and H200 processors. Shipping documents concealed the goods and recipients; more than $50 million in funding originated in China. The company’s plea agreement identifies transshipment through Singapore and Malaysia. This is admitted criminal conduct involving falsified documentation and illegal exports through transshipments, yet what did Nvidia do about the facilitators of that transshipment who were still getting chips from Nvidia?

Similarly, in March, federal prosecutors alleged that defendants connected to Super Micro arranged purchases through a Southeast Asian intermediary and diverted at least $510 million in servers to China within weeks in spring 2025, then used the dummy servers described at the outset to cover their tracks. In October, broker Ting-Wei Sun pleaded guilty to four counts, including export-control conspiracy and obstruction. Similarly, Taiwan has indicted a senior Nvidia manager as the central figure in faking a compliance audit so that 74 servers of top-line B300 chips could reach China. Given these apparent leaks and holes across Nvidia’s supply chain, any reasonable observer would conclude the company needs to monitor its own customers and employees far more carefully.

Whenever Washington weighs a firmer line on chips, the same warning reliably surfaces. Push Beijing too hard, skeptics caution, and China will choke off the rare earth magnets powering everything from fighter jets to electric motors. That anxiety is already dictating policy. After the administration let Nvidia resume H20 sales to China last year, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick was candid about the bargain, explaining, “We put that in the trade deal with the magnets.” Export controls once considered untouchable have stayed on the bargaining table ever since.

Beijing’s current leverage is undeniable. According to the International Energy Agency, China mines roughly 60% of the world’s magnet rare earths, refines around 90%, and makes nearly 95% of finished permanent magnets. Its grip tightens further on heavy rare earths like dysprosium and terbium, which keep magnets working at high temperatures inside military hardware and electric vehicle motors. Benchmark Mineral Intelligence found that as recently as 2024, the West depended on China for 99% of those heavy minerals. Nor has Beijing hesitated to flex that advantage. Export controls imposed in April 2025 on seven heavy rare earths remain in force. In June, China blacklisted two American firms championed by federal policymakers, MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, and this month it extended a pause on broader restrictions by just two months.

Their dominance, however, is a product of policy rather than geology, and it can erode far faster than conventional wisdom expects. Rare earths are not actually rare. All 17 occur outside China, and the U.S. Geological Survey estimates that more than 40 percent of reported global reserves—and an even larger share of identified deposits—lie beyond China’s borders, including 11 million metric tons in Brazil alone, along with major deposits across Australia, Canada, Greenland, Tanzania, and the United States. As late as the 1980s, the world’s leading producer was not in Asia at all, but a single mine in California’s Mojave Desert called Mountain Pass. China assembled its commanding lead in roughly twenty years.

Japan has already demonstrated how swiftly the balance can shift. After Beijing halted exports to Japan in 2010, Tokyo bankrolled Australia’s Lynas and cut China’s share of Japanese rare earth imports from over 90% to roughly 60% within a decade. Lynas has since become the first producer outside China capable of separating both dysprosium and terbium.

The true bottleneck is not ore, but processing capacity and permits, both of which can be solved in years instead of decades. While the IEA estimates that meeting demand for magnet rare earths outside China will require roughly $60 billion over the next ten years, the figure is a modest fraction of the $1.2 trillion in U.S. economic activity Bloomberg calculates depends on these materials. Washington has begun laying the groundwork, pairing a Pentagon stake and price floor for MP Materials with the $12 billion Project Vault stockpile and $565 million for Brazil’s Serra Verde, which supplies those same vital heavy rare earths.

Permitting remains the stubborn obstacle. S&P Global calculates that taking a new American mine from discovery to production averages 29 years, slower than every country on earth except Zambia and almost ten years behind Australia. Delays on such a scale turn responsible oversight into outright paralysis. Clearing the logjam demands an “Operation Warp Speed” for critical minerals, built on hard permitting deadlines, one lead agency per project, guaranteed offtake for allied producers stretching from Brazil and Canada to Africa, and strict environmental standards enforced upfront without endless litigation. Smart environmental policy is essential, yet reflexive obstruction simply ships both the mining and the pollution straight to China.

Beijing’s rare earth card is a wasting asset, provided Washington chooses to make it one. The answer is to build, not to bend, and certainly not to keep weakening the export controls standing between America’s best technology and the People’s Liberation Army.

Congress holds subpoena power, and they ought to wield it to get to the bottom of how the Chinese military is obtaining banned Nvidia chips. Nvidia’s shipment ledger for every GPU sent to Southeast Asia since October 2022 is fair game and in the company’s possession, as are any location data its fleet-management software has collected, its customer files and communication with US regulators, including the Department of Commerce.

State attorneys general are paving the way, as California Attorney General Rob Bonta has already subpoenaed Nvidia on how chips reached China and whether investors were misled.

Export controls were meant to keep America’s best chips out of China’s war machine, yet the evidence shows that Nvidia’s most sensitive chips are already fueling the Chinese military in its quest to degrade US national security. The only crime greater than smuggling these chips into the hands of the Chinese military would be our neglect in letting them get away with it.

As the line often attributed to Lenin goes, “Capitalists will sell you the rope used to hang them.”
