CachyOS November 2026 release

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

CachyOS, a performance-focused distribution based on Arch Linux, has announced its November 2026 release. Notable changes in this release include a new recovery environment ("cachyos-recovery") for rolling back broken systems, Easy Anti-Cheat (EAC) v2 support for games that require EAC to run, version 2.0.0 of the CachyOS Package Installer, and more.

I think that is because for quite some games, a well-designed anti-cheat actually adds value. For public competitive games, cheating is a real problem, and can easily make the difference between a fun game, and a frustrating experience. This is different than with videos etc. – I don’t have any benefit from them being DRM’d vs free. The best case is, I don’t notice much difference to the unlocked video, and from there it only gets worse – so the unlocked version is always better. With games, it is more ambivalent – without a working anti-cheat, certain games definitely get worse, so it is easier to convey a need for strong anti-cheat measurements to the end users. > they're trying to best-effort solve a theoretically impossible problem I think you are technically correct here (the best kind of correct), but that also misses the point a bit; especially for games. No mitigation or security measurement is perfect; it is always about making things more expensive for the attacker – expensive not only as in money, but also time and knowledge for example. And the harder it is to circumvent an anti-cheat trivially/unnoticed, the less people will cheat, and the more people will have a better game experience.

In the DRM case, your computer is siding with the music/movie/media industry and against you, because it's in their interest to not allow you to make copies. In the gaming case, your computer is siding with the "community" (as represented by the game vendor), because you might want to have it help you cheat, and they'd rather it didn't. In the gaming case, it also exposes a shortcoming of the game architecture, where either by necessity or lack of care, computations of interest to the overall community are pushed out to individual clients, instead of taking place on a centralized server under the control of said "community" and its representatives. Anyway, TL;DR, commercial interests find general purpose computing to be a bit of a nuisance, and "open source" "developers", nowadays mostly employed by big corporations, are happily playing along in the name of "security"... The future used to be a lot brighter in the 80s... :(

Uh, "aiming" and other competitive aspects are by their very nature up to the individual client. You are not entitled to play a multiplayer game (especially one hosted on someone else's computer) under your own rules; all participants have to agree on the rules, and to adhere to them, as a condition of participation. This is no different from any other formalized human competition. > In the gaming case, your computer is siding with the "community" (as represented by the game vendor), because you might want to have it help you cheat, and they'd rather it didn't.
