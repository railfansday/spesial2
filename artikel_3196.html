Meta’s ‘Project Gryffindor’ used schools to grow Instagram, NPR reports

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Internal Meta decks pitched Instagram’s school programme as a way to fuel teen sign-ups. Meta says NPR’s story on Project Gryffindor mischaracterises a safety tool for schools.

Meta planned to use schools to sign up more teenagers for Instagram, internal documents show. It called the plan “Project Gryffindor”. NPR’s Katie McQue reported the documents on Wednesday. Lawyers obtained them in discovery in the US lawsuits that accuse social media companies of harming young users.

Publicly, Meta presents its School Partnership Program as a safety tool. It lets educators report bullying and safety concerns, and lets schools post notices to students and parents. Meta launched it in 2025 and expanded it in September, when it said almost 17,000 schools had signed up.

Internal presentations framed school networks as a way to grow Instagram, according to NPR. A 2024 deck on Project Gryffindor named user “acquisition”, “friending” and “invites” as goals.

“We believe network connections will fuel organic and unattributed acquisition,” the presentation states.

A 2023 presentation said the programme would “give teens a new reason to join” Instagram. It also questioned Meta’s reliance on its most active teen users to bring their friends in.

“Our current strategy of relying on power user teens to get less active friends onto IG may not be effective if there are full communities of teens that have not yet joined IG,” it stated.

Features include school banners on teen profiles and a directory where students can find classmates who use the app. Meta also sent push notifications to students at specific schools. It called them “school blasts”, according to a legal brief filed for school districts.

The aim was to “tip schools from inactive to active via network effects”, the brief says.

One of the documents NPR used is a court exhibit filed in the case in January.

Meta spokesperson Liza Crenshaw rejected NPR’s account.

“This story completely mischaracterizes a positive program that helps thousands of U.S. schools protect their students online, communicate with them safely about extracurricular activities, and easily report safety issues,” Crenshaw said in a statement to NPR.

In the past year, Meta reviewed over 90% of bullying reports from participating schools within 48 hours, she said. Parents with Teen Accounts can block their children’s access during the school day, she added. Meta did not respond to NPR on sextortion and security concerns.

Online safety experts told NPR the programme could make teens easier targets for predators and scammers. Cybersecurity investigator Paul Raffile said sextortion scammers try to learn where a victim goes to school. They later threaten to send images to classmates and teachers.

“Once an account is embedded within a school and has an active school profile, they can see all sorts of things like directories of the different clubs, different groups on campus, and that’s a sextortionist’s dream,” Raffile said.

Meta also described the programme as developed “in partnership” with the education nonprofit ISTE+ASCD. The group told NPR it only did contract work on the reporting tool, which ended in 2025.

The documents surface after a run of legal losses for Meta over young users. In August, Meta agreed to a settlement worth up to $17.1bn with dozens of state attorneys general. It denied their claims. Co-defendant Snap settled a teen case in November, days before trial.
