The Skirted Furniture Trend Is Taking Over Homes Again, but Designers Say This Is the Right Way to Do It

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Frills and flounce are suddenly all the rage.

Pictured above: This primary suite, designed by Elliott Interiors, features skirted furniture that acts as both a playful design detail and a unifying layout element.

Sofas, sinks, and settees: What do they all have in common? They look great in a skirt. A hallmark of ‘80s and ‘90s decor and the traditional English country-house aesthetic, skirted furniture is back. We’ve been quietly watching the trend evolve, with designers pleating, draping, and dressing everything from bed frames to breakfast banquettes.

“With the rise of traditional design becoming more widely accepted, especially by younger generations, details like skirted sinks, skirted dining chairs, and sofas can be seen everywhere,” says Claire Staszak of Centered by Design. “I think because skirted furniture harkens back to something that feels authentic, aged, and charming, it really appeals to me as an aesthetic choice.”

As design moves out of its minimalist era, skirted furniture is here to bring whimsy back. “I think people are having more fun with their homes right now, and adding a skirt to something is a pretty easy upgrade to accomplish,” says Bridget Tiek, principal designer at the Louisiana-based design firm, TIEK BYDAY. “Existing furniture pieces or even architectural items, sinks, cabinets, can be transformed.”

This feeling of transformation is what interior designer Heather French believes is driving this romantic detail back into the modern-day trend cycle. “I think people are craving softness in their lives. Hard edges and echoing rooms don’t give you that, but fabric does. It quiets a room and makes it feel lived-in and welcoming.”

Before you get a little too skirt-happy, though, make sure you’re using them intentionally—it’s easy to go overboard with the flounce. Here’s how designers recommend using furniture skirts throughout the home.

You may love the idea of a furniture skirt, but the wrong fabric (and the way it’s gathered) can ruin it. Always consider your fabric’s colors, lines, and finish. In the above space, Staszak used a pleated skirt to bring character and French-country flair to the scullery of a revamped 1896 Victorian.

“You should also consider when a gathered skirt, straight skirt, pleated skirt, or ruffled skirt is the right look for your interior,” says Staszak. “For instance, a straight skirt can look very chic in a more contemporary setting, whereas a ruffled skirt is much more appropriate for a French or country aesthetic.”

Consider the rest of your home’s aesthetic to guide the best style of skirt to use.

“I’ve used it in traditional cabins and in contemporary homes, and it’s always the application that makes it work. A crisp box pleat reads very differently from a relaxed gathered skirt,” says French.

“For us, it’s all about casual luxury and being thoughtful about where furniture legs actually belong. I don’t like an overly leggy room,” says French.

French and her husband brought softness to this stone-walled New Mexico bathroom using rounded edges, plush fabrics, and a powdery blue hue.

Sorry, maximalists. This is one furniture style that is best used in a restrained manner.

“You probably only need one skirted item per room! Especially if you are going bold with pattern,” says Tiek.

Even the most delicate details can pack a statement. Tiek and her team used burgundy gingham to add color, contrast, depth, and an air of playfulness to the above breakfast nook.

Ditsy florals, gingham, and tartan have been flooding our feeds lately—and these classic patterns make the perfect choice for furniture skirts.

This laundry room by The Misfit House features a sink skirt in a bold coffee-hued gingham, which nicely contrasts the clean lines of the subway tile walls and complements the hues in the splattered tile.

“With all the options for pattern right now in textiles, the skirt offers a great opportunity to showcase fabric in a different way from traditional drapes or window treatments. Because skirts are often gathered, they look good with fabrics that have smaller repeats and more of an all-over pattern,” Staszak explains.

While we love a good DIY, designers say that furniture skirts look their best with impeccable tailoring—so if you don’t have the know-how to make your own, opt for a professional’s touch.

“Have the skirting and any hardware professionally done,” French advises. “DIY versions can easily look sloppy. The more tailored the skirt, the more intentional it looks, and that’s what makes it feel like a design choice rather than a cover-up.”

For instance, this daybed, tucked into a Martha’s Vineyard dressing room by the team at Sees Design, blends in seamlessly because of its perfect seams.
