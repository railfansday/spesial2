Why High Self-Esteem Doesn't Guarantee Self-Acceptance

Can you feel good about yourself and still struggle to accept yourself? Yes. Self-esteem and self-acceptance are related, but they aren’t the same. Understanding the difference may help explain why someone who seems confident can still feel utterly shaken by a setback—and why unconditional self-acceptance can be a steadier foundation.

Western societies pay lots of attention to self-esteem. People seek therapy to raise it, parents worry about harming their children’s self-esteem, and partners may blame relationship problems on low self-esteem. A healthy self-esteem can benefit mental health. One meta-analysis of 77 studies on depression and 18 on anxiety found that higher self-esteem predicted less subsequent depression and anxiety.

But self-esteem has a less familiar cousin: Self-acceptance. If self-esteem is a positive evaluation of your worth, self-acceptance means accepting yourself as a whole person, including your strengths, weaknesses, and feelings. The late psychologist Albert Ellis coined the term unconditional self-acceptance, or USA, to mean accepting yourself without making your worth depend on success, failure, imperfections, or other people’s approval.

Research suggests that self-esteem and USA overlap but are not identical. In one study, USA was associated with less anxiety, better moods after setbacks, and lower narcissism, even after accounting for self-esteem, suggesting that self-esteem and USA might reflect different pathways to psychological health.

If the two attributes are related but distinct, what might different combinations look like? Imagine a matrix with self-esteem on one axis and unconditional self-acceptance on the other.

The grounded self has high self-esteem and high self-acceptance. The imperfectionist has low self-esteem but high USA: They may have a poor evaluation of themselves, yet accept themselves as a person. The self-critic has low levels of both. But it's the conditional achiever, who has high self-esteem but low USA, that fascinates me most.

Wait—how can someone feel good about themselves but struggle to accept themselves? Two patterns may help explain this.

Pursuing healthy self-esteem is worthwhile. But I’d argue that embracing unconditional self-acceptance matters even more.

First, raising your self-esteem can be difficult. If you believe a lot has gone wrong in your life, you may feel like you have compelling evidence for a negative view of yourself. That view can change, but it could take time. Learning to accept your imperfections, failures, and weaknesses may be a more manageable starting point than trying to feel good about yourself.

Second, life is uncertain. Career success, physical appearance, and relationships can change. People lose jobs, retire, age, and sometimes lose a partner. Accepting yourself without conditions gives you a steadier point of reference through those changes—a north star that helps you find your way when life shifts.

Third, paradoxically, unconditional self-acceptance could make self-improvement less threatening. If failure feels like proof that you’re worthless, it makes sense to avoid trying. But when a setback doesn’t determine your worth, you can pursue a goal without living under constant threat. Accepting a weakness doesn’t mean refusing to change it; it can actually make change feel safer.

Here are seven ways to begin practicing unconditional self-acceptance.

Develop a mantra that captures unconditional self-acceptance. Below is one I’ve developed. If it speaks to you, feel free to use or adapt it. Say it to yourself every morning. Let the words soak into your mind before beginning each day:

I am human. I am imperfect. I am whole. I accept my past. I accept my flaws. I accept myself.

Practicing unconditional self-acceptance can be liberating. It replaces the endless task of proving you’re impressive enough with something steadier: improving what you can, accepting what you can’t change, and not using either as a yardstick to measure whether you’re worthy. That’s a vision of a life worth embracing.
