Netflix’s New KPop Demon Hunters Replacement Is an Instant Fantasy Classic

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Steps turns a tired trope into something worth watching

Fairy tale revisionism has been a popular storytelling trope to unpack for a while now. What if Maleficent from Sleeping Beauty was a victim of oppression? What if Peter Pan was queer and in love with Captain Hook? What if fairy-tale heroes and heroines were just stand-ins for celebrity culture? What if the Wicked Witch of the West was good, actually?

A quarter-century after Shrek first married the princess and turned the entire genre on its head, these subverted fairytales have become about as commonplace as the original stories that inspired them. So when Netflix announced a new animated movie told from the perspectives of Cinderella's Ugly Stepsisters, it wasn't exactly an inspired choice. Disney already did that two decades ago with Cinderella II: Dreams Come True (2002) and Cinderella III: A Twist in Time (2007), with a live-action film following the unlikable duo set for release in the future.

With that in mind, I was ready to dismiss Steps, from directors Alyce Tzue (Soar) and John Ripa (Raya and the Last Dragon), as yet another revisionist fairy tale swimming in Shrek's muddy wake. But after actually watching the movie, I came away impressed. Steps brings together the best of Frozen and KPop Demon Hunters, while making the Cinderella tale its own in a way we haven't seen before with one clever twist. In Steps, the villain isn't Cinderella's evil stepfamily, it's conformity.

The idea that Frozen is a movie about sisterhood has always rankled me. While Elsa and Anna are certainly sisters, that relationship almost immediately falls by the wayside in favor of Anna’s love life. By the time they finally reunite, what's supposed to be the movie's emotional climax didn’t move me an inch.

Steps easily avoids this pitfall. The film follows Lilith (Ali Wong) and Margot (Stephanie Hsu), the infamous, ugly stepsisters of beautiful Cinderella (Amanda Seyfried). While Steps gives the sisters’ rivalry its due, their first introductions are saccharine-sweet. As two families become one, the girls find genuine camaraderie and affection in one another. This sharply contrasts with the original tale, in which the stepsisters' ugliness stems from both their appearance and their cruel treatment of Cinderella. And even when Steps gives the love story between Cinderella and the Prince (Daniel Radcliffe) more room to shine, it still pales in comparison to the screen time given to the topsy-turvy relationship among Lilith, Margot, and Cinderella.

Tzue and Ripa also cleverly shift the tale’s focus from the stepsisters' superficial appearance to how Lilith and Margot’s East Asian heritage makes them stand out in a Western world, both in how they look and in the clothes they wear and the food they eat. While Cinderella and her friends play with pristine dolls and teacups, Lilith and Margot are drawn to Eastern tales and a geisha doll with a snake for a body.

Tzue and Ripa also use their fairy tale setting, the kingdom of Alouette, to emphasize the differences between Lilith and Margot, and their stepsister, Cinderella. This world is practically besieged by an 18th-century French rococo aesthetic. The wood of the buildings looks like chocolate, the floors like flowers. It all perfectly matches the fluffy, sweet design of a fairy-tale storybook.

Alouette’s design immediately makes Lilith, Margot, and their dressmaker mother, Li Mei (Gwendoline Yeo), stand out for their East Asian style. At first, this isn’t a problem. Li Mei’s husband, Edmund (Matt Lowe), and his daughter, Cinderella, deeply love their new family. Two delightful scenes — the family sitting down for dinner and the girls playing together — show two very different cultures coming together.

Unfortunately, domestic bliss isn’t meant to last. In a brutal scene that shows how early prejudice against minorities can begin, an insecure young girl named Priscilla (Nikki Glaser) grows jealous when her friends and Cinderella admire Lilith and Margot’s differences. She tricks the stepsisters into entering a storm drain and locks them in. When they call out to Cinderella for help, she succumbs to peer pressure instead and abandons them.

Naturally, Lilith takes this to heart. In a sequence eerily reminiscent of Megamind’s villain montage, she decides that if the people of Alouette want her to be bad, then bad she will be. In a kingdom as colorful as Alouette, this means Lilith heading down to her local shopping mall and picking out the grungiest goth fashion you can think of.

From then on, the Cinderella tale continues as usual — up until Lilith gets her hands on a magical wand and everything starts to go very wrong.

The star-studded cast of Steps works hard to give depth to characters who, for all intents and purposes, were completely shallow in their original tales. Wong is known for her sharpness and her comedy. She meshes well with this more modern take on the ugly stepsister, but she’s just as capable of hitting you where it hurts when the story calls for it. Her anger at being unaccepted and belittled by the people around her, while Cinderella only has to exist to be loved, is heartbreaking to watch.

Yet what surprises me most about Steps is how daringly Tzue and Ripa craft a story that warns against conformity. The closest Steps comes to addressing — at least in dialogue — that Lilith, Margot, and their mother experience racism is a brief meta sequence in which Lilith asks why she and her sister are white in modern adaptations of Cinderella. (“Creative license!” Bette Midler as the Fairy Godmother responds.) Even so, the point doesn’t need to be stated outright. It’s obvious to any viewer that Lilith, Margot, and their mother experience prejudice throughout the story.

Steps isn’t perfect. At times, its dialogue is so aggressively modern that it feels out of touch. It did get a few laughs out of me (the reason why the Prince needs to test people’s feet instead of simply remembering Cinderella’s face is pure genius), but the quippy dialogue keeps the humor from fully landing. Despite these few quibbles, it’s hard to deny that Tzue and Ripa have done something special with their take on Cinderella, transforming the tired trope of fairy-tale revisionism into one of the most daring and innovative adaptations I’ve seen in a long time.

Steps releases for streaming on Netflix on Nov. 20.
