The Hunger Games Meets The Walking Dead In This 40-Episode Sci-Fi Thriller Series

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

On paper, The Hunger Games and The Walking Dead are two distinct franchises. But picture a dystopian world where the apocalypse is not ruled by zombies but by monstrous humans themselves. Even the games they play among themselves to increase their class divides are more political and terrifying. There lies the premise of a compelling 40-episode sci-fi thriller.

Loosely based on the French graphic novel Le Transperceneige and Bong Joon Ho's movie of the same name, Snowpiercer has a distinct post-apocalyptic setup. Some shows, like The Walking Dead, are open world survival thrillers. Others, like Silo, unfold in underground bunkers. Snowpiercer primarily takes place in a titular train that endlessly circles Earth.

Finding shelter in a constantly moving train does not sound too bad when the planet outside is frozen beyond repair. In Snowpiercer's world, however, the man-made divides and horrors inside the train are far more disturbing than the thought of freezing to death. Owing to this, it seems fair to compare Snowpiercer with both The Hunger Games and The Walking Dead.

Panem, in The Hunger Games, weaponizes spectacle and controlled scarcity to exploit the lower classes. They quietly aggravate the existing divides between oppressed districts while ensuring the Capitol remains safe and sound. A similar system can be noticed in Snowpiercer. Just like Panem's districts in The Hunger Games are numbered based on economics, humanity is divided through a train's cars in Snowpiercer.

While the elite accommodate the front cars of the trains and enjoy every luxury, the ones at the back rot in inhumane conditions. Much like President Snow in The Hunger Games, the train's creator, Wilford, also has a cult-like following where he is almost treated like an overarching deity. Similar to Jennifer Lawrence's Katniss Everdeen, Snowpiercer also finds its reluctant rebel in Andre Layton.

He emerges from the depths of the lower classes but, like Katniss, realizes that overthrowing a tyrant is far easier than leading a deeply divided population without becoming a tyrant yourself.

Similar to the characters in The Walking Dead, the ones in Snowpiecer, too, gradually experience moral erosion in their post-apocalyptic world. Alliances keep shifting, and, instead of uniting against one common evil, the people get lost in their own petty conflicts and selfish desire to survive. Despite these overlaps, though, Snowpiercer finds its distinct voice through its detective noir drama.

Overall, Bong Joon Ho's Snowpiercer is an absolute masterpiece. The show does not even come close to being as gripping and intense as the award-winning film. However, the series still makes good use of its long runtime to expand the world-building from the movie. While the movie quickly moves from one car to another, the show carefully reveals how everything from water supply to food production works.

Instead of jumping from one action set piece to another, the show paces itself and introduces gripping mysteries to keep viewers hooked. The movie is star-studded, with Chris Evans, Tilda Swinton, Ed Harris, Song Kang-ho, Jamie Bell, and Octavia Spencer serving as its leads. Even the show, however, boasts memorable performances from Jennifer Connelly, Daveed Diggs, Rowan Blanchard, and Sean Bean.

All in all, the Snowpiercer show never comes off as a forced extension of the original movie. It also does not try too hard to draw any connections to the film, which helps it etch its own solid identity like The Hunger Games and The Walking Dead.

Snowpiercer is set aboard a perpetually moving train that carries the last survivors of humanity after Earth is frozen into an uninhabitable wasteland. As passengers remain divided by a rigid class system, former detective Andre Layton is drawn into a murder investigation that exposes growing unrest and ignites a struggle over who should control the future of the train.
