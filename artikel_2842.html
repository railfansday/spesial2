Yankees Birthday of the Day: Giancarlo Stanton

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

On most days in our series profiling one Yankee per day, the player in question has seen his Yankees career come to a close. Thousands of people have donned the pinstripes, and in most cases, that period of their lives has come to an end, allowing us time to look back and reflect on their careers.

Today, we celebrate the birthday of one Giancarlo Stanton, a man whose Yankees career has not yet finished. But Stanton's Yankee tenure has already stretched nine years, with just one guaranteed year left on his contract. The bulk of his Yankees story has been written. It's a story with more ups and downs than I think any one of us expected, and as he stares down what could be the final chapter of his career, his Yankees legacy stands as one of the strangest among the many greats that have played for the team.

Giancarlo Cruz-Michael StantonBorn: November 8, 1989 (Panorama City, CA)Yankees Tenure: 2018-present

Giancarlo Cruz-Michael Stanton was born in 1989 to Mike Stanton and Jacinta Garay in Panorama City, California, a Los Angeles neighborhood that was also home to former Yankee Zack Britton. Stanton grew up a Dodgers fan and became a multi-sport star Notre Dame High School in Sherman Oaks, excelling at football, basketball, and of course baseball.

Born Giancarlo, Stanton preferred to be called Mike for part of his childhood. "Why did you name me [Giancarlo]?" he asked his mother in high school. However he was called, Stanton was a prodigious athlete, and received scholarship offers from UCLA and UNLV, ultimately accepting a scholarship from USC to play baseball.

He would never make it to campus, as the Florida Marlins took Stanton in the second round of the 2007 MLB Draft, and Stanton decided to forgo his scholarship and accept a $475K signing bonus from the Fish. Stanton would quickly make a name for himself as a prospect, crushing 39 homers in the minors in 2008 and rocketing towards the top of industry prospect lists, thanks to his sterling performance and undeniable physical tools.

By 2010, Stanton ranked among the top five prospects in baseball per Baseball America and Baseball Prospectus, with scouts lauding not just his immense raw power, but also his impressive mobility for a player his size, not to mention a cannon arm that helped make him a high-quality defensive right fielder. After smashing 21 homers in just two months at Triple-A, the Marlins called the 20-year-old Stanton up in June, and he quickly established himself as one of the most exciting young talents in the National League, hitting a grand slam at home against the Rays for his first career home run.

Stanton would hit 22 homers and run a 118 OPS+ as part of a strong rookie campaign in Florida. In his first full season in 2011, Stanton hit 34 bombs with a 141 OPS+ across 150 games, kicking off a stretch in which Stanton was solidified as one of the game's premier sluggers. Between 2011 and 2016, Stanton hit 186 homers, finished runner-up to Clayton Kershaw for the 2014 NL MVP, and posted a 145 OPS+, all while continuing to play a strong right field. His level of play was so stellar and consistent that he convinced the notoriously stingy Marlins to pay up, inking Stanton to a 13-year, $325-million contract extension after the 2014 season that was at that point the richest contract signed in the history of American sports. Along the way, he took a trip to Europe and decided to embrace his old name, and started to go by Giancarlo once more.

And it turns out, all this was just an appetizer for Stanton's historic 2017 campaign. Playing in a famously pitcher-friendly home park in Miami, Stanton made what was at the time the most spirited run at a 60-homer season in the post-steroid era, ultimately finishing with 59 dingers in a season that earned him the NL MVP award. Only Barry Bonds, Mark McGwire, Sammy Sosa, Aaron Judge, Roger Maris, Cal Raleigh, and Babe Ruth have ever hit more in a single season.

Stanton was at the peak of his powers, and that's when New York came calling to see if he'd want to use those powers in service of an actual pennant chase. From the moment he signed his historic deal with the Marlins, those on the outside always wondered if it was a contract meant to be traded.

Indeed, with Stanton on top of the world and in MVP form, the Marlins' new ownership group—which included former Yankees star Derek Jeter—took the opportunity to cash out, sending Stanton (and $25 million) to the Yankees for the modest return of Starlin Castro and prospects Jose Devers and Jorge Guzmán.

It was a thunderous move, the Yankees seizing the chance to pair Judge, coming off a historic season of his own, with an equally powerful running mate. With the Bombers riding high after a surprising 2017 season, the possibilities seemed endless as Stanton came to town. The Yankees had two MVP-caliber bats in the middle of their order, a bedrock of young talent, a deep farm system and even deeper pockets. Stanton's arrival signaled that the time was now, for the Yankees to re-cement themselves as baseball's Evil Empire, to establish a new reign of dominance over the league.

On Opening Day, Stanton smashed two homers as the Yankees won in Toronto. The next week, Stanton, Judge, and Gary Sánchez homered in a 7-2 win over the Rays at Yankee Stadium. The new era truly had begun, the first of what would seemingly be endless nights where the Yankees' power trio would all homer and lead the team to victory.

But no sooner did the era begin that it started to end. Judge was hit by a pitch in July and missed two months, only returning at the end of the season. Sánchez, the club's fabulously talented catcher, struggled through an injury-plagued season and hit .186. Stanton was left to carry the lineup, and he did a fine, if unspectacular, job of it. Though he didn't reach the heights of his 2017 MVP campaign, Stanton provided reliable production while the sluggers around him were struck down, hitting .266/.343/.509 with 38 homers and 100 RBI. The Yankees survived the injuries to Judge and others and won 100 games for the first time since 2009 thanks in part to Stanton's steady presence, though their postseason run ended in a disappointing ALDS loss to the Red Sox.

Stanton and the Yankees returned in 2019, looking forward to a healthier year from their stars that would allow their sluggers to click at the same time. Little did they know that Stanton's body was entering a period of unreliability that lasts until this day.

When the Yankees had acquired Stanton, his injury record was long, but perhaps not too worrisome. Although Stanton had missed significant time in multiple seasons with the Marlins, much of that could be attributed to freak accidents, such as when he hurt his wrist on a swing in 2015, or the scary incident in 2014 that saw Stanton get hit in the face by a Mike Fiers fastball (soon becoming one of the league's first stars to wear a more protective helmet). But the signs were there. In 2013, Stanton missed six weeks with a hamstring strain. He missed a few weeks at the end of the 2016 season with another hamstring problem. Just two soft-tissue injuries, but they hinted at what would prove to be Stanton's biggest foe in the second half of his career.

In April of 2019, Stanton hit the IL with a biceps strain. He'd return in June but go back on the IL with an injury to a ligament in his knee. He posted a 138 OPS+ for the season in just 18 games. Stanton played only 23 games (with a 145 OPS+) in the COVID-shortened 2020 season thanks to another hamstring strain. He strained his quad early in 2021, and when he returned, the Yankees largely used him as a designated hitter. The decision paid dividends, as Stanton enjoyed a relatively clean bill of health the rest of that year, posting a 136 OPS+ in 139 games.

Although injuries had already ravaged his body by this point and relegated him to DH duty, there was hope that now, with Stanton focused on providing value with his bat and staying off the outfield grass, the slugger would be able to stay productive and in the lineup. The first half of 2022 lent credence to that hope, as Stanton and Judge blitzed opposing pitchers; both hit at least 10 homers in the season's first 32 games, just the third time in franchise history two players had done so. Stanton ran an OPS near .900 for the first two months of the season, but injuries struck again. While Judge would keep up his early-season pace and set a new AL home run record, Stanton started to have ankle issues in late May, issues that would plague him for the rest of the year. Stanton would miss 52 games and post the worst batting average, OBP, and OPS+ figures of his career even when he was on the field.

Five years into his Yankee tenure, Stanton had become a strange and polarizing figure in Yankee lore. When healthy, he was still great, if not as great as his peak with the Marlins. He also authored nigh-countless signature moments in the playoffs, always finding a way to contribute come October no matter how banged up he'd been from March to September, smashing a home run in the 2018 Wild Card Game against Oakland, one in the 2019 ALCS versus Houston, and going deep four times in the 2020 ALDS against the Rays.

That was the strange paradox of Giancarlo Stanton as his Yankee tenure developed; he felt indispensable at times, but he was also the most unreliable player on the roster. The story continued in 2023 and 2024; Stanton suffered another hamstring in 2023 as he limped to the worst season of his career with an 87 OPS+, and in 2024, he strained his hamstring again. This time, though, he healed in time for the playoffs, almost single-handedly leading the Yankees to their first AL pennant in 15 years and earning 2024 ALCS MVP honors. Stanton bombarded Cleveland with four homers in five games; Guardians pitchers had no answers.

That preceded a career renaissance. In 2025, Stanton, of course, got injured, but he returned midseason and put together the best stretch of regular-season performance in his Yankees career. His 155 OPS+ on the year was his highest as a Yankee, but he went through an unholy midsummer stretch during which Aaron Judge was dealing with an elbow injury, carrying the Yankees with a 1.253 OPS and nine homers in August.

The renaissance was oh-so brief. Stanton revealed after the year that he'd been dealing with injuries to both his elbows, and after a so-so start to 2026, Stanton hit the IL with a calf problem with a seemingly minor lower-leg injury that ended up scuttling the rest of his regular season. Stanton returned to pinch-hit in the playoffs, but ultimately struck out twice in two plate appearances after playing just 24 games during the regular season.

That is where we stand with him today. Say the name "Giancarlo Stanton" and the first thing that should come to mind is his immense power, the laser-beam home runs that only he can hit. The towering grand slams, the signature playoff moments that he's authored in pinstripes. But also at the front of mind is everything that's been missing. Stanton is both a towering figure in Yankee history and an absence.

For all the great moments he's created, he's also missed so many, and at the end of the day, after hitting 267 homers with a 147 OPS+ in eight years as a Marlin, he's hit 189 homers with a 123 OPS+ in nine years as a Yankee — though that doesn't include the 18 career playoff blasts in 50 games, as many as Mickey Mantle in 65 games.

Stanton is a walking, mighty contradiction. He's a player whose fingerprints are all over many of the Yankees' most memorable moments from the last decade, and yet his absences loom just as large. He's the most physically imposing human to ever play Major League Baseball, and the most fragile. The man gifted with some of the greatest physical tools in the game's history is opposed and thwarted almost entirely by his own body.

Today, we wish him a happy 37th birthday as he and the Yankees stare into the unknown. He has one year left on his deal, and reports say he'll come to spring training next year with a chance to show what he's got left. He could have a 2025 miracle run left in him, or nothing. He's just 44 homers from 500, a Hall of Fame-caliber figure that's so close and yet so, so far. We will all be so lucky if, at age-37, Stanton finds just enough of the elixir of youth to stay on the field for some stretch of time, long enough to swat a few more mythical home runs and create a few more memories.
