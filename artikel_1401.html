Emotion regulation capacity lacks a shared neural signature

Cognitive reappraisal refers to changing how one thinks about a situation to alter its emotional impact. It is central to well-being1 and engages prefrontal regulatory circuits2, prompting the assumption that their activity reflects a person’s capacity to regulate emotions3,4,5. Yet evidence for this assumption remains inconclusive and has not been established at scale. Here we analysed 40 task-based functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) datasets in a preregistered consortium with 2,175 participants and show that three widely used indicators of reappraisal capacity—trait questionnaires, task-based affective ratings and amygdala down-regulation—do not converge on a common core. Moreover, none of the three indicators showed the expected association with canonical prefrontal regulatory circuits. Trait questionnaires showed no detectable neural associations across the brain and task-based affective ratings were instead associated with responses in the amygdala and sensorimotor cortices. At the process level, canonical control circuits were engaged during both emotion generation and reappraisal, challenging their presumed specificity for emotion regulation. These findings caution against using activation in putatively regulatory brain regions to infer how well a person regulates emotions, with implications for neural biomarkers, interventions and psychoeducation that link emotion regulation difficulties to specific regional brain deficits.

Emotion regulation is a core determinant of mental and physical health1. It encompasses the strategies that people use, intentionally or automatically, to influence their emotional experience. Among these, cognitive reappraisal—changing the interpretation of a situation to alter its emotional impact—has received particular attention in psychology, psychiatry and the broader public. It is central to cognitive-behavioural therapy and linked to resilience, lower symptom burden and improved daily affect1.

Neuroimaging studies have extensively mapped the neural basis of reappraisal. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) consistently implicates a network of lateral and medial prefrontal regions, often along with reduced amygdala activation during reappraisal of negative stimuli2. These findings have informed neuromodulatory interventions for emotion regulation deficits6,7,8. Still, they are based primarily on within-person designs that contrast experimental conditions such as ‘reappraise’ versus ‘view without regulation’ in the same individuals, and many studies and broader theories extend these neural within-person mechanisms to explain between-person individual differences in well-being or psychopathology4,5. This extension is not necessarily warranted.

Extending within-person neural mechanisms to explain between-person differences risks the ecological fallacy9: associations at one level need not hold at the other. For instance, people who habitually use reappraisal report better well-being than others. However, in daily life, reappraisal often occurs in response to stressors and may therefore coincide with worse-than-average momentary mood.

Compounding this, prefrontal regions that are engaged during reappraisal also participate in working memory, response selection and pain processing10—limiting their specificity as emotion-related markers of regulatory function. Task designs, moreover, may not mirror naturalistic regulation, raising questions about ecological validity and relevance for individual-difference research11,12. A recent systematic review found partial effect convergence in prefrontal and amygdala circuits, but studies were underpowered13 (median n = 25) and too heterogeneous for formal meta-analysis3. In turn, meta-analyses on reappraisal in mental disorders have yielded mixed results, with inconsistent findings in the prefrontal cortex4,14,15.

To resolve this, we conducted a preregistered consortium analysis of 40 fMRI datasets on the neurobiology of emotion regulation capacity (NIDER consortium; n = 2,175;  Methods, ‘Consortium procedures’ and Supplementary Table 1)—approximately 20 times larger than previous efforts3—with sufficient power to detect even small between-person associations (r = 0.10; Supplementary Methods, ‘Power analysis’). In this context, reappraisal capacity refers to individual differences in whether, and how effectively, people use reappraisal to reduce negative affect. We focused on three widely used indices that are often assumed to reflect related facets of reappraisal capacity: trait questionnaires, task-based affective ratings following reappraisal, and amygdala down-regulation during reappraisal. Prior work has linked these three indices with each other and with prefrontal activity during reappraisal3,16,17,18,19. To our knowledge, this assumption has not been tested at scale. Notably, the most widely used trait questionnaires are generally understood to index self-reported habitual tendencies to reappraise rather than the ability to implement reappraisal. We nonetheless included them given their proposed functional link to such ability16,17,18 and their widespread use.

We explored whether the three indices converge on a shared core, and whether any of them relate to activity in canonical emotion regulation circuits during common reappraisal tasks2. Our findings have direct implications for neuromodulatory interventions, biomarker development and the validity of individual-difference research in affective neuroscience.

In a first step, we aimed to assess the overlap between the three main indices of cognitive reappraisal capacity: trait questionnaires, task-based affective ratings following reappraisal, and amygdala down-regulation. If these three indices represent a similar core construct, or are otherwise functionally related, they should show a meaningful association. Individual consortium study teams calculated the correlations between the three indices of reappraisal capacity based on a standardized coding protocol using functionally predefined regions of interest, ensuring that higher values on each index indicate higher reappraisal capacity. Study-level results were aggregated with random-effects meta-analyses, which is statistically equivalent to person-level random-effects mega-analysis for the preregistered analyses and was intended to circumvent data sharing thresholds (Supplementary Methods, ‘Meta- vs mega-analytic approaches’). The evidence for null results was further quantified using Bayes factors (Methods, ‘Bayesian evidence’).

There was a statistically significant association between amygdala down-regulation and task-based affective ratings (P = 0.010; Table 1 and Forest plots in Extended Data Figs. 1–3). As hypothesized, participants with stronger down-regulation of the amygdala via cognitive reappraisal showed a better down-regulation of task-based affective ratings. Yet, the effect size was comparatively small (r = 0.08): To detect this effect size with a statistical power of 80%, a single study would need to test 1,105 participants (Supplementary Methods, ‘Power analysis’).

There was no significant association between trait questionnaires and either outcome—that is, amygdala down-regulation or task-based affective ratings (P > 0.06, BF10 ≤ 0.16; Table 1). Confidence intervals ruled out even small effect sizes (r = 0.10), for which statistical power was above 88% given the maximal observed between-study heterogeneity (τ = 0.07; Supplementary Fig. 1). The overlap (shared variance) between the three indices of cognitive reappraisal capacity is shown in Fig. 1, which indicates no shared overlap between all three. Bayes factors substantially favoured the null hypothesis for associations with trait questionnaires (BF10 ≤ 0.16). Between-study heterogeneity was not statistically significant in any of the three tests (P > 0.17) and was particularly small for the non-significant correlations (τ = 0.00 and 0.06), indicating that these null findings are unlikely to be explained by differences in study design.

All studies made use of trait questionnaires that primarily assess beliefs about habitual reappraisal use. Although relations to implementation ability, as measured by experiments, are theoretically and empirically expected16,17,18, trait questionnaires that directly measure beliefs about such abilities might show stronger associations. To test this, we performed a subsequent analysis based on three large studies that additionally administered modified versions of the emotion regulation questionnaire to capture such beliefs about abilities20. Fixed-effects meta-analysis confirmed that beliefs about habitual use and implementation ability are strongly related (r = 0.67; Table 1). Supporting the null results for habit questionnaires, there was substantial evidence against an association between these ability trait questionnaires and task-based affective ratings (BF10 = 0.13). By contrast, the association between trait ability questionnaires and amygdala down-regulation was statistically significant (P = 0.010), but in the opposite direction, meaning that beliefs in higher reappraisal capacity were related to less effective down-regulation of the amygdala. This represents strong evidence against an effect in the expected direction (one-sided BF10 = 0.05).

To address the second research question, we examined whether individual differences in the three reappraisal indices were systematically related to neural activity during cognitive reappraisal beyond the amygdala. We first tested these associations in canonical emotion regulation networks derived from prior meta-analytic work2 (Fig. 2a) and then complemented these confirmatory analyses with exploratory whole-brain analyses, both outlined in the preregistration (Methods, ‘Statistical analyses’). This strategy was intended to assess whether the three indices showed associations with established regulatory circuitries and whether any observed effects converged on overlapping or distinct neural regions.

There was no statistically significant association between trait questionnaires and neural activity during reappraisal (Fig. 2b). The correlations with average activity in the two predefined networks were negligibly small and the null hypothesis strongly favoured by Bayes factors (both |r| ≤ 0.03, P > 0.25, uncorrected, BF10 < 0.02; k = 37, n = 2,091). No voxels survived the correction for multiple comparisons in the voxel-wise networks of interest or the whole-brain approach. All brain-wide effect sizes were smaller than |r| = 0.11 (mean = −0.01, range −0.10 to 0.09).

These null results were stable when using a jackknife procedure—that is, no statistically significant voxels when repeating the meta-analysis while leaving out one study at a time. They were further supported by Bayesian posterior odds for voxels across the canonical emotion regulation networks2 (that is, Bayes factors corrected for multiple comparisons; Methods, ‘Bayesian evidence’). The null hypothesis was decisively favoured for 98% of voxels with odds over 100:1, there were no voxels with favourable odds for the existence of an effect, and only two voxels favoured the null in an ambiguous range below 3:1. The power to detect a small effect (r = 0.10) was 96% for average network responses (τ = 0.07). Power in voxel-wise analyses was mostly above 80% in simulated scenarios for this effect size, especially for the network of interest approach (Supplementary Methods, ‘Power analysis’).

As in the previous section, a fixed-effects meta-analysis of three studies that provided trait questionnaires specifically targeting beliefs about ability yielded no statistically significant results (both a priori networks |r| < 0.04, P > 0.511, BF10 < 0.07, uncorrected; k = 3, n = 444). No voxels survived thresholding; all Bayesian posterior odds favoured the null hypothesis (mean = 0.00, range 0.00 to 0.25).

As for trait questionnaires, there was no statistically significant association between average down-regulation of task-based affective ratings and average neural activity during reappraisal in the preregistered regulatory networks, with strong support for the null hypothesis by Bayes factors (both |r| < 0.03, P > 0.22, BF10 < 0.03, uncorrected; k = 31, n = 1,958). No voxels within these networks survived correction for multiple comparisons in voxel-wise analyses.

Within canonical emotion regulation networks2, about 98% of voxels showed null effects that were decisively supported by Bayesian posterior odds (mean = 0.00, range 0.00 to 0.29), with only 12 voxels falling into an indecisive range (mean = 0.84, range 0.34 to 2.52). By contrast, five voxels in the brainstem (caudal pons) provided substantial evidence for an effect (mean = 7.31, range 4.84 to 13.89). Using uncorrected Bayesian posterior odds, evidence for effects was observed in 55 voxels exceeding 3:1 odds, including the amygdala (mean = 69.63, range 3.02 to 1,055.27). This pattern aligns with the uncorrected preregistered findings from the amygdala region-of-interest analyses (see ‘Divergence of capacity indices’).

For whole-brain analyses, the jackknife procedure indicated that results were highly sensitive to the inclusion of data from Brehl et al.21 (Extended Data Fig. 4), potentially owing to its large sample size in combination with study-specific effects (for example, data were collected in the context of the first COVID-19 outbreak). In the full analysis, a negative association with task-based affective ratings was observed in a cluster of 86 voxels outside the canonical regulatory networks. Excluding data from Brehl et al.21 increased the extent of significant voxels substantially to 587, mainly in sensorimotor and dorsal attention regions, with smaller clusters in visual cortex, caudate nucleus and brainstem (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Table 1). Across these regions, better down-regulation of negative emotions was associated with lower activation (mean = −0.11, range −0.16 to −0.09). Together, these results suggest that classically emotion-generating regions, but not emotion-regulating regions, are indicative of emotion regulation capacity, albeit with small effect sizes.

To facilitate the psychological interpretation of the significant neural activation pattern, we conducted automated meta-analytic decoding using Neurosynth, which quantifies the association between whole-brain activation maps and large-scale term-based meta-analytic patterns derived from the neuroimaging literature (for example, ‘pain’, ‘fear’ and ‘motoric’). Neurosynth decoding against 50 topic maps from 11,406 fMRI studies indicated that the meta-analytic activation map is most similar to language processing (‘comprehension’: r = 0.17, ‘sentence’: r = 0.16 and ‘semantic’: r = 0.14) and most dissimilar to sensorimotor processing, consistent with the deactivation observed in whole-brain analyses (‘hand’: r = −0.25, ‘movements’: r = −0.22, ‘motor’: r = −0.17).

Top-down theoretical models predict that effective reappraisal should be associated with lower amygdala and higher prefrontal responses—an opposing pattern. Between-person differences in overall brain responsivity12,22, however, could blur such regional specificity.

Contrary to theoretical predictions, participants with stronger amygdala responses during reappraisal also showed substantially stronger responses throughout the rest of the brain in the same contrast (mean r = 0.27, range −0.08 to 0.68; Extended Data Fig. 5; correlation signs are reversed in the figures because amygdala down-regulation was coded as [view negative − reappraise negative]). This included both regulatory networks (first: r = 0.29, P < 0.001; second: r = 0.24, P < 0.001; k = 35, n = 2,088) and more than 170,000 voxels brain-wide, with only a single voxel showing a negative correlation (Fig. 2d). A person with a larger amygdala response than others, therefore, also tends to show larger responses in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and elsewhere—making the expected opposing pattern almost undetectable with standard fMRI individual-difference analyses.

This pattern was not unique to the amygdala. For all pairs among 489 anatomical regions23 (Methods, ‘Inter-region correlations’) in an independent reappraisal dataset24 (n = 33), any two brain regions were on average highly correlated across participants (r = 0.58, s.d. = 0.18).This means that individuals who respond more strongly in one region tend to respond more strongly everywhere across the brain, compared with other people. Crucially, this was not specific to reappraisal: the same pattern held across 18 studies spanning negative emotion, pain and cognitive control10 (mean r = 0.37; Fig. 3a), with between-person correlations among canonical resting-state network responses25 reaching 0.50–0.80 (Fig. 3b). Person-level whole-brain responsivity thus potentially reflects pervasive confounding processes for regional fMRI inference—an issue that affects not only reappraisal research, but also the broader task-based individual-difference literature.

These profound individual differences in global signal responses are in conflict with the argument that most fMRI tasks have been designed to elicit strong within-person effects, at the cost of meaningful between-person variance, despite their common use in individual-difference research26. We tested this potential misalignment by analysing trial-wise responses to negative pictures in two large samples with population-representative variance in negative emotional dispositions, using the most common reappraisal task12 (Methods, ‘Variance decomposition’). For trial-wise task-based affective ratings, the majority of variance was at the within-person level (92–93%; Fig. 4). By contrast, for neural responses in the amygdala and regulatory networks, about 60% of variance was on the between-person level. Thus, associations are likely to be reduced because common fMRI task designs prioritize robust within-person condition effects over between-person effects26, a mismatch that is further amplified by large individual differences in global signal responses.

Finally, we tested whether multivariate neural signatures provide more sensitive indices of reappraisal capacity than anatomically defined regions or networks. Neural signatures are predictive decoding models that combine activity across many brain regions to estimate the presence or intensity of a psychological state in new individuals. We focused on three established candidate signatures: a negative affect decoder, a decoder of reappraisal as a dynamic state, and a decoder trained to predict individual differences in reappraisal success from task-based affective ratings (Methods, ‘Multivariate neuro-affective signatures’).

Using the four largest studies (n = 730), we first examined whether these signatures differentiated emotion generation from reappraisal (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Table 2). The negative affect decoder was strongly expressed during emotion generation (d = 0.69) but increased further during reappraisal (d = 0.15), indicating that its expression did not follow the expected reappraisal-related reduction in negative affect. The reappraisal state decoder showed stronger negative expression during emotion generation (d = −0.41) than positive expression during reappraisal (d = 0.30), suggesting limited specificity for reappraisal as opposed to variation in emotional state. The reappraisal ability decoder showed substantial negative expression across both contrasts (d = −0.22 for emotion generation and d = −0.37 for emotion regulation). Consistent with these ambiguous task-response profiles and univariate null findings, none of the three signatures was significantly associated with reappraisal capacity, whether indexed by trait questionnaires or task-based affective ratings (P > 0.08; Fig. 5b and Extended Data Table 3). Descriptive correlations were near-zero (|r| ≤ 0.06), with confidence intervals excluding effects larger than |r| = 0.15 and Bayes factors favouring the null hypothesis (BF10 < 0.25), especially for the negative affect decoder (BF10 < 0.09).

A similarly unexpected pattern emerged for the broader systems commonly used to distinguish emotion generation from regulation. The regulatory network was involved in both processes, as recently observed27 (d = 0.22 for emotion generation and d = 0.37 for emotion regulation). Notably, removing the outlying study 4 altered the pattern substantially, resulting in a stronger preference for emotion generation (d = 0.18) than for regulation (d = 0.08), the latter having an inconclusive Bayes factor (BF10 = 0.90). By contrast, the amygdala was selectively engaged during emotion generation (d = 0.16) and was not modulated during reappraisal (d = 0.01, BF10 = 0.03; detailed statistics are in Extended Data Table 2).

Despite extensive research on the neural basis of cognitive reappraisal, the assumption that brain activity during reappraisal reflects individual differences in regulatory capacity has rarely been tested at the scale needed for robust effects. Here we found a notable lack of convergence among three widely used indices of reappraisal capacity. Only the association between task-based affective ratings and amygdala down-regulation was statistically significant, yet with an effect size (r = 0.08) so small that more than 1,000 participants would be needed for 80% power—far exceeding typical sample sizes in the field. Trait questionnaires showed no association with either index, with Bayes factors favouring the absence of effects. None of the three indices showed the expected relation with activity in canonical prefrontal emotion regulation circuits. These findings call for a fundamental reassessment of how reappraisal capacity is measured and interpreted across behavioural and neural levels of analysis.

The lack of convergence challenges a central assumption in affective neuroscience and clinical neuroimaging: that questionnaires, task-based affective change and neural responses provide complementary windows onto a shared underlying regulatory capacity, albeit with unique aspects. This matters because the extent to which common reappraisal task findings inform claims about emotion, psychopathology and intervention targets may be overstated if individual indices capture only distinct aspects of regulatory functioning. Indeed, research on emotional coherence shows that experiential, behavioural and physiological channels often diverge because they operate on different time scales and are shaped by different causal influences and biases28. Trait questionnaires reflect recollected beliefs about reappraisal habits and ability across diverse real-life contexts; task-based affective ratings capture momentary regulatory effects under constrained experimental conditions; and neural responses may additionally reflect processes outside conscious awareness29, while being shaped by neurovascular and scanner-related factors that are unrelated to regulation. The present findings therefore do not merely show weak measurement convergence; they suggest that common indices of reappraisal capacity, in fact, may carve the construct at different joints, without the widely assumed contribution of the prefrontal cortex.

For task-based affective ratings, we found associations in emotion-generative rather than prefrontal emotion-regulatory regions: the amygdala, the caudal pons, and—when excluding an outlier dataset from the early COVID-19 era—sensorimotor and dorso-parietal attention networks, outside of canonical emotion regulation regions. The role of the amygdala remains debated: although it tracks concurrent negative affect in well-powered studies30,31, it also responds to positive and otherwise salient stimuli4,32. We further confirmed with four large samples that it is not consistently modulated by reappraisal27. The amygdala is better understood as a neuromodulatory hub facilitating attention, memory encoding and consolidation33, and allostatic responding—processes that are relevant to emotion generation but not specific to it. One possibility is that reappraisal changes the quality or valence of an emotional response without proportionally reducing its salience.

Our finding of sensorimotor associations is consistent with extended process models that assign a role to sensorimotor cortices and the brainstem in the action phase of regulation27. Recent large-scale evidence indicates that even in the absence of overt motor responses, sensorimotor cortices carry rich information about emotion generation27, are modifiable by reappraisal strategies27, are related to individual differences in stress vulnerability12,34, and are a promising target for neuromodulation in depression35. Neurosynth decoding further indicated language-related activation, consistent with the verbal nature of reappraisal. Together, these patterns suggest that individual differences in affective down-regulation are more closely tied to salience-allostasis and perception–action-related processes than to canonical prefrontal regulatory circuits36. Notably, canonical regulatory circuits were similarly engaged during both emotion generation and reappraisal, challenging the commonly assumed dichotomy between bottom-up excitation and top-down control in emotion regulation27.

The absence of associations between trait questionnaires and task-based or neural indices points to an explanatory gap that can be understood through process models of emotion regulation1,27. Most trait questionnaires capture habitual tendencies across multiple temporal stages, including encountering and recognizing reappraisable situations, deciding whether to regulate, and selecting reappraisal over competing strategies. From one angle, previous work showed associations between reappraisal habits, ability and neuro-behavioural implementation16,17,18, including causal evidence that neuromodulation affects reappraisal habits19. Similarly, we found a very strong correlation between habit and ability questionnaires, pointing towards a functional relation on a conceptual level. From another angle, standard fMRI tasks bypass much of the naturalistic reappraisal sequence: participants are instructed when and how to regulate, receive practice trials and respond to standardized stimuli optimized for reliable group-level effects. Therefore, individuals with higher reappraisal capacity might derive their real-world advantage less from universally superior neural implementation than from selecting, initiating and sustaining reappraisal in personally meaningful contexts, and from encountering environments that more readily afford successful reappraisal37. Relatedly, higher self-reported reappraisal tendency may partly reflect greater regulatory need rather than greater regulatory success: individuals experiencing stronger negative emotions may have more occasions to engage in reappraisal38. At the same time, highly intense negative emotions may render reappraisal less suitable than alternative regulation strategies39, relating reappraisal success to situational demands and individual differences in emotional reactivity4,12. This aligns with broader arguments that successful self-regulation reflects complex person-situation interactions rather than being governed by a context-general regulatory resource40. Future paradigms should therefore additionally engage other regulatory stages including strategy selection, personally relevant stimuli and spontaneous deployment.

The gap between task-based affective ratings and concurrent neural responses raises a different set of issues. Within persons, trial-by-trial affective ratings can be decoded from neural responses with large effect sizes, particularly with distributed multivariate neuro-affective signatures30. Even anatomically localized responses, such as the amygdala, track within-person fluctuations in affect with smaller but theoretically meaningful effect sizes30,31. This robust within-person correspondence demonstrates that brain–behaviour alignment is achievable and supports the value of process-oriented research for identifying neuromodulation targets.

Between persons, however, brain–behaviour associations are systematically much smaller12—a dissociation also observed for non-fMRI physiological markers of emotion28. Neural degeneracy provides one principled account: multiple neural configurations can support similar regulatory outcomes, and the specific configurations recruited may differ markedly across individuals41, particularly in higher-order association cortices where anatomical variability is substantial42. Yet degeneracy alone cannot explain the magnitude of the within–between discrepancy. Process-oriented fMRI studies routinely identify replicable regional response patterns, as reflected in meta-analyses of reappraisal2, and population-level models trained to decode within-person affective states generalize well to unseen healthy and clinical cohorts43. This indicates substantial representational overlap between individuals on a process level.

Three additional factors are likely to contribute. First, we demonstrated that established multivariate neuro-affective signatures can produce large effect sizes (dmax = 0.69) while conflating emotion generation and regulation, limiting their psychological interpretability and showing no association with reappraisal capacity. Second, standard reappraisal tasks were designed for process-based research and may not elicit the stable individual differences needed for strong brain–behaviour associations26. In support of this, we found little variance on the between-person level in task-based affective ratings (7–8%), even in two large samples with population-representative variance on affective traits12. Third, we found that between-person fMRI responses are strongly shaped by person-level whole-brain responsivity—potentially owing to factors such as neurovascular variation22—producing highly correlated regional responses that limit anatomical specificity. This extends to canonical resting-state networks, where correlations reached 0.50 to 0.80 across multiple task domains. This complicates efforts to interpret individual differences in task–fMRI responses through influential clinical models, including the triple-network model, suggesting antagonistic relations between canonical networks44. These global effects are not removed by calculating contrasts between conditions. As a result, most between-person variance in neural responses likely reflects confounded individual differences, in stark contrast to task-based affective responses. This highlights a fundamental misalignment, potentially alleviated by tasks designed to specifically induce individual differences while accounting for whole-brain responsivity12,45.

These findings might have direct implications for how neural correlates are used to guide clinical inference and neuromodulatory interventions. The absence of prefrontal individual-difference associations does not invalidate process-oriented findings: the neural systems identified in within-person reappraisal research could remain viable targets for neuromodulation, as they reflect the implementation of reappraisal regardless of between-person variation. However, our results support caution against inferring individual regulatory capacity from regional brain activation—whether in the prefrontal cortex, amygdala or elsewhere—as is common in clinical neuroimaging models of emotion dysregulation4,5. The success of neuromodulatory interventions targeting these regions8,19 may reflect distributed network reorganization rather than correction of a specific regional deficit35,46, consistent with evidence that different stimulation sites can achieve similar clinical outcomes. This fits a systems neuroscience perspective where single regions cannot be interpreted in isolation. Supporting this, recent well-powered studies linked individual differences in affective functioning with reappraisal-contingent connectivity between the amygdala and prefrontal areas (ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), dlPFC and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC))47, as well as shifts along primary gradients of macroscale neurofunctional organization48. Further testing these approaches represents a natural next step for the NIDER consortium and individual-difference neuroimaging of emotion regulation more broadly.

Our sample comprised non-clinical populations, limiting generalizability to groups with greater symptom severity and variability. A population-representative sample would show 1.3–1.5 times greater variance in reappraisal habits49, potentially enabling slightly larger effect sizes. More importantly, transdiagnostic samples covering the full range of clinical dimensions could reveal meaningful individual-difference associations that are absent in non-clinical adult individuals, while also introducing potential confounders. Most included studies used habit-oriented questionnaires,although the small number of studies with additional ability-focused measures still had sufficient power for most small effect sizes in fixed-effects analyses. Finally, whereas our meta-analytic approach is statistically equivalent to mega-analysis for voxel-outcome correlations, subject-level data would enable person-specific decoding models—a promising direction for future work on neural heterogeneity.

Together, our findings challenge the assumption that prefrontal activation during reappraisal reflects individual differences in regulatory capacity. The three most commonly used indices of reappraisal capacity do not converge, and none relates to canonical emotion regulation circuits in the expected direction. More broadly, person-level whole-brain responsivity represents a pervasive methodological challenge for task-based fMRI research on individual differences—one that extends across emotional, cognitive and pain domains. Large-scale resting-state studies with sample sizes in the thousands have similarly failed to identify replicable neural correlates of emotion-related traits13, underscoring the need for better alignment between theoretical constructs, behavioural tasks, and neural measures12. Progress will require methodological refinements—including connectivity and gradient-based approaches—as well as a conceptual re-framing of what reappraisal capacity means, how its multiple components are best measured, and the level of analysis at which each measure is expected to predict real-world emotional well-being. Until then, strong claims about a person’s emotion regulation capacity based on any specific brain region should be made with considerable caution.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they measured (1) task-based fMRI (2) during a cognitive reappraisal task, (3) using negative stimuli, (4) to compare a down-regulation condition with a view-without-regulation condition, and (5) included a trait-like questionnaire measure for cognitive reappraisal (6) in a healthy sample. The latter criterion was used to avoid excessive heterogeneity in disorder presentation and medications (see ‘Towards transdiagnostic neural systems’).

Contributing research groups were invited using two strategies. First, we conducted a systematic literature search, contacting 64 authors via e-mail or Researchgate. Second, we used mailing lists of academic societies to advertise the consortium. In total, this led to 32 contributing research teams with 40 datasets comprising up to 2,175 participants. For an overview of study characteristics see Supplementary Table 1. Nine studies were unpublished. For 31 studies (77.5%), teams indicated they had not performed any of the targeted main analyses of the present paper. There were no further assessments of selection bias. Most papers used the emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)51 to measure reappraisal, while six used the cognitive emotion regulation questionnaire (CERQ)52, and only one study used a different questionnaire (FEEL-E)53. The majority of studies used pictorial stimuli. Two studies used videos, one study used autobiographical memory cues, one study used Cyberball, and one study used a different game task.

We conducted a systematic literature search of articles using the PubMed, Web of Science and PsychINFO databases. See Supplementary Fig. 2 for a flow chart depiction of the screening and selection of studies via literature search. We searched for the following keywords: ‘emotion regulation’ cross referenced with ‘fMRI’ or ‘neuroimaging’ or ‘functional magnetic resonance imaging’ or ‘functional MRI’. This search process yielded a total of 2,633 potentially relevant articles on 7 July 2021 (after duplicates were removed). Two independent reviewers (J.Z. and M. Schweizer) systematically examined titles and relevant abstracts using the Rayyan website54 to determine whether an article fulfills the criteria to be screened. The following criteria were applied: The study included original empirical results, was written in English or German, included adult healthy participants, and assessed an explicit emotion regulation paradigm during fMRI measurement. In a next step, for all remaining studies full texts were screened by three independent reviewers (J.Z., M. Boll and D. Lang) and they were included if (1) participants were told to use reappraisal strategies to modulate a negative emotion, (2) an emotion regulation questionnaire was assessed (for example, ERQ), (3) a control condition was included in which participants were confronted with emotional stimuli but did not regulate their emotions, and (4) negative stimuli were used in the emotion regulation task. Finally, a total of k = 61 studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria. Relevant articles in the authors’ library were also reviewed for titles that might have been missed by our literature search. Studies identified in this manner (k = 3) were collected for inclusion. Of the k = 64 studies, k = 17 reported the correlation between fMRI BOLD activation (‘reappraise negative’) and emotion regulation questionnaire data. The authors of all 64 studies were contacted.

Research groups prepared up to three unthresholded group-level statistical images, containing voxel-wise t-statistics for the between-person correlation of the whole-brain neural response in the [reappraise negative – view negative] contrast and the three outcomes of interest. These outcomes were (1) trait questionnaires of cognitive reappraisal, (2) the person-wise averaged decrease of task-based affective ratings during ‘reappraise negative’ versus ‘view negative’ conditions, and (3) and person-wise averaged decrease of amygdala responses during ‘reappraise negative’ versus ‘view negative’ conditions.

A homepage with detailed instructions and a Matlab example analysis script was provided to support analyses. Contributors were instructed to compute the three measures of reappraisal capacity so that higher values correspond to better reappraisal capacity. For task-based affective ratings, this entailed (re-)coding for higher values to correspond to a more negative emotional response and calculating person-wise averaged down-regulation of affect from the contrast [view negative – reappraise negative]; thus, a greater reduction in negative affect via reappraisal leads to higher values. Similarly, for amygdala down-regulation, average activity was extracted from the [view negative – reappraise negative] contrast, as higher values correspond to a better down-regulation of the amygdala for sign congruence with the other two outcomes. A standardized amygdala mask was provided for all research groups based on the intersection of an anatomical amygdala mask (SPM anatomy toolbox v2.2b) and a functional emotion-generating network from an emotion regulation meta-analysis2, which ensures the anatomical and functional specificity of the amygdala region of interest.

Furthermore, contributors provided direct bivariate correlations between the three reappraisal outcomes (for example, trait questionnaires and task-based affective ratings) as well as demographic and other study information (Supplementary Table 1).

Images were quality controlled individually by plotting t-values on a brain template, histograms, and performing brain-wide thresholding at false discovery rate (FDR) = 0.05 to check for plausibility. Collectively, quality was controlled by checking for outlier studies within the same emotion regulation outcome category (for example, mean whole-brain activation, Mahalanobis distance, and covariance matrices). Two images were removed from whole-brain analyses due to insufficient coverage55,56. Effective sample sizes for tests are reported in the results section.

Study-level estimates were aggregated within a random-effects meta-analytic framework. Under standard assumptions, this approach closely approximates subject-level mega-analysis for the associations examined here, while reducing barriers to data sharing57 and potentially increasing the number of includable studies and overall precision58,59 (see Supplementary Methods, ‘Meta- vs mega-analytic approaches’ for details).

First, we assessed the concordance between the three outcome measures (trait questionnaires, task-based ratings, amygdala down-regulation) using random-effects meta-analysis implemented in the R package meta using the DerSimonian and Laird approach to estimate between-study heterogeneity60.

Second, we tested associations between the three outcomes and two neural emotion regulation networks based on a recent process-based meta-analysis on emotion regulation2; networks 1 and 2 (Fig. 2). These networks are thought to be predominantly implicated in the cognitive control (versus emotion-generating) aspects of emotion regulation and mostly include regions in the prefrontal, parietal, and temporal cortex. Images were resampled to the same spatial resolution of 2 × 2 × 2 mm³. The statistical core procedure was done in three preregistered stages. In the first stage, we tested the correlation between average activity in the two networks of interest, respectively, and reappraisal capacity, with Bonferroni corrections for two tests. In the second stage, we performed voxel-wise tests in the combined two networks with FDR correction based on the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (FDR = 0.05, approximately 10,000 voxels). In a third stage, we repeated the second-stage analysis on the whole brain (approximately 180,000 voxels). Between-study heterogeneity was tested for statistical significance based on whole-brain data and corrected with the same FDR procedure.

To perform the image-based fMRI meta-analysis, we developed custom Matlab code which interfaces with the fMRI-specific functionality of the CanlabCore toolbox23. Our procedure converts study-wise unthresholded t-maps to correlation maps and therefore, together with sample size information, offers voxel-wise estimates of effect size, statistical significance, and between-study heterogeneity via the DerSimonian and Laird approach61,62. The code was validated by testing congruence with meta-analytic packages in R as well as performing a small meta-analysis on six openly available group-level images of physical pain, which detected typically involved brain regions and was successfully decoded as ‘pain’ using Neurosynth63 (Supplementary Methods, ‘Testing the custom meta-analytic functions’). Neurosynth decoding was performed analogously as described in the main text.

We calculated Bayesian posterior odds for focal effects to quantify the relative evidence for the null hypothesis (that is, no effect exists) against the alternative hypothesis (that is, an effect exists) based on established interpretational thresholds64. These analyses were performed using Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis65. We used objective literature-based prior distributions for effect sizes and between-study heterogeneity. For effect sizes, we used a normal moment prior66 approximating the empirical distribution of meta-analytic correlations across individual-difference research67, with a mode at r = 0.20 (Supplementary Fig 3). For between-study heterogeneity (τ), we used a one-sided t-distribution (scale factor = 0.15, df = 1), approximating the empirical distribution found for over 700 meta-analyses68 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Bayesian posterior odds were not corrected for multiple comparisons in the three preregistered overlap tests (see ‘Divergence of capacity indices’) and the preregistered tests on average network activation. Therein, the null and alternative hypothesis are assumed to be equally likely (prior odds = 1:1), making the Bayesian posterior odds identical to the Bayes factor. For voxel-wise tests, we calculated Bayes factors across all four canonical networks involved in emotion regulation, including both aspects of emotion regulation and generation2, consisting of about 20,000 voxels. Due to this large number of comparisons belonging to the same global hypothesis, we performed Bayesian corrections for multiple comparisons by adjusting the prior odds to be equal for the global null (no effect across all voxels) versus the alternative hypothesis (an effect in at least one voxel) using Jeffrey’s method69. Like Bonferroni correction, this method is conservative when tests are not independent. Therefore, we calculated the number of effective independent tests based on the spatial correlation structure70, indicating 53 independent tests as a basis for multiplicity correction.

Anatomical regions were defined using the CANlab 2018 combined atlas in MNI152 space, which integrates cortical and subcortical regions from several established parcellations23. The full set of 489 regions, including atlas labels, anatomical or network descriptions and references to source parcellations, is provided in the Supplementary Atlas File. For each pair of regions, between-person Pearson correlations were calculated across participants using region-wise activation estimates. Correlations were then summarized across all region pairs to characterize the overall degree of shared between-person variation in regional activation.

To obtain trial-level neural responses, additional first-level general linear models (GLMs) were estimated using a beta-series approach in which each trial’s (n = 45) viewing epoch was modelled with a separate regressor, yielding 45 trial-specific parameter estimates for subsequent ROI analyses. Additional regressors in the beta-series GLM included a regressor reflecting all rating periods (to account for motor responses) as well as the six realignment parameters (to account for head motion-related activation). Subsequently, each trial-specific beta map was used to extract mean amygdala and regulatory network response scores for each trial and used for the variance decomposition, parallel to the task-based affective ratings.

To stabilize variance estimates for all three outcomes, we first removed outlier observations outside 3-times the interquartile range within each participant (9–113 out of 16,155 observations). Then, we removed whole outlier participants outside 3-times the interquartile range based on their average ratings (1–4 out of 359 participants). Next, we fit intercept-only linear mixed models to decompose variance between (random intercept) and within (residual) individuals.

Analyses included three candidate signatures: a negative affect decoder trained to predict task-based affective ratings (affective state)30, a decoder discriminating reappraisal from other competing processes (reappraisal state)71, and a decoder trained to predict individual differences in reappraisal success from task-based affective ratings (affective trait)72. All three were developed in relatively large samples, while the first two were additionally validated in multiple independent samples. Generally, these neural signatures consist of brain images containing regression weights. For each decoder, person, and condition, we calculated the signature expression by taking the dot product between the respective contrast (beta) image and the signature image. Pattern-expression scores, as well as the amygdala and regulatory network responses, were z-standardized within studies to transform them from the arbitrary site-specific fMRI metric to a comparable Cohen’s d metric. Specifically, for differences between conditions, values were standardized on the pooled within-condition variance separately for each study. Then, a linear mixed model was calculated with condition, study, and their interaction as fixed effects and a random intercept for each participant. Aggregate estimates and confidence intervals were obtained from estimated marginal means averaged equally across studies73. For correlations with reappraisal capacity, pattern-expression scores were calculated for the contrast [reappraise negative – view negative]. Pattern-expression scores and reappraisal capacity measures were z-standardized within studies and then analysed using pooled individual-level linear regressions across studies. These approaches equate to fixed-effects mega-analysis (that is, without study-specific random intercepts and slopes), as modelling between-study heterogeneity is severely limited with only four studies.

For model-derived contrasts, approximate Bayes factors were calculated from the corresponding t-statistics and denominator degrees of freedom using the default Cauchy prior for standardized mean differences implemented in the BayesFactor package74.

Aims, literature search strategy, and statistical analyses were preregistered on PROSPERO: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42021243155. A notable error in the preregistration was the statement that only studies using pictures as stimuli are included. Rather, the meta-analysis included all types of stimuli, which we stated correctly on the accompanying homepage and the mailing list advertisements. Regardless of this error, the overwhelming majority of studies used pictures as stimuli (88%) and the low, statistically non-significant between-study heterogeneities as well as the jackknife procedures indicate that stimulus type does not influence the results.

The participating studies followed their respective institutional approval processes at the time of data collection, including applicable ethical approval and informed consent procedures. For the present meta-analysis, participant-level analyses were performed locally by the contributing research groups, and only study-level summary statistics and group-level statistical images were shared. No new data were collected and no participant-level or identifiable information was accessed by the meta-analysis team. Therefore, no separate institutional review board approval or formal exemption was sought for the present secondary analysis.
