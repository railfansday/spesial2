The S&P 500 Is Flashing Warning Signs. Here's Why Long-Term Investors Should Stay the Course.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

High valuations don't tell the whole story.

By Adam Levy – Nov 19, 2026 at 1:45PM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -1.91%) just entered its fifth year of the current bull market that kicked off in 2022 when the Federal Reserve lowered rates just as the generative AI revolution was taking off.

But now, with the Fed raising rates again and investors worried about financing and regulations slowing the growth of AI, investors have been capitulating. The benchmark index has experienced significant volatility in November following the midterm elections, an inflation report that came in higher than expected, and Jensen Huang's resignation from Nvidia.

Investors point to a major warning sign that the S&P 500 has been flashing since May, suggesting stock prices could collapse. While long-term investors shouldn't ignore the warning sign, understanding the current context is important.

Ultimately, it's not a good enough reason to stop holding stocks. Here’s why.

One of the most common valuation metrics used to project long-term investment potential is the cyclically-adjusted price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio. Developed by Nobel laureate Robert Shiller, the metric takes the last 10 years of earnings of all the S&P 500 companies, adjusts the total for inflation, and compares that number to the current price of the index. The goal is to smooth out economic cycles and provide a clear understanding of where prices stand today.

Historically, a higher CAPE ratio is correlated with lower future returns for stocks. Likewise, a low CAPE ratio corresponds to high returns. In May, the CAPE ratio exceeded 40 for just the second time dating back to 1870.

The CAPE ratio peaked at around 44 in late 1999 and remained elevated throughout 1999 and 2000. The S&P 500 level peaked in March 2000 before collapsing 49% over the next two and a half years.

Indeed, history doesn't paint an optimistic picture about the stock market. But context is key. The CAPE ratio is, by definition, looking in the rearview mirror to try to see where we're going. While it's important to check your mirrors, a driver who doesn't look out the front windshield is much more likely to make a mistake.

The CAPE ratio isn't much use in predicting short-term returns; it's used to forecast long-term returns. But we only have one usable data point on what happens to the market in the long term when the CAPE ratio goes above 40. The 10-year returns from 1999 to 2009 included not just the dot-com bubble, but the great financial crisis of 2007-2008. Those events are unrelated, but they occurred in the same 10-year period, resulting in the so-called lost decade. The 2007 crash didn't happen just because stock prices were high in 1999.

The CAPE ratio is unreliable at calling peaks in the stock market, too. While 1999 is an example of when it successfully did so, the CAPE was also elevated throughout the late 1990s, when it climbed above 30. The only other time it had reached 30 was right before the Great Depression. If investors sold when the ratio reached 30 in the 90s, they'd miss out on years of further growth. The ratio also exceeded 30 in 2017, and it's mostly stayed at or above that level except for a few pullbacks since.

One reason for elevated valuations is that corporate profits are so much stronger than they've been in the past. During the dot-com bubble, a lot of stocks had egregious P/E ratios or ratios that were incalculable because they didn't have any profits. Today, the largest companies in the S&P 500 are immensely profitable and sport below-average P/E ratios on a forward-looking basis.

Analysts expect profits to keep climbing faster than average. The analyst consensus calls for earnings growth of 16.2% in 2027, well above the historic average. Overall, the S&P 500 sports a forward P/E ratio around 18, which is below its average valuation for the past 10 years. That suggests the index can keep climbing from here if analysts are even close to correct.

Importantly, the CAPE ratio can decline even as stock prices rise. We've already seen that pattern play out with the standard P/E ratio for the S&P 500 this year, as earnings grow faster than stock prices. The CAPE ratio just takes longer to catch up since it looks back 10 years.
