Tiny ocean animals reveal a hidden foundation of marine life

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Tiny ocean animals depend on different food sources across the Pacific, revealing important differences in marine food chains.

Microscopic algae form the base of many ocean food chains, but bacteria also play a major role.

In the warm, nutrient-poor tropical Pacific, researchers found that roughly half of the food supporting tiny crustaceans called Calanus copepods could be traced back to bacteria.

Near the Arctic, in the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea, more than half was supplied instead by dinoflagellates, a group of algae.

Calanus is an important food of fish and seabirds, and a change in what feeds it can therefore be passed on to animals higher in the food web.

The study was led by Hyuntae Choi and Kyung-Hoon Shin of Hanyang University ERICA in South Korea; the co-authors include Brian Popp of the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa.

The copepods were collected on cruises between 2015 and 2020.

What the work provides is therefore a comparison among regions at particular moments; it is no record of change in a single place.

Copepods are small crustaceans that drift with ocean currents. They include Calanus, which feed by filtering tiny food particles from the water.

Adults were collected from two research ships, the Isabu and the icebreaker Araon, by means of nets pulled up from a depth of about 660 feet (200 meters).

From the tropical and subtropical Pacific, the sampling sites extended through the subarctic Pacific and the Bering Sea as far as the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea.

Copepod proteins were then broken down in the laboratory into their separate amino acids. Of these, the essential amino acids cannot be made by animals, which must get them from food.

Production of those amino acids by each source at the base of the food web yields its own mixture of carbon isotopes (slightly heavier and lighter forms of the same element).

Because passage up the food chain leaves the mixture almost unchanged, it serves as a fingerprint.

The proportion derived from each of six groups was then estimated with a statistical model; the groups included diatoms, dinoflagellates, two kinds of cyanobacteria (bacteria that use sunlight) and bacteria that feed on organic matter.

Such proportions refer to the origin of the food and not to what each copepod actually ate, since part of it may first have passed through other microbes.

The tropical Pacific is strongly layered: a warm surface layer mixes little with the nutrient-rich water below it. The condition is known as stratification, and relatively few algae grow there.

Satellite measurements of chlorophyll, the pigment with which algae capture sunlight, ranged there from 0.03 to 0.13 milligrams per cubic meter, against as much as 7.86 near the Bering Strait.

For these tropical waters, the model put about 31% of the copepods’ food base down to bacteria that feed on organic matter.

Cyanobacteria that turn nitrogen gas into a usable nutrient added about 20%, and algae about 47%.

Bacteria thus supplied roughly as much of the food base of tropical copepods as algae did.

Farther north, the share from algae increased with latitude, reaching about 74% in the southern Chukchi Sea.

The share of bacteria feeding on organic matter fell to about 16%, and that of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria to about 7%.

The greater contribution from algae came mainly from dinoflagellates, single-celled organisms that use sunlight and of which some also eat other microbes.

In the tropics, dinoflagellates and diatoms contributed similar shares, about 19% and 18%. In the southern Chukchi Sea, dinoflagellates reached about 52%, whereas diatoms stayed near 15%.

The small share of diatoms is notable, because diatoms were the most common algae counted in the northern water samples (although at some sites dinoflagellates were equally or more numerous).

The ships reached these waters after the bloom of algae had ended, when nutrients were scarce and the sea ice had largely melted.

Dinoflagellates and other small algae tend to become more common under those conditions, the authors noted.

The researchers suggested that diatoms may be digested more slowly, or that copepods may choose other food. Across all sites, the dinoflagellate share increased with chlorophyll, a relationship shown by no other food source.

Nitrogen in the same amino acids was also used to estimate the number of feeding steps between the copepods and the bottom of their food web.

One estimate counts mainly the steps through animals. A second also counts steps through protists, single-celled grazers that eat bacteria and algae.

In the tropics, the second estimate exceeded the first by less than one step. At subarctic and Arctic sites, it exceeded the first by more than one step.

The authors wrote that the larger difference indicates more feeding on protists at higher latitudes during summer.

The difference between the estimates was also greater where the dinoflagellate share was higher.

The team therefore proposed that dinoflagellates able both to use sunlight and to eat microbes may be an important link between microbes and Calanus in the north.

Algae need nitrogen, which here came from two main sources: nitrate dissolved in seawater, or nitrogen gas turned by cyanobacteria into living tissue.

The two sources carry distinct nitrogen isotope signatures, which allowed the contribution of each to be estimated.

In the tropical Pacific, about 29% of the nitrogen in algae was estimated to come from nitrogen gas. Bacteria break down what the cyanobacteria make, and the nitrogen released is taken up by the algae.

In the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea, by contrast, nitrate supplied an estimated 91% of the algae’s nitrogen.

The authors linked the tropical pattern to stratification, which limits the amount of nitrate reaching the sunlit surface where algae grow.

“Importantly, our study shows how warming-driven changes in stratification and nutrient dynamics are reshaping planktonic food webs,” Shin said.

The study nevertheless compared different regions and did not follow a single region as it warmed.

Warm Pacific water flowing north through the Bering Strait in summer has sped up the loss of sea ice in the western Arctic Ocean, the authors noted.

Between 2015 and 2017, no year-to-year differences were found in the diets of copepods from the subarctic Pacific to the southern Chukchi Sea.

Summer surface water in the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea was, however, warmer in 2019 and 2020 than in 2016 through 2018.

An Arctic species, Calanus glacialis, also became less common there, while Pacific species increased.

The authors wrote that food-chain measurements from those warmer years may differ from the ones reported.

Not every copepod was identified to species, and tropical and northern waters were sampled in different months, so that seasonal changes could not be checked.

It also remains unclear whether dinoflagellates that eat other microbes carry the carbon pattern of an alga or that of their prey, an uncertainty that could blur the fingerprints.

The authors called for isotope measurements across more of the North Pacific and over more years, to see how these food chains vary as the ocean keeps warming.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
