The Container Store Has About 100 Stores, and a Cyberattack Stopped Its Online Orders. How Many Days of Sales Could Your Store Lose?

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at security.hackerme.ai

If you take orders on a website, this is worth a look this week. The Container Store's website stopped taking orders after a cyberattack. On Friday, 20 November 2026, parent company Neighborhood Intelligence said The Container Store had restored its website and systems after a cybersecurity incident the weekend of 14-15 November. Physical stores stayed open, using other ways to process sales. The company has not said how long online ordering was down. How many days of sales could your store lose if your website could not take orders?

Neighborhood Intelligence said in a 20 November 2026 statement that its wholly owned subsidiary, The Container Store, had restored its website and systems following a cybersecurity incident "this past weekend." Chain Store Age, reporting that announcement, dates the incident to the weekend of 14-15 November. The Record, Retail Dive and Chain Store Age all covered the same company statement. The company's own release was also listed the same day on the Neighborhood Intelligence investor-relations site and on BusinessWire; those full pages could not be opened directly for this draft (the IR pages returned a block, and BusinessWire timed out), so the wording below is taken from the outlets that quoted it.

The company said that after it detected suspicious activity, it activated its security procedures and proactively suspended online order-taking at The Container Store to protect customer information and its systems. The Record described the event as a cyberattack that forced the company to shut down online ordering and limited in-store payment processing. All brick-and-mortar stores stayed open, the company said, and served customers through "alternative transaction-processing procedures."

Neighborhood Intelligence said it hired independent cybersecurity experts to help with the investigation, containment and restoration. The investigation is still going. The company said it does not believe customer financial data was affected, because The Container Store does not store or maintain consumer financial or payment information. The Record, writing about the same statement, said that so far it does not appear the attackers stole customer financial data.

The company said the incident was isolated to The Container Store. Other Neighborhood Intelligence businesses, including Bed Bath & Beyond, Overstock, buybuy BABY and Kirkland's, were not affected. It said it expects a minimal impact on consolidated revenue for the quarter, and that any impact on profitability is expected to be mitigated by business interruption insurance. CEO Marcus Lemonis said the company "made the deliberate decision to prioritize the protection of our customers and our systems while keeping our stores open and serving customers." The Record says The Container Store has about 100 stores across the U.S., and that Neighborhood Intelligence, a rebrand of Bed Bath & Beyond, bought the company in July for $150 million.

What the company has not said matters as much as what it has. Retail Dive asked what caused the incident, whether it was a ransomware attack, and how long the website was down. The company did not answer those questions. It has not publicly confirmed ransomware, and it has not named a group.

Separately, on 5 November 2026, the ransomware group n0n claimed, in a listing reported by DeXpose, that it had attacked The Container Store and taken a shopper chat corpus of 46,013 typed customer messages, search session analytics, 9,305 named customer email addresses from the account portal, and a staff directory. The listing was marked "NOT PAID â€” EXPOSED." That claim has not been independently verified. Chain Store Age and The Record both note that the company has not publicly addressed the n0n claims, and Chain Store Age says there has been no independent verification that the listing is related to the later weekend incident. This article treats those figures as an unverified claim, not as confirmed facts. The Record also reports that n0n emerged in September and, according to cybersecurity experts, specializes in ransomware and data extortion. That is background on the group, not proof that it caused this outage.

The company says only The Container Store's website and systems were affected, and that its other brands were not. The Record says the chain has about 100 U.S. stores. Shoppers who tried to order online during the outage could not complete those orders. People in physical stores could still buy, using backup payment procedures.

The wider lesson is for any store that takes orders on a website, including a much smaller shop. If checkout stops, online sales stop, even if the physical store stays open. A large chain may have business interruption insurance and a team that can switch to backup payment procedures. Many small stores do not.

The Container Store is a well-known retailer, and its parent says it still expects only a limited effect on the quarter, in part because of insurance. A small shop often does not have that cushion. If your website or checkout is down for a weekend, or for several weekdays, those online orders do not come in later by themselves. Customers go somewhere else. That is why the question is how many days of sales your own store could lose, not how a large chain will book the quarter.

The company's statement about payment data is specific and limited: it says it does not store consumer payment information, and it does not believe that data was affected. The investigation is still open. That does not automatically protect other customer details, and it does not restore missed online sales. The unverified n0n listing, if it were ever confirmed, pointed at emails, chat messages and a staff directory, not payment cards. Even when card numbers are not stored on your site, a downtime event can still cost you days of sales.

Write down a simple backup: phone orders, in-store only, a second checkout tool, or a manual process. Know who decides to take the site offline, who talks to customers, and how you keep selling. The Container Store could keep its stores open with alternative payment procedures. If you sell mostly or only online, you need a plan before the site stops.

We have also written about cases where a website itself was forced offline, including a hacked homepage that took the world hockey federation's site down and courts that went dark when one vendor was hit.

Ask your developer or host what on your site can be reached from the public internet: the store, the admin page, checkout plugins, staging copies, and any leftover tools. A free website security scanner can show what an outsider can see from the internet, without logging in. It is not a full review of your business, and it will not by itself prove you are safe.

Keep the store, checkout and plugins updated. We have covered store-specific risks such as a checkout plugin that can hand a stranger your admin login and a credit-card stealer hidden in online store checkouts.

The Container Store's statement about payment data is one important check. Also ask where customer names, emails, order history and staff directories live, who can access them, and how you would tell customers if something went wrong. You do not need to wait for a confirmed ransomware label to prepare. Have a recent backup you have actually tested, and a short list of who to call if the site or checkout stops.
