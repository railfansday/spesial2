Nvidia Stock Has Swung 5% or More in a Single Day 5 Times in November. Here's How Big a Position I Think Makes Sense.

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

The chip giant's profits more than doubled last quarter, but its stock shed over a quarter of its value in November.

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 27, 2026 at 9:53PM EST

Nvidia (NVDA +0.80%) stock moved 5% or more in a single session five times in November, measured from one day's close to the next. From January through October, it had done that seven times total.

Shares closed at $149.30 on Wednesday, Nov. 25, the last full trading day before Thanksgiving. That left them down about 27% for the month and about 38% off the record $239.24 they closed at on Oct. 6. During the same part of November, the S&P 500 (^GSPC +1.60%) dropped about 5%.

Nvidia's profits, though, more than doubled year over year last quarter. I think the stock still earns a spot in a diversified portfolio, but at about the weight it already has in the S&P 500 (roughly 6% of a portfolio), not much more.

Four of November's five big days were drops. Shares sank 9% on Nov. 5 and about 5% on Nov. 10. The worst was on Nov. 20, a 14% loss that put the stock at $125.53, almost 48% under its October record. Nvidia then jumped 31% on Nov. 23 before giving back about 6% on Nov. 24.

Nov. 25 nearly made it six. The stock sank as much as 9% in the session, to $141.17, before finishing down around 4%. The S&P 500 dipped just 0.25% that day.

Over November's 18 trading days through Nov. 25, Nvidia shares moved an average of nearly 6% a day, up or down. The S&P 500's average daily move was around 1.5%, so Nvidia swung about four times as much as the market.

Many investors own Nvidia without ever picking it. The stock was around 6% of the State Street SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY +1.62%) as of Nov. 24, the fund's second-largest holding.

That weight is a sound guide to how much Nvidia a diversified portfolio should carry overall. It's the stake an investor gets just by owning the S&P 500, without betting for or against the company.

At around 6% of a portfolio, Nvidia's fall from Oct. 6 to Nov. 20 would have cost the whole portfolio around 3%. At 20%, the same fall would have cut nearly 10% off the total.

The business is the reason, and it's still growing at a remarkable pace. In the quarter that ended Oct. 25, 2026 (the third quarter of Nvidia's fiscal 2027), revenue climbed 103% year over year to $115.8 billion. Earnings per share also more than doubled on a non-GAAP (adjusted) basis, rising 111% to $2.68.

Adjusted earnings per share have gone up every quarter, from $1.59 in the three months that ended in January to $2.68 in the three months that ended in October. And each quarter's gain was a bit bigger than the one before.

For the last four quarters, Nvidia's adjusted earnings per share came to $8.36. The $149.30 closing price is around 18 times those earnings. FactSet's Nov. 20 report put the S&P 500 at 22.9 times its own earnings for the past 12 months, so on profits already reported, Nvidia costs less than the index as a whole. Against what analysts expect Nvidia to earn per share in fiscal 2028 (which ends in January 2028), the stock trades at around 9 times earnings.

That leaves the stock looking reasonably priced to me even if profits stopped growing, and cheap if they keep rising.

But these profits aren't guaranteed. Accounts receivable (money customers owe Nvidia) totaled $81.0 billion at the end of fiscal Q3, over double the $38.5 billion at the end of fiscal 2026 in January. Part of this came from longer payment terms for some big, multi-quarter deals with investment-grade customers. And a slowdown in spending on AI might hurt Nvidia's earnings and its stock at the same time.

In a diversified portfolio, I think something near Nvidia's S&P 500 weight, around 6%, is about right, with room for a point or two more at most. Nvidia's rising profits and its low price against expected earnings are good reasons to own the stock. But an investor holding much more than that might find another 48% fall tough to stomach. And investors who already have most of their money in S&P 500 index funds may own about enough already.
