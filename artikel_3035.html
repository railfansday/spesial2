The S&P 500 Just Whipsawed Near a Record High. Here’s Why I’m Lowering My Expectations, Not Selling.

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Short-term volatility isn't necessarily something to be feared.

By David Dierking – Nov 22, 2026 at 12:26PM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

Volatility in the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) has picked up since the midterm elections. That week was the index's worst since April 2025, and it now sits roughly 10% below its all-time high.

That kind of volatility doesn't usually happen when the S&P 500 is near its all-time highs. It's often considered a sign that the conditions that lifted stock prices to records may no longer be in place. And investors are dealing with some real concerns about where the market heads from here.

While bounces like the one that took place the week ending Nov. 13 might feel like relief, history shows that might not be the case.

I went back and looked at S&P 500 history since 1970. Specifically, I looked for periods of market swings that look just like the one we experienced. Over history, there were 19 instances in which the index fell by at least 2.5% one week and rebounded by more than 2.5% the next week, while still within reach of an all-time high.

The good news is that recent history has shown solid gains for the S&P 500 following these periods. Since 2018, all six occurrences produced positive returns six months later, with a median return of more than 11%.

The bad news is that prior to that, the results are incredibly mixed. There are more instances of negative forward returns, and the gains six months later were often minimal. Plus, some of these examples came during some of history's worst bear markets.

Data source: Investing.com and author's analysis of S&P 500 daily closing prices, 1970 to Nov. 13, 2026. A case is a weekly drop of at least 2.5% followed immediately by a weekly gain of at least 2.5%, with the index finishing within 5% of its record close. Six months = 26 weeks; 12 months = 52 weeks.

The one thing history has shown us is that the market's biggest moves, both up and down, tend to cluster. You'll find several of these days bunched up around the tech bubble, the financial crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

That's why the bounces aren’t necessarily relief. They can be part of a high-volatility environment in which investors are trying to determine how assets should be priced. It's not a rally built on financial strength or confidence. It's typically built on uncertainty. And that's why volatile periods like this can be potential signs of longer-term trouble.

But as the data shows, it may be just fine. There are several examples in this data set of the S&P 500 generating double-digit gains in the months following. That's why it's usually not advisable to trade in these kinds of markets. There's really no way of knowing if you'll be right or wrong. And that feels more like gambling than anything.

Honestly, I wouldn't make any radical changes to my asset allocation, such as selling stocks and moving to cash. I'd keep funneling money to my investment accounts regularly.

The reason I'd hold tight is that most of what happens in the short term is noise. Even over seemingly longer periods of six to 12 months, the S&P 500 could easily move in either direction. I won't need my money for 15 years, so I'm more than happy to ride it out and leave the guesswork to others.

And it's important to remember that volatile periods like this are normal when investing in stocks. It should be expected, and you should be prepared to ride it out, assuming you don't need the money for a decade or more as well. Keep a disciplined approach and focus more on your long-term goals.
