Should You Build a Custom Web App or Use SaaS?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

For most businesses, SaaS is the better starting point. It usually handles standard tasks such as accounting, payroll and email faster and more cheaply than a custom build. A custom web app makes sense when your workflow is unusual, when the software gives you an edge, or when standard tools can't meet your needs without costly workarounds.

The easiest test is what people do on it.

A website mainly shares information. Visitors read pages, look at products or fill in a contact form. A company homepage or a blog is a website.

A web app is software that runs in a browser and lets people get something done, such as booking an appointment, approving an invoice, tracking an order or managing customer records. Common web app examples include customer portals, booking systems and internal dashboards.

SaaS (software as a service) is ready-made software, usually web-based, that businesses access through a subscription. Common SaaS examples include online accounting tools, team chat apps and email marketing platforms. A custom web app is built for one business and shaped around how that business works.

The web application vs website comparison is mainly about purpose. The SaaS vs custom question is about ownership, control and fit.

A hybrid setup lets you build only the part that makes your business different while still using standard tools for everything else. No-code platforms can also sit somewhere in the middle. They work well for simple internal tools, prototypes and early versions of an idea, but they can become limiting when you need complex logic, greater scalability or the flexibility to move off the platform later.

Klarna, the payments company, shows how this can play out. In March 2025, TechCrunch reported that Klarna had started consolidating data from several SaaS tools, including its customer management system, into an internally developed tech stack. In a post on X, Klarna CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski clarified: "So no, we did not replace SaaS with an LLM." On whether other companies would take the same path, he wrote, "Will all companies do what Klarna does? I doubt it."

Even Klarna's approach combines its own technology with other software, and its CEO doesn't expect every business to copy it.

A better question than "what's the web application development cost?" is "what will this cost us over three years compared with our current tools?"

Here's a simple way to run that comparison using made-up numbers. Say a SaaS tool costs $40 per user each month and you have 50 users. That's $24,000 a year, or $72,000 over three years. With per-user pricing, the cost generally rises as you add people.

A custom web app might cost more upfront, followed by yearly costs for hosting, updates and fixes. Put your actual quotes into the same three-year comparison and the right answer often becomes clearer. Sometimes it's still SaaS.

A custom web app still needs work after launch. Someone has to pay for hosting, apply security updates, fix bugs, keep up with browser and framework changes, and add features as the business changes. Ask for these costs in writing before you commit.

AI coding tools can speed up the first version of a custom web app development project, but they don't remove this ongoing work. People who understand the code still need to review, secure and maintain it.

SaaS has its own after-launch questions. Before you commit to a vendor, check whether you can export all your data, how long the contract locks you in, and what happens if prices rise or a feature you rely on disappears.

When the signals do point toward building, custom web application development can turn a process that doesn't fit any off-the-shelf tool into software built around how your business works.

A sensible plan is to rent what's standard, build only what makes you different, and understand the real cost of each option before you choose.
