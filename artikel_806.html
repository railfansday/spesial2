The footballer-turned-BookTuber who made an obscure Tolstoy novella a belated bestseller

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Once named the best player in Brazil, Gustavo Scarpa’s literature obsession has inspired thousands to become bookworms

Julián Scatolaro noticed something odd was occurring when several people came into his bookshop over the space of a few days, all asking for Leo Tolstoy’s novella The Death of Ivan Ilyich.

The 35-year-old, who owns a bookshop in the small Argentine city of Chajarí, 311 miles from Buenos Aires, said the sudden interest in a book written in 1886 was surprising enough. But this was particularly strange because when his customers ask for works by the Russian writer, they usually want War and Peace. In a video he posted about the mystery, he called it the “Tolstoy enigma”.

Strangest of all was what he learned after an internet search: the source of the interest was Gustavo Scarpa, a 32-year-old Brazilian footballer.

“I’ve been named the best player in the Brazilian league [in 2022] and won two Copa Libertadores titles [South America’s equivalent of the Champions League, in 2020 and 2021], and I’ve never felt as famous as I do now – and not because of football, but because of my book recommendations,” Scarpa said in an interview at the training ground of his team, Atlético Mineiro.

The left-footed attacking midfielder had a six-month spell at Nottingham Forest in 2023 before returning to Brazil to join Atlético Mineiro, one of the country’s most popular clubs.

But Scarpa is now enjoying a second life as a “BookTuber”. His recommendations regularly go viral in his country – and had already sent The Death of Ivan Ilyich to the top of Amazon Brazil’s overall sales ranking before inspiring the excitement at Scatolaro’s bookshop in neighbouring Argentina, which, unlike Brazil, is not even a Portuguese-speaking country.

His literary fame has grown so much that in late October he was invited to be one of the presenters at Brazil’s leading literary awards and, for one night, swapped his kit and boots for a suit, tie and dress shoes to present the newly created Reading Promotion category, for influencers who encourage the habit. Perhaps next year he will be among the nominees.

“This is all fascinating,” said Alicia Klein, a sports columnist for the website UOL. “It’s almost a social experiment: will people listen if a classic of Russian literature is recommended by a footballer? They clearly did – and they liked it!”

What makes it even more striking, in her opinion, is that Brazilian footballers often come from poor backgrounds, with limited access to education, and that even those who had better opportunities “mostly choose to remain disengaged” from social or cultural debates.

She wondered whether, in a country where more than half the population has not read a single book in the previous three months, Scarpa’s influence could be an “antidote” to that of global star Neymar, who is now struggling as a player at Brazil’s Santos but is still highly active on social media, promoting everything from frozen food to car insurance.

“Footballers are role models for a lot of children. While Neymar was advertising betting companies [before they were banned in Brazil], Scarpa was recommending books,” said Klein.

Scarpa said his passion for books came late in life. Raised in the interior of São Paulo state, he had barely read anything beyond the books assigned at school and the Bible – twice – as he became an evangelical Christian, as most players in Brazil are.

After emerging at Rio de Janeiro’s Fluminense, he moved to São Paulo’s Palmeiras, where in 2019 a fitness coaching intern said that for every goal or assist Scarpa registered, he would give him a book. There would be plenty of them to come over what became the best years of his career.

“I would read even while walking around the training ground; before matches, the other players would be listening to music, and I’d be off in a corner with my headphones on, reading,” said Scarpa, who admits some teammates would occasionally tease him for it.

He began reviewing books with short, humorous captions on his Instagram account. On Niccolò Machiavelli’s The Prince: “liked this one. Didn’t find it as controversial as people said”. Franz Kafka’s The Trial was “incomprehensible, but cool”. Stephen Hawking’s Brief Answers to the Big Questions was “pretty cool”, too – “even though we disagree on a few things LOL”.

His reviews had already attracted curiosity in the Brazilian press but really took off when, while recovering from an injury, he began recording them on video for social media and his YouTube channel.

It coincided with a good run for Atlético Mineiro, which may have helped more fans embrace his BookTubing. A group of them created Galeitura – a play on the Portuguese words “galo”, meaning rooster, the club’s mascot, and “leitura”, meaning reading – and then a book club that also organises donations and activities in schools. With more than 130,000 followers on social media it has now inspired supporters of other clubs to do the same.

“A lot of people came to us saying they had never read a book or that they didn’t like reading, but had started because of Scarpa,” said one of Galeitura’s organisers, 22-year-old literature student Carolina Marques Martins. “And some also said they thought The Death of Ivan Ilyich would be much harder to read than it actually was.”

Vladimir Tolstoy, the author’s great-grandson and director-general of the Leo Tolstoy State Museum in Moscow, recently recorded a video thanking Scarpa, saying his “popularity has allowed Tolstoy’s work to become a bestseller in your beautiful country”.

Other recent Scarpa recommendations include Zora Neale Hurston’s Their Eyes Were Watching God and William Golding’s Lord of the Flies; when we spoke at the training ground he was reading Art Spiegelman’s The Complete Maus.

His favourite genre is “self-destruction”, he said. “Like Dostoevsky – something a bit difficult; not melancholic, but about internal conflicts.” His favourite book? Victor Hugo’s Les Miserables.

He said he had persuaded five teammates to start reading, while the coach, Eduardo Domínguez, an Argentinian, said Scarpa’s book recommendations were helping him learn Portuguese.

“I’ve been in football for more than 20 years, and I know how passionate fans can get, to the point of killing someone simply for supporting a different club,” said Scarpa. “So to see rival fans coming together around reading, inspired by a movement that was itself inspired by me, is incredibly rewarding.”
