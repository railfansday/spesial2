9 of the world's deepest lakes, from Crater Lake to Baikal

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Earth's 9 deepest lakes include one buried under Antarctic ice and a sea that counts as a lake. See how deep each one goes.

Stand at the edge of a lake whose floor no one can see, and the mind starts filling the dark water with stories. People have played this game for a long time. Myths from many parts of the world circle back to the bottomless lake, a body of water with no visible end below the surface. Storytellers have stocked those depths with monsters, drowned cities, hoards of treasure, mermaids, and the realms of the dead. Each image answers the same unease. Water hides what lies beneath it, and a basin with no apparent bottom hides everything.

Modern tools have stripped away much of the old guesswork. Sonar, remote cameras, and miniature submarines let scientists probe places that earlier generations left to fantasy. The mystery survives. A sounding turns a fabled abyss into a pit with a charted limit, yet a number on a map does little to quiet questions about what lives down there or what lurks out of reach. Hard data and stubborn imagination pull against each other in these places, and neither side has won. Exact numbers can show how far the descent runs. They cannot reveal what the lake has kept.

Britannica counts down the nine deepest lakes on Earth in a list that Noah Tesch wrote and the publication's editors fact-checked. The countdown opens with the shallowest of the nine and works toward the deepest, giving each entry's depth in feet with the metric equivalent alongside. Every entry pairs that figure with a short portrait touching on where the lake sits, how it formed, what inhabits it, how people use it, or what sets it apart. The list does not say how or when anyone took the readings. This article reports each figure as printed. The slides that follow keep the same order, from the ninth spot up to the first.

Crater Lake plunges 1,943 feet (592 meters) in the Cascade Range of southwestern Oregon, farther down than any other lake in the U.S. The basin is young by geologic measure. It formed about 7,700 years ago, after the giant volcano Mount Mazama erupted and collapsed. Water fills the space where the mountain stood. The country's record for depth, in other words, belongs to a basin that took shape while people lived in the region.

The Klamath may carry an echo of the disaster. Many believe the Klamath Indians' oral traditions about the lake's origins preserve a genuine memory of the event, wrapped in myth. Such traditions move by voice, from one generation of speakers to the next. Any memory of Mount Mazama's fall would have endured thousands of years of retelling on speech alone. If the belief holds, the Klamath account stands apart from the old tales that place monsters, drowned cities, and realms of the dead beneath bottomless waters. A real catastrophe sits at its core.

Crater Lake has no stream feeding it and no channel carrying water away. Precipitation, the rain and snow that land on the basin, provides its main supply. Without rivers, the lake receives nothing that streams would carry in from nearby slopes. The National Park Service credits the lake's deep blue color and clarity to water that contains almost nothing except water.

A single cataclysm explains most of the traits the lake shows today. Mount Mazama's destruction opened the basin, left a closed bowl filled from the sky, and may have given the Klamath a narrative worth passing on. The depth, the legend, and the blue water all trace back to one doomed peak in the Cascades. No other lake in the countdown has a creation story that many believe reflects its true and violent beginnings.

Great Slave Lake drops 2,015 feet (614 meters) in Canada's Northwest Territories, edging past the depth Crater Lake reaches in Oregon. Its name comes from an Athabascan-speaking group, the Slave or Slavey. The lake bears a people's name, yet few people live around it.

Two limbs, the North Arm and the East Arm, give the lake an irregular outline. The East Arm divides into two sections, with Christie Bay to the south and McLeod Bay to the north. The Slave River provides the main inflow, and the Mackenzie River drains the basin toward the Arctic Ocean. The river shares the Slave name, so one label marks both the basin and its chief source of water. Its outflow ends in a polar sea, the frozen north whose climate governs daily life here.

A punishing northern climate keeps people scarce along these shores. Yellowknife, the territory's biggest city, holds fewer than 20,000 residents. Every other town in the Northwest Territories has fewer people still. Yellowknife sits on the North Arm, which puts the territory's largest population center on the lake's own shoreline.

The same cold turns the frozen surface into a seasonal roadway. Britannica notes that the few residents hardy enough to stay enjoy certain perks, and the ice delivers one of them. Great Slave Lake freezes thick enough to bear cars and trucks for almost half the year. Each winter day, several hundred vehicles take an ice road that cuts the trip between Yellowknife and the community of Dettah. Traffic on that scale, in a territory with so few inhabitants, suggests the shortcut forms part of ordinary winter routine for many locals.

The shortcut disappears for the rest of the year, when the ice cannot hold that traffic. Travelers $TRV between Yellowknife and Dettah lose their route across the lake in those months. One climate both thins the population and builds the winter route its residents drive.

Lake Ysyk extends 2,192 feet (668 meters) down in Kyrgyzstan, and its surface stays open through winters in which temperatures fall to −15 °F (−26 °C) as a matter of routine. The Kyrgyz name for the lake, Ysyk-köl, translates as "Hot Lake," a title the unfrozen water earns each year.

Scientists credit the lake's slight salinity and geothermal activity in the surrounding area for keeping ice from forming. UNESCO points to low salinity and warm springs as well, and it adds the basin's depth to the list of causes. The same plunging basin that earns Lake Ysyk a spot in this countdown helps it hold off the cold. Depth cannot do the job alone, though. Great Slave Lake, the previous entry on the list, falls just short of Ysyk's measure, yet its ice hardens into a winter roadway. Ysyk's chemistry and the heat beneath it supply what its basin cannot.

The Scythians, the earliest settlers of Kyrgyzstan, left gold and bronze artifacts that have turned up near the lake, evidence of a long human presence on its shores. The lake also served as an important stop along the Silk Road. Medieval settlements lie in its shallows, remnants of periods when the water stood lower. The record stretches across eras, from the country's first settlers through the Silk Road years to medieval villages now under water.

The lake's buried history and great depth have drawn researchers and treasure hunters alike. The two groups chase the same waters for different prizes, one after knowledge and the other after riches. Expeditions set out from time to time in search of a "Kyrgyz Atlantis," ancient ruins that legend places in the lake's deeper reaches. The submerged medieval sites show the lake has risen over human communities at least once before. Their presence gives the Atlantis legend a real pattern to build on.

Lake Nyasa runs 2,316 feet (706 meters) to its floor and supports exceptional biodiversity across habitats that stand apart from one another. Three physical traits carve out those zones: the lake's length, its depth, and the layered temperatures of its water. Nyasa's outline is relatively long and narrow, so the basin stretches far in one direction while its water column stacks layer upon layer below the surface.

Each trait splits the lake along a different axis. Length spreads conditions out from one end to the other. Depth and temperature layering pile them from the surface down. Together they turn a single basin into a set of separate environments, and a creature suited to one stretch or layer meets very different conditions in the next.

Cichlids make up the great majority of the species living in those environments. One group of fish supplies most of the variety that sets Nyasa apart. Counting the lake's species comes close to counting its cichlids.

Nyasa's richness of life traces back to the shape and structure of its basin, and most of that richness swims as cichlids.

O'Higgins/San Martín Lake reaches 2,742 feet (836 meters) down, deeper than any of the four lakes before it in the countdown, yet Britannica judges it probably the least known lake on the list. Its basin lies in a stretch of the Patagonian Andes where few people live. The sparse population fits that obscurity.

The Argentina-Chile border runs across the water, and each country gives the lake its own name. Chileans call it O'Higgins. Argentines call it San Martín. The double title writes the border into the name itself. Readers meet the division before they learn anything else about the place.

Glacial meltwater enters from the west, where the O'Higgins Glacier reaches the lake. The glacier also sheds rock flour, stone ground to a fine powder, and the particles stay suspended in the water at high concentrations. Those particles turn the lake a milky turquoise.

The ice that tints the lake shares its name with the Chilean title for the water.

Lake Vostok holds water 2,950 feet (900 meters) deep beneath nearly 2.5 miles (4 km) of Antarctic ice, making it the largest known subglacial lake. Its frozen ceiling sets it apart from every other entry in the countdown. No boat crosses its surface, and no shoreline offers visitors a view. The ice overhead runs more than four times thicker than the water column below it. Of the full distance from the open air down to the lake floor, the water itself fills less than a fifth.

Confirming what the ice concealed took decades. Scientists suspected from the 1970s onward that a large body of fresh water sat trapped beneath the Antarctic sheet. British and Russian researchers produced exact measurements of the lake only in 1996. The long wait shows that the ice guarded even the basin's basic outline.

Mapping the basin left a larger question open. Nobody could collect water from the lake or install sensors under so much ice. Any biology in Vostok stayed a puzzle as a result. Knowing the lake's size told scientists nothing about whether anything lived in it, and settling the matter required a sample of the water itself.

A drilling team broke the impasse in 2012 by boring all the way down to the lake's surface. The breakthrough arrived 16 years after the exact measurements and more than 30 years after the first suspicions of a buried lake. Reaching the water posed a harder problem than sizing it. Scientists who examined the recovered samples report finding many new forms of bacterial life. Their account puts living organisms under a frozen lid that exceeds the depth of every lake in the countdown, Baikal included. If the samples bear that out, the ice that held drills at bay for so long never shut life out of the lake.

The Caspian Sea runs 3,360 feet (1,025 meters) deep, and only Lake Tanganyika and Lake Baikal sink farther on this list. Its name calls it a sea, yet Britannica counts it among the lakes and leaves the label unresolved. The question of what to call it does nothing to its depth, which earns it the third spot in the countdown.

The Caspian stretches between the Caucasus Mountains and the Central Asian Steppe. From north to south it covers nearly 750 miles (1,200 kilometers), and its average width comes to 200 miles (320 kilometers). The length works out to almost four times the width, giving the water the outline of a long corridor. A shape so drawn out leaves room for conditions at one end to bear little resemblance to those at the other.

The floor tilts toward the south. Across the northern third, the water averages about 20 feet (6 meters) deep. The southern third averages about 1,000 feet (300 meters), roughly 50 times the northern figure. Even that southern average falls short of the depth Crater Lake reaches at the shallow end of this countdown. The listed depth more than triples the southern average, so some part of the basin must drop far below the typical floor of either third. A single headline number hides a body of water that is shallow across one broad stretch and deep across another. The middle third goes without an average in the list, leaving the shape of the slope between the two extremes unknown.

Commercial fishing and shoreline tourism carry significant weight in the economies of the countries around the sea. A list built on depth says nothing about those industries. Much of the sea's value to its neighbors flows through its fish and its shores, two assets the 3,360-foot figure does not capture.

Lake Tanganyika runs 4,710 feet (1,436 meters) deep along a border shared by Zambia, Burundi, Tanzania, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Its floor sits 1,350 feet below the Caspian Sea's, the widest gap between any two neighboring entries in the countdown. Only Lake Baikal outstrips it, by 605 feet.

Tanganyika shares its long, narrow profile with Lake Nyasa, and Britannica credits both with extraordinary biodiversity. The likeness ends at the surface. Tanganyika's basin reaches more than twice the depth of Nyasa's. Shape and species richness link the pair, while vertical scale divides them.

Shore communities have lived off Lake Tanganyika's fish since the Stone Age. Commercial methods reached the lake in the 1950s, and Britannica ties them to the overfishing of recent decades. A food source that sustained lakeside villages across thousands of years has come under strain within a single lifetime.

Lake Baikal plunges 5,315 feet (1,620 meters), farther than any other lake in the countdown. It outstrips Lake Tanganyika, its nearest rival, by 605 feet (184 meters), a margin wide enough that the final spot never looks contested.

Baikal reaches more than 2.7 times as far down as Crater Lake, the opening entry on the list. A full 3,372 feet (1,028 meters) separate the floors of the two basins. Crater Lake holds the U.S. record, yet it could sink into Baikal and leave more than half of Baikal's drop still below it.

Baikal's living inventory sets it apart as sharply as its sheer descent. Many of its plants and animals exist nowhere else on Earth. Other entries on the list share that trait. Homegrown species run through the countdown as a pattern, and Baikal supplies one of its strangest examples.

The Baikal seal, also called the nerpa, stands out among the most striking of those residents. No other seal species lives in fresh water alone. Every other kind of seal spends at least part of its existence outside fresh water. The nerpa is an outlier within its own family.

The nerpa's arrival remains a puzzle. Baikal sits hundreds of miles inland, and nobody knows how the seal's ancestors got there. The distance from any coast sharpens the riddle. An animal whose relatives all use waters beyond the freshwater world somehow took hold deep in the interior of a continent. Baikal closes the countdown with a resident everyone can name and nobody can account for.
