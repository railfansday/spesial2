The neural plasticity that underlies adaptation and learning requires countervailing homeostatic mechanisms to maintain the stability and responsiveness of neural circuits. Previous work has described several forms of synaptic homeostasis1, but the mechanisms that detect changes in activity and transduce these into altered neurotransmission have remained poorly understood. Here we show that high-frequency stimulation alters the subsequent properties of neurotransmitter release to confer a presynaptic form of homeostasis, independent of postsynaptic receptors. The mechanism involves vesicular SNARE protein (v-SNARE) VAMP7 (vesicle-associated membrane protein 7), which reduces neurotransmitter release by inhibiting the entry of Ca2+ specifically through P/Q-type presynaptic Ca2+ channels. Previous work has implicated target SNARE proteins (t-SNAREs) in the inhibition of Ca2+ channels2, but without clear physiological rationale. We show that the activity-dependent regulation of release mediated by VAMP7 requires the t-SNARE syntaxin. Although VAMP7 acts at the plasma membrane to inhibit P/Q-type channels, it localizes predominantly to a subset of synaptic vesicles made by adaptor protein-3 (AP-3) that respond specifically to high-frequency stimulation3,4. The localization of VAMP7 to AP-3-derived synaptic vesicles thus provides a mechanism for the activity-dependent delivery of VAMP7 to the plasma membrane, where it associates with syntaxin to inhibit P/Q-type Ca2+ channels and transmitter release. This homeostatic form of metaplasticity operates at multiple excitatory synapses, and in its absence, the fraction of FOS+ neurons increases several fold, revealing a general property of excitatory neurotransmission that might also maintain the sparsity of neural connections.

Homeostatic synaptic plasticity can involve pre- or postsynaptic mechanisms. In response to a global change in activity, mammalian central neurons alter the expression of glutamate receptors1,5,6,7. By contrast, a presynaptic form of homeostasis occurs at the neuromuscular junction. First established in Drosophila melanogaster, this involves increased release of glutamate in response to reduced postsynaptic receptor function8,9. An effect of activity on neurotransmitter release has also been reported at central synapses10,11,12,13,14,15,16,17,18. Although previous work has identified several elements that are required for the different forms of homeostatic plasticity, the mechanisms involved remain poorly understood.

To determine the time course over which presynaptic homeostatic plasticity develops, we suppressed network activity by treating primary mouse hippocampal cultures, which contain almost entirely glutamatergic neurons (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b), with the glutamate receptor antagonists 6-cyano-7-nitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (CNQX) and 3-(2-carboxypiperazin-4-yl)propyl-1-phosphonic acid (commonly known as APV). We monitored the consequent changes in transmitter release using a fusion of the strongly pH-sensitive GFP variant ecliptic pHluorin to a luminal loop of the vesicular glutamate transporter VGLUT1 (VGLUT1–pH). Quenched at the low pH of synaptic vesicles (SVs), the pHluorin-based reporter brightens on exposure to the higher external pH at exocytosis; the reacidification that accompanies endocytosis results in requenching. Figure 1a,b shows that silencing with CNQX and APV increases both the rate and the extent of VGLUT1+ SV exocytosis. The reacidification that accompanies endocytosis also slows (Fig. 1a,b), but stimulation in the presence of the v-type H+-ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin to block SV reacidification results in an increase in the proportion of VGLUT1+ SVs available for release, independent of any change in endocytosis16 (Fig. 1c). We detect no change in the punctate distribution of SVs or the active zone (Extended Data Fig. 1c). To assess the initial release probability, we monitored glutamate release at individual presynaptic boutons using the glutamate sensor iGluSnFR (ref. 19). Stimulating at a low frequency (0.2 Hz), we find that silencing the cultures increases the proportion of synapses that release glutamate (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1d). Because a change in release probability can reflect a change in the presynaptic Ca2+ entry that triggers release, we monitored presynaptic Ca2+ with the low-affinity Ca2+ dye Fluo5F. Figure 1e,f shows that silencing the cultures greatly increases the presynaptic Ca2+ response to both 5-Hz and 25-Hz stimulus trains. This large increase in Ca2+ entry might be expected to increase the release probability more than the doubling that was observed, but we used iGluSnFR only to indicate whether release occurred, and this binary measure underestimates the actual increase in release probability. In addition, stimulation at 1 Hz and 5 Hz triggers Ca2+ entry with near 100% reliability in all presynaptic boutons (Extended Data Fig. 2). Thus, silencing cannot increase the proportion of responsive boutons, excluding effects on action potential invasion of the nerve terminal. Ongoing neural activity thus seems to reduce glutamate release by suppressing presynaptic Ca2+ entry.

To determine whether activity can reverse the effects of silencing, we stimulated the neurons with repeated 25-Hz bursts. We find that two to three bursts reduces the Ca2+ entry in silenced neurons back to baseline, with no effect on presynaptic Ca2+ entry in unsilenced cultures (Fig. 1f). To assess the effects on release, we imaged VGLUT1–pH. Stimulation at 25 Hz also reverses the increased rate and extent of release due to silencing, with no effect on the unsilenced neurons (Fig. 1g). Thus, activity can acutely reverse the homeostatic increase in transmitter release. Stimulation reverses the effects of silencing despite the continued presence of glutamate receptor antagonists, indicating that the mechanism does not require the activation of postsynaptic receptors and is thus induced presynaptically. Silencing with tetrodotoxin (TTX) for 24 h similarly enhances presynaptic Ca2+ entry and transmitter release that reverses rapidly with repeated 25-Hz stimulation (in the absence of TTX) (Fig. 1h,i), supporting the role of neural activity, rather than being a phenomenon restricted to the inactivation of glutamate receptors.

To determine whether activity suppresses release in a more physiological preparation, we extended the analysis to Schaffer-collateral synapses in the stratum radiatum of CA1 hippocampus. We compared the effect of 100 action potentials (APs) delivered at either 5 Hz or 50 Hz (followed by a 20-s gap to allow some recovery) on the response to test stimulation at 5 Hz. With intervening stimulation at 5 Hz, the final stimulus train shows incomplete recovery of the first response but synaptic depression similar to that observed with the first train (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3a), indicating that the properties of release have not changed. Despite delivering the same number of APs, intervening stimulation at 50 Hz increases facilitation and reduces subsequent synaptic depression (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 3a). This suggests a reduction in the initial release probability that enables the accumulation of Ca2+ without depleting SVs20—a fundamental change in the properties of release.

We wanted to understand how high-frequency stimulation suppresses subsequent transmitter release. Previous work has shown that the heterotetrameric adaptor protein AP-3 produces a subset of SVs from endosomes21,22, distinct from the production of canonical SVs at the plasma membrane (or immediately after endocytosis) by clathrin and the related adaptor AP-2 (refs. 23,24,25). We previously found that loss of AP-3 impairs release specifically to stimulation at a high frequency3,4. Thus, we investigated whether loss of AP-3 impairs the suppression of release that follows high-frequency stimulation. Slices from mocha mice, which lack AP-3, show increased rather than reduced synaptic depression after 50-Hz stimulation (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 3b). Consistent with the role of AP-3 in producing SVs that are responsive specifically to strong stimulation (that is, of low release probability), AP-3 is thus required for the suppression of release after high-frequency stimulation, and previous work has shown that loss of AP-3 has little effect on response to the 5-Hz test stimulation itself3.

The dependence on AP-3 predicted that a specific component of SVs made by this adaptor is responsible for the suppression of release after strong stimulation. Indeed, a small subset of SV proteins depend on AP-3 for targeting to SVs. These include the zinc transporter ZnT3 (ref. 26), the phospholipid flippase ATP8A1 (ref. 3) and the endolysosomal v-SNARE (vesicular soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) VAMP7 (refs. 27,28). Because a zinc transporter seemed to be more likely to influence postsynaptic glutamate receptors29 than to affect release, we focused on the two other proteins. Although enriched in mossy-fibre terminals, ATP8A1 and VAMP7 are highly expressed throughout the hippocampus, including in CA1 and CA3, and localize to SVs30 (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis indicates that both genes are expressed in CA3 pyramidal neurons31 (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Western blot analysis confirms the enrichment of both proteins in SVs from the stratum radiatum and stratum lacunosum-moleculare (Extended Data Fig. 4d). In hippocampal slices from Atp8a1-knockout (KO) mice, 50-Hz stimulation still reduces subsequent synaptic depression in the CA1 stratum radiatum (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3c). However, loss of VAMP7 (Vamp7 KO) increases synaptic depression, and 50-Hz stimulation does not alter the increased depression (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 3d). Loss of VAMP7 thus seems to account for the role of AP-3 in the effect of high-frequency activity on subsequent release.

Differences in synaptic depression might result from differential recruitment of Schaffer-collateral input, but varying the stimulus intensity threefold does not affect the kinetics or the extent of short-term depression with 5-Hz stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 3e). The effect also persists under more physiological recording conditions (1.5 mM Ca2+, 1 mM Mg2+), which reduce the initial release probability but still allow 50-Hz stimulation to reduce the subsequent release probability in wild-type (WT) but not in Vamp7-KO slices (Extended Data Fig. 4e,f).

To assess the release probability directly, we took advantage of the use-dependent N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) antagonist MK-801. Slices pre-incubated in MK-801 were subjected to a conditioning train at either 5 Hz or 50 Hz while the postsynaptic cell was held at −70 mV. Stimulating at 0.2 Hz to assess the ‘initial’ response, NMDAR-EPSCs were then recorded at +40 mV in the continued presence of MK-801. As shown in Fig. 2f, previous 50-Hz stimulation of WT slices slows MK-801 blockade to a greater extent than does previous stimulation at 5 Hz, whereas blockade by MK-801 in Vamp7-KO slices does not change with previous stimulation. Thus, high-frequency stimulation reduces the release probability, and loss of VAMP7 blocks this effect.

VAMP7 is widely expressed throughout the nervous system (Extended Data Fig. 5a). We thus examined three other facilitating synapses: perforant-path synapses formed by layer 2 and 3 neurons of the entorhinal cortex onto dentate granule cells; corticothalamic synapses made by cortical layer 6 pyramidal cells onto ventrobasal thalamic relay neurons; and cerebellar parallel-fibre synapses onto Purkinje cells. Similar to CA1 stratum radiatum, 50-Hz stimulation at each of these synapses reduces synaptic depression (Extended Data Fig. 5b–d). In addition, loss of VAMP7 increases synaptic depression and eliminates the effect of 50-Hz stimulation, supporting a general role for VAMP7 in neurotransmission. Loss of VAMP7 does not affect the presynaptic expression of canonical SNARE proteins in SVs, the punctate distribution of SVs or the active-zone protein Piccolo (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b), suggesting that VAMP7 has a specific effect on release.

If VAMP7 is required for suppression of release after high-frequency stimulation, it should also have a key role in the increase in release due to silencing. We thus used VGLUT1–pH to monitor SV exocytosis. In unsilenced cultures, loss of VAMP7 increases SV exocytosis relative to WT across a range of stimulation frequencies (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d). Although silencing with glutamate receptor antagonists increases release from WT neurons, silencing does not further increase release in Vamp7-KO neurons (Fig. 2g). Thus, the increased release observed in the absence of VAMP7 occludes the effect of silencing, suggesting that silencing inactivates VAMP7. Consistent with the hypothesis that VAMP7 has a role in the inhibition of release, overexpression of VAMP7 inhibits the exocytosis of VGLUT1–pH and VGLUT2–pH, particularly at a high frequency (Extended Data Fig. 6c,d).

We next investigated how VAMP7 suppresses release. In hippocampal slices, loss of VAMP7 impairs synaptic facilitation and increases depression with low-frequency stimulation (Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 7a), consistent with the results in culture and suggesting an increased initial release probability. Mossy fibres in the hippocampus express high levels of VAMP7 (refs. 28,32), and a recent study33 has investigated the properties of mossy-fibre synapses in the absence of VAMP7. In contrast to our findings in CA1 stratum radiatum, this study found no change in the properties of transmitter release at mossy-fibre synapses33. This might reflect a difference between the two synapses, but the experiments were also performed at room temperature rather than at the 35 °C used here. We thus repeated the analysis of Schaffer-collateral synapses in CA1 stratum radiatum at room temperature, and found that the effect of Vamp7 KO on 5-Hz and 25-Hz stimulus trains disappears (Extended Data Fig. 7b). Vamp7 KO has no effect on the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) at room temperature, but at 34–35 °C it results in a clear reduction in the PPR, consistent with an increased release probability at this more physiological temperature34 (Extended Data Fig. 7c). At 34–35 °C, mossy-fibre synapses show modestly reduced facilitation in the absence of VAMP7 (Extended Data Fig. 7d), consistent with an increase in the release probability at this synapse as well as at Schaffer collaterals. Temperature thus accounts for the discrepant results, and VAMP7 has qualitatively similar effects at both Schaffer-collateral and mossy-fibre synapses.

The analysis of initial release probability after stimulation suggested that loss of VAMP7 increases the release probability (Fig. 2f). A direct comparison of WT and Vamp7-KO slices confirms this observation (Fig. 3c). Because the initial release probability mostly reflects Ca2+ entry, we varied the external Ca2+ concentration and found that 4 mM Ca2+ occludes the effect of Vamp7 KO on release by Schaffer collaterals (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 7e), consistent with an increase in Ca2+ entry owing to the loss of VAMP7. To determine whether loss of VAMP7 affects Ca2+ at individual presynaptic boutons, we used the low-affinity Ca2+ indicator Fluo5F to monitor Ca2+ entry at the boutons of cultured hippocampal neurons labelled with the red-shifted styryl dye FM4-64 (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Figure 3e shows that at all frequencies tested (1–50 Hz), cultured Vamp7-KO neurons exhibit higher evoked Ca2+ transients than do WT cells, and that the low-affinity Fluo5F does not saturate under these conditions (Extended Data Fig. 8b). At a high frequency, Vamp7-KO but not WT neurons progressively accumulate Ca2+, perhaps because an increased Ca2+ load overwhelms the mechanisms for Ca2+ clearance.

To determine whether the effect of VAMP7 on Ca2+ entry extends to other v-SNAREs, we used lentiviral transduction of the light chain of tetanus toxin (TeNT) to cleave the canonical SV SNARE VAMP2 (ref. 35). Despite complete cleavage of VAMP2 (Fig. 3f) and the resulting inhibition of release (Extended Data Fig. 8c), TeNT has no effect on the punctate localization of SV and active-zone proteins (Fig. 3g), and does not alter the presynaptic entry of Ca2+ at a wide range of stimulation frequencies in WT or Vamp7-KO neurons (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e). Because TeNT also cleaves the related v-SNAREs VAMP1 and VAMP3 but not VAMP7 (ref. 36), the regulation of presynaptic Ca2+ entry by v-SNAREs seems to be unique to the non-canonical v-SNARE VAMP7.

VAMP7 localizes mainly to intracellular membranes, so we wondered how it could suppress presynaptic Ca2+ entry evoked by stimulation. Depolarization of the nerve terminal activates voltage-gated channels to allow Ca2+ entry, but release from intracellular stores might contribute37,38,39. To determine whether VAMP7 inhibits presynaptic Ca2+ entry across the plasma membrane, we took advantage of a mutation that blocks the endocytosis of VAMP2, trapping it at the cell surface40 (Extended Data Fig. 9a–c). Introducing the equivalent mutation (M140A) into VAMP7–pH disrupts its punctate localization at presynaptic boutons in hippocampal cultures (Fig. 3i and Extended Data Fig. 9c), greatly increases the surface fraction, as determined by quenching with cell-impermeant, low-pH buffer (Fig. 3j and Extended Data Fig. 9d), and eliminates the response of VAMP7–pH to stimulation at multiple frequencies (Fig. 3k and Extended Data Fig. 9e). VAMP7(M140A) thus localizes almost exclusively to the plasma membrane. In Vamp7-KO neurons, VAMP7(M140A) reduces presynaptic Ca2+ entry back to the level observed in WT neurons (Fig. 3l) and inhibits SV exocytosis to the same extent that WT VAMP7 does (Extended Data Fig. 9f). Thus, VAMP7 inhibits presynaptic Ca2+ entry at the plasma membrane.

The presynaptic voltage-gated Ca2+ channels that trigger transmitter release generally belong to the N and P/Q types41. To determine which type is inhibited by VAMP7, we took advantage of neurotoxins that block specific channel types. Figure 3m shows that ω-agatoxin IVA, which targets Cav2.1 (the P/Q/-type voltage-gated calcium channel), greatly reduces the increased presynaptic Ca2+ entry that is triggered at a wide range of stimulation frequencies in cultured Vamp7-KO neurons, including the progressive increase in Ca2+ that is observed with high-frequency stimulation. ω-Agatoxin also inhibits Ca2+ entry in WT neurons, but the extent of inhibition is greater in Vamp7-KO neurons. Consistent with this, previous work has shown that Ca2+ entry specifically through P/Q-type channels changes as a function of neural activity17. By contrast, the N-type Ca2+ channel inhibitor ω-conotoxin GVIA has a more modest effect that is similar in Vamp7-KO and in WT neurons (Fig. 3m and Extended Data Fig. 10a). The L-type Ca2+ channel inhibitor nimodipine and R-type inhibitor SNX-482 have little effect on presynaptic Ca2+ entry in WT neurons, and little effect in Vamp7-KO neurons stimulated at a low frequency (Extended Data Fig. 10b). However, nimodipine substantially reduces Ca2+ entry in Vamp7-KO neurons stimulated at 25 Hz (Extended Data Fig. 10b). Thus, VAMP7 inhibits P/Q-type Ca2+ channels at all frequencies and L-type channels at a high frequency. Because previous work has implicated intracellular Ca2+ stores in transmitter release42,43, we also depleted intracellular Ca2+ using the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) inhibitor thapsigargin. Thapsigargin reduces Ca2+ entry at low and high frequencies, but loss of VAMP7 has no effect on the extent of inhibition (Extended Data Fig. 10c).

To determine whether the observed effect of VAMP7 on P/Q-type Ca2+ channels accounts for the changes in transmitter release in the Vamp7 KO, we used slice recording. ω-Agatoxin IVA inhibits release (1 AP) to a greater extent in Vamp7-KO slices than it does in WT slices (Extended Data Fig. 10d,e), consistent with the disinhibition of P/Q-type channels. By contrast, ω-conotoxin has the same, small effect in both WT and Vamp7-KO slices, and nimodipine has essentially no effect on the response to single stimuli in either genotype (Extended Data Fig. 10d,e). A change in Ca2+ entry should also influence short-term plasticity. Extended Data Fig. 10e,f shows that ω-agatoxin reduces synaptic depression to a much greater extent in the Vamp7 KO than in the WT, reflecting a greater reduction in release probability. By contrast, ω-conotoxin has little effect in either WT or Vamp7 KO. Thus, loss of VAMP7 increases the reliance on P/Q-type channels specifically for release.

Next, we examined how VAMP7 contributes to the inhibition of P/Q-type Ca2+ channels. Previous work has shown that the t-SNAREs syntaxin and SNAP25 interact with N- and P/Q-type Ca2+ channels at a specific (‘synprint’) site between the second and third homology repeats44,45,46. In heterologous expression systems, the t-SNAREs inhibit voltage-gated Ca2+ channels47, but when assembled with synaptotagmin, their disinhibition has been suggested to promote the coupling of release to Ca2+ entry48,49,50. The role of v-SNAREs in this form of regulation has remained unclear. We find that loss of VAMP7 does not alter the punctate distribution of Cav2.1 or Cav2.2, or their colocalization with the SV protein synaptophysin or with the active-zone protein Piccolo (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 11a,b). However, loss of VAMP7 reduces the colocalization of syntaxin 1 with Cav2.1 (Fig. 4b), whereas colocalization with Cav2.2 does not change (Extended Data Fig. 11c). In terms of biochemical interaction, VAMP7 and syntaxin 1 co-immunoprecipitate with P/Q-type channels in synaptosomes from WT mice (Fig. 4c). In membranes from Vamp7-KO mice, however, less syntaxin co-immunoprecipitates with the channel, suggesting that VAMP7 stabilizes the interaction of syntaxin with Cav2.1. By contrast, the association of SNAP25 with the channel does not depend on VAMP7 (Fig. 4c).

To determine whether syntaxin and SNAP25 are required for the effect of VAMP7 on Ca2+ channels, we made use of the clostridial neurotoxins that cleave these t-SNAREs51. Botulinum neurotoxin C (BoNT/C) cleaves syntaxins52, and previous work has shown that it increases Ca2+ entry53,54. Complete loss of syntaxin 1 impairs the development of hippocampal cultures55, so we added the holotoxin one day before imaging, which results in the cleavage of syntaxin 1 without apparent toxicity (Extended Data Fig. 11d,e). BoNT/C increases presynaptic Ca2+ entry in primary neuronal culture stimulated at frequencies from 1 Hz to 50 Hz (Fig. 4d). With 1-AP stimulation, treatment of Vamp7-KO neurons with BoNT/C produces no further increase in Ca2+ entry, and loss of VAMP7 has no additional effect on the increase due to BoNT/C (Fig. 4d), indicating that VAMP7 and syntaxin are both required to inhibit Ca2+ entry triggered by single APs. Consistent with a common mechanism, the increased Ca2+ entry produced by BoNT/C in response to 1-AP stimulation is inhibited by ω-agatoxin IVA, but not by the N- and L-type channel inhibitors, supporting the disinhibition of mainly P/Q-type channels (Fig. 4e). Although BoNT/C also partially cleaves SNAP25 (Extended Data Fig. 11d), complete cleavage of SNAP25 by lentiviral transduction of the more selective BoNT/A light chain54 has minimal effect on Ca2+ entry in WT neurons (Fig. 4f and Extended Data Fig. 12a,b). In the Vamp7 KO, however, BoNT/A increases Ca2+ entry with low-frequency as well as with high-frequency stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 12c). Thus, in contrast to VAMP7 and syntaxin, which are both required to inhibit Ca2+ entry through P/Q-type channels, SNAP25 regulates Ca2+ entry only in the absence of VAMP7.

To understand how VAMP7 and syntaxin contribute to the inhibition of presynaptic Ca2+ channels, we first determined whether the proteins can form a complex. Expressed in HEK293 cells, Strep-tagged Cav2.1 immunoprecipitates VAMP7 when co-expressed with syntaxin 1 and the auxiliary Ca2+ channel subunits β3 and α2δ1 (Fig. 4g). SNAP25 can by itself associate with the channel, but—consistent with the selective effect of BoNT/C but not BoNT/A on Ca2+ entry in WT neurons—syntaxin 1, but not SNAP25, is required for co-immunoprecipitation of VAMP7 by Cav2.1 (Fig. 4g). Deletion of the synprint domain in Cav2.1 eliminates the interaction (Extended Data Fig. 13a). Extending the analysis to cortical neurons, immunoprecipitation of GFP–VAMP7 introduced into the Vamp7 KO pulls down Cav2.1, particularly when syntaxin 1 is also introduced (Extended Data Fig. 13b).

In addition to a SNARE domain, VAMP7 contains several domains not present in VAMP2 that might account for the inhibition of P/Q-type Ca2+ channels. In particular, VAMP7 contains a longin domain, which binds to AP-3 and influences SNARE-complex formation27,56. To test the role of this and other domains, we introduced luminally GFP-tagged WT and mutant VAMP2 and VAMP7 into cortical cultures from the Vamp7 KO. Figure 4h shows that VAMP2 interacts as anticipated with syntaxin and SNAP25 but does not immunoprecipitate endogenous Cav2.1. By contrast, VAMP7 immunoprecipitates Cav2.1 as well as the t-SNAREs, but the longin domain is not required (Fig. 4h). Chimerae of VAMP2 and VAMP7 in the SNARE domain are all sufficient to immunoprecipitate Cav2.1, but removing the linker between the longin and SNARE domains eliminates the interaction with Cav2.1. Consistent with this, replacing the linker in VAMP7 with the VAMP2 linker disrupts the interaction, and replacing the linker in VAMP2 with the VAMP7 linker promotes it, but none of these mutations interferes with the formation of SNARE complexes (Fig. 4h).

If the linker is required for interaction of VAMP7 with Cav2.1, it should also be required for VAMP7 to inhibit Ca2+ entry. Introducing WT and mutant VAMP7 into cultured hippocampal neurons from the Vamp7 KO, we find that deletion of the longin domain does not affect the inhibition of Ca2+ entry by VAMP7 for either 1 AP or 25-Hz stimulation (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 13c). However, a larger deletion that includes the linker eliminates the inhibition of Ca2+ entry. As shown above for the interaction with Cav2.1, replacing the linker in VAMP7 with the VAMP2 linker also eliminates the inhibition of Ca2+ entry, and replacing the linker in VAMP2 with the VAMP7 linker enables the inhibition (Fig. 4i). Interaction with Cav2.1 might retain VAMP7 at the plasma membrane, but the linker mutant defective in Cav2.1 binding exhibits exo- and endocytosis kinetics indistinguishable from those of the WT (Extended Data Fig. 13d). Thus, the VAMP7 linker is required specifically to bind to and inhibit P/Q-type Ca2+ channels.

Plasma membrane VAMP7 inhibits P/Q-type Ca2+ channels, but VAMP7 localizes predominantly to the subset of SVs made by AP-3. Because AP-3-derived SVs respond specifically to high-frequency stimulation3, we hypothesized that localization of VAMP7 to these SVs provides a mechanism for the activity-dependent delivery of VAMP7 to the plasma membrane, where it inhibits presynaptic Ca2+ entry. This hypothesis makes several predictions. First, strong stimulation should suppress release in a VAMP7-dependent manner. Indeed, repeated stimulation of WT hippocampal neurons at 50 Hz reduces the subsequent exocytic response monitored with VGLUT1–pH (Fig. 5a). Although there are many potential causes, loss of VAMP7 greatly reduces the decline (Fig. 5a), even though the initial response is greater in the absence of VAMP7. Extending the analysis to CA1 hippocampus, repeated 50-Hz bursts progressively reduce synaptic responses in WT slices, and loss of VAMP7 again attenuates the decline (Extended Data Fig. 14a). Loss of AP-3 has a similar but more modest effect because although delivery of VAMP7 to the plasma membrane is impaired, the absence of AP-3-derived SVs limits the exocytic response3 (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 14a). In the absence of VAMP7, loss of AP-3 limits release even at a low frequency (Extended Data Fig. 14b), presumably owing to the absence of low-release-probability SVs made by AP-3.

Second, silencing neurons should reduce the inhibition of Ca2+ entry by allowing the clearance of VAMP7 from the plasma membrane. To test this prediction, we used VAMP7–pH, quenching at low external pH and normalizing to total protein in NH4Cl to estimate the cell-surface fraction. As shown in Fig. 5c (and Extended Data Fig. 14c), neurons silenced for one day in glutamate receptor antagonists or TTX exhibit less cell-surface VAMP7 than do controls. The slow endocytic recycling of VAMP7 might contribute to the time course (hours) over which an increased release probability develops after silencing3 (Fig. 1a,b). Consistent with the inactivation of VAMP7 by silencing, silenced WT neurons show less colocalization of syntaxin with Cav2.1 (Extended Data Fig. 14d), similar to the Vamp7 KO (Fig. 4b).

Third, blocking regulated exocytosis should prevent the inhibition of Ca2+ entry produced by high-frequency stimulation. To test this, we silenced neurons after the cleavage of SNARE proteins with clostridial toxins (Fig. 5d). In contrast to WT neurons silenced for 24 h, which show suppression of Ca2+ entry by repeated burst stimulation at 25 Hz, neurons treated with BoNT/C show high Ca2+ entry that is not suppressed by burst stimulation (Fig. 5e). However, syntaxin is also required to inhibit Ca2+ entry. We thus expressed TeNT light chain, which inhibits exocytosis but does not increase Ca2+ entry under these conditions (Extended Data Fig. 8c,d). In this case, burst stimulation progressively reduces Ca2+ entry after silencing, but this occurs more slowly than it does in controls without TeNT (Fig. 5e). Previous work has shown that the exocytosis of AP-3-derived SVs depends predominantly on VAMP2, but residual release persists at a high frequency owing to the TeNT-resistant VAMP7 (ref. 3), and this presumably accounts for the suppression of Ca2+ entry by stimulation even with expression of TeNT. Indeed, substantial cell-surface VAMP7 persists despite TeNT expression (Extended Data Fig. 14e), and this might account for the unchanged presynaptic Ca2+ entry noted above with TeNT expression (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 8d). BoNT/A has only a modest effect on Ca2+ entry (Extended Data Fig. 12b) but mostly prevents the suppression of Ca2+ entry produced by burst stimulation after silencing (Fig. 5e). At a high frequency, BoNT/A also blocks SV exocytosis more completely than TeNT does (Extended Data Fig. 14f). Thus, exocytosis is required for the suppression of Ca2+ entry by high-frequency stimulation.

The analysis of Ca2+ entry should also enable us to monitor the activity-dependent inhibition of Ca2+ entry by VAMP7 in real time. To determine the kinetics of inhibition, we stimulated neurons in culture at 10 Hz for 5 s, then delivered single test pulses at 1 Hz. Figure 5f shows that the increased Ca2+ entry observed in silenced neurons does not decline substantially during the burst. Rather, the inhibition develops several seconds after stimulation. We infer that this delay reflects the time from the arrival of VAMP7 at the plasma membrane to the inhibition of P/Q-type Ca2+ channels. Finally, the increased Ca2+ entry produced by silencing should enable the recruitment of low-release-probability AP-3-derived SVs by low-frequency stimulation, as observed with loss of VAMP7 (Extended Data Fig. 14b). To monitor the fusion of AP-3-derived SVs, we used VAMP7–pH, and find that silencing specifically increases their release at low frequency (Extended Data Fig. 14g). Thus, silencing enables the recruitment of SVs that would otherwise require stronger stimulation, changing the set point for release.

If VAMP7 acts homeostatically to restrain transmitter release, its loss should increase the activity in neural networks. To test this role in vivo, we quantified expression of the immediate early gene Fos, which increases in response to recent neural activity57. In WT mice, expression of FOS is restricted to a small proportion of hippocampal neurons. By contrast, Vamp7-KO mice show a two- to fivefold increase in the number of FOS+ neurons across multiple hippocampal fields (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 15). In addition, FOS immunoreactivity per cell shifts upward. VAMP7 thus limits the strength of synaptic activation in vivo. However, the increased proportion of FOS+ cells in the KO suggests that VAMP7 also limits the number of postsynaptic neurons activated.

Our results elucidate a presynaptic form of homeostatic plasticity. Silencing cultured neurons increases the initial release probability by increasing presynaptic Ca2+ entry. High levels of activity reduce release and Ca2+ entry, and this occurs despite the continued presence of ionotropic glutamate receptor antagonists, indicating that induction occurs presynaptically rather than postsynaptically. The reduced release probability due to strong stimulation requires SVs made by AP-3, which respond specifically to high frequency. Among the proteins that localize specifically to AP-3-derived SVs, VAMP7 mediates the activity-dependent suppression of Ca2+ entry and release. VAMP7 acts by inhibiting P/Q-type presynaptic Ca2+ channels specifically, indicating that this form of homeostatic plasticity is also expressed presynaptically, and canonical v-SNARE VAMP2 does not have this role. Although VAMP7 at the plasma membrane inhibits P/Q-type channels, the predominant localization of VAMP7 to SVs made by AP-3 confers the activity dependence required for its role in homeostatic plasticity.

Previous work suggested that SNARE-complex formation might alleviate the inhibition of Ca2+ channels by syntaxin and SNAP25, but the role of v-SNAREs remained unclear48,49. We now find that the role of VAMP7 in Ca2+ entry requires syntaxin, not the other presynaptic t-SNARE SNAP25. VAMP7 promotes the association of syntaxin 1 with Cav2.1 in neurons, and syntaxin—but not SNAP25—promotes the association of VAMP7 with Cav2.1 by heterologous expression. Rather than relieving the inhibition of Ca2+ channels by t-SNAREs, VAMP7 thus serves as an activity-dependent rheostat for the inhibition of Ca2+ entry by a complex involving syntaxin. Both the interaction with Cav2.1 and the inhibition of Ca2+ channels depend on a unique, highly conserved linker between the longin and SNARE domains of VAMP7 that diverges in VAMP2, suggesting that although it associates with syntaxin, VAMP7 interacts directly with Cav2.1. Consistent with the specificity conferred by direct interaction, VAMP7 inhibits mainly P/Q-type channels, with little effect on N-type ones, but it also suppresses Ca2+ entry through L-type channels activated at a high frequency. Thus, we describe a new function for VAMP7 independent of its role in membrane fusion3.

How this mechanism influences information transfer is unclear. The increase in release probability due to protracted inactivity makes the synapse more sensitive to information about timing than about frequency of firing. Strong stimulation converts this high release probability to a low one, increasing the sensitivity of the synapse to frequency. The role of VAMP7 in P/Q-type Ca2+ channel regulation is also asymmetrical. Disinhibition of the channels requires hours of silencing, whereas strong stimulation inhibits the channels acutely. This asymmetry reflects the mechanisms involved: clearance of cell-surface VAMP7 by endocytosis, and delivery of VAMP7 by exocytosis. However, the asymmetry also contributes to the properties of this homeostatic mechanism, increasing the memory for recent activity as well as the speed at which this plasticity operates.

Our results show that the role of AP-3-derived SVs extends beyond transmitter release at high frequency. The exocytosis of AP-3-derived SVs in response to strong stimulation also increases the cell-surface expression of proteins that influence subsequent release. Although AP-3-derived SVs do not contribute to the initial release probability directly3, they influence the properties of release from SVs that are not made by AP-3. Regulation of cell-surface VAMP7 could thus contribute to the synaptic metaplasticity observed with changes in ongoing activity58.

In summary, activity regulates the cell-surface expression of VAMP7 by driving the exocytosis of AP-3-derived SVs. At the plasma membrane, VAMP7 associates with syntaxin to inhibit P/Q-type Ca2+ channels specifically, thereby influencing the probability of release to mediate a presynaptic form of homeostatic plasticity. VAMP7 is widely expressed by neurons, and P/Q-type channels are the main presynaptic Ca2+ channel at central synapses, which suggests that this mechanism confers homeostasis throughout the nervous system. In line with this, we observe similar effects of high-frequency stimulation on release at four excitatory synapses, and loss of VAMP7 eliminates the effect of high-frequency stimulation at each. Consistent with a general role, loss of VAMP7 increases FOS immunoreactivity in vivo. The increase in the proportion of FOS+ neurons further suggests that VAMP7 limits the number of presynaptic neurons that innervate a postsynaptic cell as well as the strength of their synaptic input.

After immunoprecipitation, the beads were washed four times with lysis buffer and the bound proteins were released in 1× SDS sample buffer (NEB, B7703S).

HEK293 cells or primary cortical neurons grown in a 10-cm dish were washed with 1× PBS and lysed for 2 h at 4 °C in 1 ml lysis buffer (1% DDM, 0.1% CHS, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA and 50 mM HEPES; pH 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitors. The cell lysate was sedimented at 16,000g for 10 min, and the supernatant was incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with 40 μl Strep-Tactin XT magnetic beads (IBA Lifesciences, 21613002) or 20 μL GFP-Trap Magnetic Particles M-270 (Proteintech, gtd). After immunoprecipitation, the beads were washed four times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were released in 1× SDS sample buffer before separation by SDS–PAGE.

Proteins and all blue pre-stained protein standards (Bio-Rad, 1610373) were separated by electrophoresis through 4–20% precast SDS polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad, 5671095) and electrotransferred to nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked for 30 min at room temperature in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline, pH 7.4, with 0.1% Tween-20) containing 5% non-fat dry milk. Primary antibodies were incubated in TBS-T containing 1% non-fat dry milk for 1.5 h at room temperature. After washing three times in TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies, IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000 dilution, 926-32210) and IRDye 680RD goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:20,000 dilution, 926-68071) (LI-COR Biosciences) for 1 h. After three washes, the blots were visualized by quantitative fluorescence imaging with a LI-COR system (Odyssey v.2.0.3, LI-COR Biosciences).

As previously described3, P21–P25 mice were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal administration of ketamine and xylazine and perfused transcardially with ice-cold cutting artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) composed of (in mM) 2.5 KCl, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgSO4, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 7 d-glucose, 210 sucrose and 1.3 ascorbic acid (305–310 mOsm; pH 7.4) saturated with 95% O2/5% CO2. Brains were rapidly removed and sectioned using a vibratome (Leica) in continuously aerated cutting aCSF into near-horizontal slices (300 μm) to record from Schaffer-collateral, mossy-fibre and perforant-path synapses, and into sagittal slices for corticothalamic and parallel-fibre synapses. The slices were then transferred to recording aCSF (in mM, 119 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2.5 CaCl2, 1.3 MgSO4, 1 NaH2PO4, 26.2 NaHCO3 and 11 d-glucose; 305–310 mOsm; pH 7.4) or aCSF containing 1.5 CaCl2 and 1 MgSO4 (shown in Extended Data Figs. 7 and 11a) saturated with 95% O2/5% CO2. Slices were incubated at 32 °C for 30 min and maintained at room temperature for a further 30 min before use. A cut was made between CA1 and CA3 to prevent recurrent network activity.

All electrophysiological recordings were performed on an upright Olympus BX51WI microscope. Brain slices were continuously perfused (3 ml per min) with recording aCSF saturated with 95% O2/5% CO2. Patch electrodes were fabricated from borosilicate glass capillaries using a Flaming/Brown puller (P-87, Sutter Instruments) and exhibited resistances of 3–5 MΩ when filled with the internal solution (in mM, 135 CsMeSO3, 10 HEPES, 8 NaCl, 0.3 EGTA, 4 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, 5 QX-314 and 0.1 spermine; 290–295 mOsm; pH 7.4). All recordings were done at 34–35 °C (except for those at 21–22 °C shown in Extended Data Fig. 11b,c). AMPAR-mediated responses were recorded in the presence of 10 µM APV to block NMDAR currents and 100 µM picrotoxin to block inhibition. To measure the initial release probability, NMDAR-mediated responses were collected in the presence of 100 µM picrotoxin, 10 µM 6,7-dinitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (DNQX) and 50 μM MK-801 after 10-min incubation in the bath. To test the role of presynaptic Ca2+ channels, slices were first recorded without Ca2+ channel blockers, then incubated for 30 min in blockers or vehicle (water or 0.01% DMSO) and recorded again. A Tungsten bipolar stimulating electrode (WE3ST30.1A3 SS, MicroProbes) was positioned in the stratum radiatum (to stimulate Schaffer-collateral synapses), the hilus adjacent to the dentate granule cell layer (mossy-fibre synapses), the outer molecular layer of the hippocampus (perforant-path synapses), the internal capsule (corticothalamic synapses) and parallel-fibre synapses of the cerebellum. AMPA EPSCs were recorded in whole-cell voltage-clamp mode held at −70 mV, and NMDA EPSCs were recorded at 40 mV. Amplitude was measured from the peak response. For the recordings in CA3, l-2-amino-4-phosphonobutyric acid (L-AP4, 10 μM) was added to the bath for 10 min at the end of the recording to confirm that the responses were mediated by mossy fibres. To ensure stable recording, membrane holding current, input resistance and pipette series resistance were monitored throughout. The series resistance typically ranged from 10 to 20 MΩ, and recordings were discarded if it exceeded 30 MΩ. Only recordings with a seal resistance larger than 1 GΩ were included in the analysis. Data were collected with a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), stored using Igor Pro v.6.3.4.1 (Wavemetrics), digitized at 10 kHz and filtered at 2 kHz.

Primary hippocampal neurons were fixed for 15 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS, followed by permeabilization and blocking in PBS for 30 min with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 4% normal goat serum (NGS). Cells were incubated with primary antibodies in PBS containing 1% NGS and 0.025% Triton X-100 for 1.5 h at room temperature. After three 10-min washes in PBS, cells were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and washed again as above. Coverslips were mounted in ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (P36961, Invitrogen).

To immunostain brain slices, mice were perfused sequentially with cold PBS followed by 4% PFA in PBS. The brains were dissected, post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA in PBS and cryoprotected in PBS containing, sequentially, 15% and 30% sucrose. Sagittal sections (35 μm) were cut using a Leica CM3050S cryostat, blocked and permeabilized in PBS containing 4% NGS and 0.2% Triton X-100 and incubated in primary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 1% NGS and 0.2% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C, followed by three 10-min washes in PBS. The sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark, and washed as above and then in PBS containing 1.5 μM Hoechst 33342 for 5 min before mounting as above.

The stained cells were imaged using a Nikon Ti Microscope equipped with a CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal module, a Plan Apo 40× or 60× oil-immersion objective and an Andor Zyla scientific CMOS camera. Super-resolution structured illumination microscopy (SIM) images were captured on a DeltaVision OMX SR microscope (GE Healthcare) with a Plan ApoN 60× 1.42 objective (Olympus), 1.516 index immersion oil and three PCO 15-bit CMOS cameras. Images were acquired using a 50-ms exposure, a pre-amplifier gain of 1 and variable EM gain. Alexa Fluor 488 fluorescence was excited with a 488-nm laser and detected with a 528/488-nm emission filter. Alexa Fluor 555 fluorescence was excited with a 568-nm laser and detected with a 609/37-nm filter. Alexa Fluor 647 fluorescence was excited with a 642-nm laser and emission collected with a 683/40-nm filter. Confocal images were collected using Micro-Manager (v.2.0-gamma). SIM imaging was controlled with OMX AquireSR software (GE Healthcare) and aligned using SoftWoRx (GE Healthcare). All images were processed and analysed with ImageJ v.1.54g (NIH).

Acquired fluorescence images were analysed using ImageJ, as previously described3. For pHluorin imaging, the ImageJ Time Series Analyzer (v.3.0) plug-in was used and individual boutons were identified manually on the basis of their fluorescence in NH4Cl and hence independent of the response to stimulation. Circular regions of interest (ROIs; 10 × 10 areas of pixel intensities) were positioned manually at the centre of fluorescent punctae. Background signal was estimated from three cell-free regions and subtracted from each trace. The integrated fluorescence in each bouton was then normalized to the baseline fluorescence before stimulation and to the maximal unquenched signal obtained in NH4Cl. For Fluo5F and iGluSnFR imaging experiments, circular ROIs (5 × 5 areas of pixel intensities) were used and the data were normalized to baseline before stimulation. All acquired data were included in the analysis; no randomization or group allocation was applied. Sample sizes were chosen to detect 1–2 s.d. with 80% probability. ROIs were selected blind to the results. Analysis of the whole-cell recording was not performed blind to genotype or experimental condition but no recordings were excluded. Curve fitting and quantitative analysis were performed in GraphPad Prism (v.11.0.2).

No formal statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size, but the number of replicates in this study is comparable with those in previous reports3,4. Quantification was performed using data collected from multiple coverslips derived from at least three independent experimental preparations, as detailed in the Source Data. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism. Data generally followed a normal distribution and were evaluated by two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests or one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc comparisons. All summary values are presented as mean ± s.e.m., and n denotes either the number of coverslips or the number of recorded cells.
