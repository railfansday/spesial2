From attic insulation to water heater settings: 10 ways to lower winter energy bills

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 ways to cut your heating bill, from thermostat setbacks to attic insulation and duct sealing, target the places homes lose warmth in winter

A heating bill is, in large part, a record of warmth leaving a house. Heated air slips out through gaps around doors and windows. It rises into an attic that lacks enough insulation. It passes through glass at night, leaks from ductwork hidden in crawl spaces and drifts up an open chimney. The rest can reflect equipment working harder than it has to, such as a furnace straining against a clogged filter.

A furnace does not produce heat for its own sake. It replaces the heat a building loses to the outdoors. Every route that loss takes is a place where a household can intervene, and several of those steps need no contractor.

The 10 approaches in this slideshow fall into three broad groups. The first covers controls, meaning how and when a heating system runs. A programmable or smart thermostat can lower the temperature during sleep or time away. A ceiling fan set to spin clockwise at low speed in winter moves warm air that collects near the ceiling back toward the floor.

The second group covers the building envelope, the boundary between indoors and outdoors. Weatherstripping and caulk close drafts. Attic insulation slows the heat that rises toward the roof. Cellular shades, insulated curtains and clear window film reduce what escapes through glass.

The third group covers equipment and daily habits. Regular filter changes keep air moving freely through a forced-air furnace. An annual professional tune-up checks combustion and carbon monoxide safety. Sealed and insulated ducts deliver more heated air to the rooms they serve. A space heater can warm one occupied room while the rest of the house stays cooler, provided it is used safely. Closing the fireplace damper stops warm air from escaping up the flue.

One item addresses a different line on the energy bill. Water heating is a separate household cost from space heating. The same principle of cutting wasted heat applies to the tank and its pipes.

Which ways to cut your heating bill make sense depends on the home. A drafty older house with single-pane windows has different weak points from a newer house with ducts in an unheated attic. Climate, fuel type and heating system all shape which steps matter.

This list is a selection of approaches, not a ranking. Each slide explains how one fix works and what limits or safety points apply.

A programmable or smart thermostat lowers heating costs by dropping the indoor temperature on a schedule while people sleep or are away. The furnace, boiler or heat pump then runs less, and nobody has to remember to turn the dial down.

The mechanism is basic physics. A house loses heat to the outdoors at a rate that depends on the gap between the indoor and outdoor temperatures. When that gap narrows, heat escapes through walls, windows and the roof more slowly. A cooler house loses less energy for as long as the setback lasts. The heating system then has less lost heat to replace.

Longer setbacks have a larger effect than short ones. The overnight hours and the workday are the two long stretches most households can use. A thermostat that covers both on a fixed schedule makes the lower setting automatic every day.

A common question is whether warming a cold house back up cancels out the savings. The energy needed to reheat the rooms roughly matches the energy saved while the temperature drifted down. The house also loses less heat during every hour it sits at the lower setting. That reduced loss is where the net saving comes from. The U.S. Department of Energy publishes guidance on thermostat setbacks that addresses this point.

The two types of device differ mainly in convenience. A programmable thermostat follows a schedule the owner enters, often with separate settings for weekdays and weekends. A smart thermostat connects to home Wi-Fi and can be adjusted from a phone app. Some smart models learn a household's patterns or use a phone's location to detect when everyone has left.

Timing the warm-up matters for comfort. Setting the heat to rise shortly before the alarm goes off or before people return home means the house is warm when it is occupied. It also keeps the setback in place for as long as possible.

Heat pump owners have one extra consideration. Some heat pump systems switch to backup electric resistance heat when recovering from a deep setback. Many thermostats built for heat pumps manage that recovery more gradually to limit use of the backup heat.

Placement also affects how well any thermostat works. A unit on an interior wall reads the room more accurately. One near a drafty door, a sunny window or a heat source can misjudge the temperature and run the system at the wrong times.

Sealing air leaks with weatherstripping, caulk and a door sweep keeps heated air inside the house. Weatherstripping belongs on parts that move, caulk fills gaps in joints that stay fixed, and a sweep closes the space under an exterior door.

Drafts rarely come from one obvious hole. They gather around exterior doors and the sashes of windows that open. They also form around attic hatches and at utility penetrations, the spots where pipes, wires and vents pass through walls, floors and ceilings. Each gap may look minor on its own. As a group, these gaps give warm indoor air many exits, and cold outdoor air moves in to replace it.

Finding the leaks comes first. On a windy day, hold a hand or a lit incense stick near a suspected gap. A cool stream of air, or smoke that bends sideways, marks a leak. Daylight visible around the edges of a closed door points to the same problem.

Weatherstripping suits doors and operable windows because it compresses when they close and springs back when they open. It comes in several forms, including adhesive-backed foam tape, V-shaped vinyl or metal strips and felt. The right choice seals the gap fully without making the door or window hard to shut. A clean, dry surface helps adhesive strips stay in place through the season.

Caulk handles joints that never move. These include the seam where window trim meets the wall and gaps around pipes that enter through exterior walls. Old, cracked caulk should come out before a new continuous bead goes in. Silicone and acrylic latex are common types. The label states whether a product works indoors, outdoors or under paint.

A door sweep covers the gap at the bottom of an exterior door, which frame weatherstripping does not reach. Sweeps are strips of rubber, vinyl or brush, usually fastened along the lower edge of the door's inside face.

The attic hatch needs the same treatment. Because it lifts open, it counts as a moving part. Weatherstripping around its rim stops warm air from slipping into the attic.

Homes with fuel-burning furnaces, boilers or water heaters need enough air for safe combustion. A heating professional can confirm that a large sealing project leaves those appliances adequate ventilation.

Topping up attic insulation slows one of the main routes heat takes out of a house. Warm air rises toward the top-floor ceiling. In an attic with thin or patchy insulation, that heat passes into the roof space and out to the winter air.

Insulation is rated by R-value, a measure of how well a material resists the flow of heat. A higher R-value means more resistance. The rating depends on both the material and its thickness. A deeper layer of the same product delivers a higher total R-value.

The right target is not the same everywhere. Recommended attic R-values vary by climate zone, and colder regions call for more resistance than mild ones. The U.S. Department of Energy publishes recommended levels by zone. Homeowners can match their location to that guidance instead of relying on a single figure meant for every house.

A look into the attic shows whether the existing layer is thin. If the tops of the wooden floor joists are visible, the insulation sits below joist height. That usually means there is room to add more. Gaps, compressed patches and sections pushed aside during past repair work also reduce how well the layer performs.

Two materials are common in attic upgrades. Fiberglass or mineral wool batts are pre-cut blankets that fit between joists. Loose-fill insulation, usually cellulose or fiberglass, is blown in with a machine and settles around wiring and awkward framing. New material can generally be laid over old, as long as the existing layer is dry and free of mold.

Airflow and moisture need attention during the job. Vents at the eaves, called soffit vents, let outside air into the attic, and insulation should not block them. Plastic or foam baffles hold a channel open between the roof deck and the insulation so that air can keep moving through.

Some conditions call for a professional first. Older recessed light fixtures that are not rated for insulation contact need clearance to avoid overheating. Knob-and-tube wiring, found in some older homes, should not be buried under new material. Gray-brown, pebble-like vermiculite insulation may contain asbestos. It should be left undisturbed until a trained professional assesses it.

Anyone working in the attic should step only on joists or on boards laid across them. The ceiling drywall between joists is not built to hold a person's weight.

Closing cellular shades or insulated curtains at night slows the heat escaping through window glass. Opening them on sunny days lets solar warmth into the room. For single-pane windows, clear plastic shrink film adds an insulating layer of air at low cost.

Glass conducts heat far more readily than an insulated wall. On a cold night, a window becomes one of the coldest surfaces in a room. Warm indoor air touches the pane, cools and sinks toward the floor. That cycle pulls heat out of the room. It can also leave people sitting near the window feeling a chill even when the thermostat reads normal.

Window coverings work by placing a barrier between the room and that cold surface. Cellular shades, also called honeycomb shades, are made from fabric folded into hexagonal cells. Each cell holds still air, and still air resists the flow of heat. Insulated curtains do a similar job with thick fabric or a lined backing.

Fit also affects how well a covering works. A covering that leaves wide gaps at the sides or top lets room air circulate behind it and reach the glass. Curtains that hang close to the wall and run to the sill or floor keep more of that air in place. Shades mounted inside a window frame work best when they sit snugly against it.

Timing turns these coverings into a daily routine. Close them at dusk, when outdoor temperatures fall and the sun no longer adds warmth. Leave them closed through the night.

Open them again on sunny days. Sunlight passing through glass warms floors, furniture and walls, which then release that heat into the room. This is passive solar heating, and it requires no fuel. In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing windows receive the most direct winter sun. In the Southern Hemisphere, north-facing windows play that role. East- and west-facing glass collects direct sun for only part of the day. Coverings on those windows can stay open for a shorter stretch.

Shrink film addresses a different weakness. Kits typically include clear plastic sheeting and double-sided tape. The tape goes on the interior window frame, and the film is pressed onto it. A hair dryer then tightens the plastic until it is smooth and clear. The result is a sealed layer of air between the film and the glass, which acts much like a second pane.

That makes film a low-cost option for single-pane windows, which lack the built-in gap of double-pane units. It suits renters and anyone not ready to replace windows. The film keeps a window from opening while it is in place. It belongs on windows that will stay shut for the season, and it is meant to be removed when the heating season ends.

A clean filter and a yearly professional inspection keep a forced-air furnace from working harder than it needs to. A clogged filter restricts airflow, and a tune-up checks combustion and safety, including carbon monoxide risks.

The filter sits in the path of the air the system pulls back from the house before reheating it. Depending on the setup, it slides into a slot on the furnace cabinet or sits behind a grille on a return air vent. As it traps dust, pet hair and other particles, the material gradually fills up. Air then has a harder time passing through.

A forced-air system depends on moving a steady volume of air across its heat source and out through the ducts. When less air gets through, the blower strains. The system then runs longer to deliver the same warmth to each room. That extra run time adds to winter energy bills.

A common habit is to check the filter once a month during the heating season. Pull it out and hold it up to a light. If the material looks gray and caked, or light barely passes through, it is due for a change. Replacement intervals vary by filter type, so follow the schedule the manufacturer prints on the packaging or frame.

The frame also lists the filter's dimensions, which makes buying the right replacement simpler. Most filters carry an arrow showing which way air should flow. That arrow should point toward the furnace, away from the return duct. A filter in the wrong size can leave gaps that let air slip around it.

The annual tune-up covers what a homeowner cannot easily check. A qualified technician inspects how the furnace burns its fuel. The technician also checks whether combustion gases are venting out of the house as designed.

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. A problem with combustion or venting can go unnoticed without testing. A carbon monoxide alarm near sleeping areas adds a second layer of protection between inspections. Booking the visit before the first cold snap leaves time for any repair before the furnace runs every day.

Leaky ductwork in a forced-air heating system loses warm air before it reaches the rooms it is meant to heat. Sealing duct joints with mastic or metal-backed foil tape, and insulating ducts that run through unheated spaces, keeps more of that air on its intended path.

Picture a house with a furnace in the basement or garage. Heated air travels through a network of metal or flexible ducts to registers in each room. Every loose joint, gap or disconnected section along the way lets some of that air escape. When the leak sits in an attic, crawl space or garage, the warm air ends up in a space nobody is trying to heat.

Uneven temperatures can hint at duct trouble. A bedroom at the end of a long duct run may stay chilly while rooms nearer the furnace feel comfortable. A common response is to nudge the thermostat up. That makes the whole system run longer to make up for air lost in transit.

Exposed ducts are the place to start. Look along accessible runs in the attic, basement, crawl space and garage. Check for joints that have separated, seams that are open and connections at the registers or the furnace that look loose.

Mastic is a thick paste that is brushed or spread over seams and hardens into a durable seal. Metal-backed foil tape is the other standard option for joints. Ordinary cloth duct tape, despite its name, is not the usual recommendation for sealing ducts. Its adhesive tends to dry out and let go after long exposure to heat.

Insulation is the second half of the job. A sealed duct running through a cold attic or crawl space still sheds heat through its walls. Wrapping those runs in duct insulation slows that loss. Ducts already inside heated living space gain less from insulation. Any heat they give off stays in the house.

Many duct runs are hidden inside walls, floors and ceilings, where a homeowner cannot easily reach them. A heating contractor can assess and seal those sections. Homes heated by boilers and radiators, or by other systems without ductwork, have no ducts to seal. This step applies only to forced-air setups.

Switching a ceiling fan to turn clockwise at low speed in winter pushes warm air that has gathered near the ceiling back down into the room. The fan does not heat anything. It moves heat the furnace already produced to where people actually sit.

Warm air is less dense than cool air, so it rises and collects in a layer under the ceiling. That layering is called stratification. It is most pronounced in rooms with high, vaulted or cathedral ceilings, and in open stairwells and lofts. In those spaces, the air a few feet above the floor can feel cooler than the air near the top of the room.

Many ceiling fans have a small reverse switch on the motor housing, just above the blades. Some models change direction through a wall control, a remote or a manufacturer's app instead. Turn the fan off and let the blades stop completely before flipping a manual switch.

Direction is judged by standing underneath and looking up. In the winter setting, the blades turn clockwise. At low speed, this draws room air upward toward the ceiling. The warm layer is then displaced outward and travels down along the walls into the living space.

Fan speed also matters. A fast spin creates a breeze, and moving air across skin makes people feel cooler. That wind-chill effect is the reason fans run counterclockwise at higher speeds in summer. In winter, the goal is gentle circulation without a noticeable draft.

The mixing also changes what a wall thermostat reads. A thermostat mounted well below the ceiling measures the air around it, not the warm pocket overhead. Moving that warmer air down narrows the gap between floor and ceiling temperatures.

A ceiling fan still draws electricity while it runs. It does not change the temperature of an empty room. Switching it off when nobody is there avoids using power for no benefit. When the weather turns warm again, flip the switch back to counterclockwise for the cooling breeze.

Setting a tank water heater to 120 degrees Fahrenheit trims a household energy cost that is separate from space heating. The U.S. Department of Energy has suggested that setting for many homes. Insulating hot water pipes and older tanks also cuts the heat they lose while idle.

Water heating runs all year and often appears on the same gas or electric bill as the furnace. It is still a separate load. Turning down the thermostat on the wall does nothing for it.

A conventional storage heater keeps a full tank of water hot around the clock. Heat moves from that water through the tank walls into the surrounding room. The burner or heating element then switches on again to replace it. This cycle is called standby heat loss. The hotter the water is kept, the faster it gives up heat to a cold basement, utility closet or garage.

Incoming water from the main is often colder in winter. That leaves the heater more work to do each time the tank refills. A lower setpoint reduces how far the water has to be raised.

On many gas models, the temperature dial sits on the control valve near the base of the tank. Electric models often place their thermostats behind access panels on the side, and some have two. Power to an electric unit should be shut off at the breaker before any panel comes off. The owner's manual lists the steps for each model. Some dials show labels rather than degrees. A kitchen thermometer held under a hot tap a few hours after an adjustment shows the real temperature.

Pipe insulation deals with a second route for lost heat. Hot water cools as it travels through bare pipe, especially through unheated crawl spaces and basements. Pre-slit foam sleeves sized to the pipe slip over it and close along the seam. Exposed runs near the heater are the easiest to reach. On gas heaters, insulation should stay clear of the flue.

A tank blanket is an insulating jacket that wraps around the outside of the tank. It suits older models with less built-in insulation. Many newer tanks are already well insulated, and the label or owner's manual states whether a blanket is appropriate. On gas units, a blanket must not cover the top of the tank, the burner compartment, the flue or the controls. On electric units, the access panels and thermostat covers need to stay reachable.

A space heater can warm the one room people are actually using while the whole-house thermostat stays at a lower setting. This approach, often called zone heating, trims winter energy bills only when the central system is turned down to match.

The logic comes down to where the heat goes. A central furnace or heat pump warms every room it serves, including guest rooms, hallways and spare bedrooms that may sit empty for most of the day. Zone heating concentrates warmth in an occupied space, such as a home office during work hours or a living room in the evening. Less energy goes into rooms no one is in.

The savings depend on that trade. A space heater uses energy of its own. Running one while the central system holds the house at its usual temperature adds to the total bill rather than reducing it. Zone heating pays off when the heater takes over some of the furnace's work, not when it runs alongside it.

Room size and layout shape how well a portable heater performs. A small unit suits a single room with the door closed. One heater placed on an open-plan floor tends to run constantly without making the larger space comfortable.

Fire safety is the main limitation. Space heaters need clear space around them, away from curtains, bedding, sofas, paper and anything else that can burn. They should be plugged directly into a wall outlet. Extension cords and power strips are not built for a heater's sustained electrical load and can overheat. Many current models include a tip-over switch and automatic overheat shutoff, and buyers can confirm those features on the product label or listing. A heater should be turned off when everyone leaves the room or goes to sleep.

A related idea comes with a caution. Some households try to get the same effect by closing the heating vents in rooms they don't use. In some forced-air systems, closed vents can upset the air pressure in the ductwork. Anyone considering it should ask a heating technician first. Closing vents is not a straightforward substitute for a lower thermostat setting paired with a heater in the occupied room.

A fireplace that is not in use can still pull heat out of a home. Closing the damper whenever no fire is burning stops warm indoor air from rising up the chimney and escaping outdoors.

A chimney is built to carry hot gases up and away from the house. That same design works against a household the rest of the time. Warm air is less dense than cold air. Heated room air drifts toward an open flue and exits through the top. An open damper over a cold hearth turns the chimney into a standing vent to the outside.

The damper is a movable metal plate or flap inside the chimney. Many fireplaces have a throat damper, which sits just above the firebox and is worked by a handle, lever or chain. Some homes have a top-sealing damper instead. It is mounted at the top of the chimney and opened with a cable that runs down into the firebox.

Timing matters in both directions. The damper has to stay open while a fire burns, including while embers still smolder. Closing it too early can send smoke and carbon monoxide into the room. Once the fire is completely out and the ashes are cold, the damper should be shut.

Using the fireplace carries its own heating cost. A burning wood fire needs air to keep going, and it draws that air from the room. Much of that air has already been warmed by the furnace or boiler. It goes up the chimney with the smoke. Colder outdoor air then leaks into the house to replace it.

Several add-ons reduce the loss. Glass fireplace doors close off the opening of the firebox and limit how much room air reaches the flue. An old throat damper that has warped or rusted may no longer seal well. A tight-fitting replacement closes that gap. A chimney balloon or plug is an inflatable or foam device placed inside the flue. It blocks airflow in a fireplace that sits unused for long stretches.

A chimney balloon or plug must come out before anyone lights a fire. Leaving one in place blocks the path smoke needs to leave the house.

Safety checks belong in the same routine. Annual chimney inspection is standard safety guidance for any home with a working fireplace. An inspection looks for blockages, damage and creosote, a flammable residue that wood smoke leaves on chimney walls. It is also a chance to confirm that the damper opens and closes fully before the heating season begins.
