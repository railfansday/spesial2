Why last year's songs win this year's Grammys: the eligibility window, explained

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The Grammy eligibility window runs from late summer to late summer, which is why songs released last year often collect trophies at this year's ceremony

A Grammy handed out in February usually rewards music that came out the year before. The trophy carries one year's date, while the songs behind it often belong to the previous one. Release dates and trophy years can look out of sync as a result.

The explanation sits in the calendar the Recording Academy uses. Its eligibility period does not run from January to December. It opens on Aug. 31 or Sept. 1 and closes on Aug. 30 of the following year. Any album, single or track released inside those dates can compete at the next ceremony.

The second piece of the puzzle is naming. Each ceremony takes the year in which it is held, not the year in which its music was released. A show staged in early 2026 therefore honors work that reached listeners across parts of 2024 and 2025. The label points forward, while the music looks back.

Put those two rules together and the timing starts to make sense. A record released in the spring sits inside one window. It then waits through the fall nominations, and any trophies are handed out the following winter. A record released just after the summer cutoff faces a much longer wait. It rolls into the next cycle entirely.

The Grammy eligibility window explains why a song can win a trophy many months after listeners first heard it. It also shapes the schedule between release and ceremony. After the window closes, the Recording Academy needs time for entries, screening, voting rounds and a nominations announcement.

The 10 slides that follow are a selection of key rules and examples, not a complete rulebook. They define the eligibility period and explain its late-summer cutoff. They walk through the windows for the 2025 and 2026 ceremonies, along with the event calendar from nominations to the show. Recent winners, including Beyoncé's Cowboy Carter and Kendrick Lamar's "Not Like Us," show the rules in practice. The final slides cover releases that miss the cutoff and the pandemic years, when ceremony dates moved but the window logic held.

The Grammy Awards honor music released within a fixed eligibility period, not within a calendar year. That period runs from Aug. 31 or Sept. 1 through Aug. 30 of the following year.

The Recording Academy, the organization behind the Grammys, sets the dates for each awards cycle. The opening day falls on either Aug. 31 or Sept. 1, depending on the cycle. The closing day is always Aug. 30.

Each cycle uses only one of those two start dates. The correct window for a given year is the one the academy sets for that cycle.

A release date decides where a song or album lands. Its position within the window determines which awards cycle it belongs to.

The year on a record's release does not settle the question alone. Two albums released in the same calendar year can belong to different Grammy cycles. The deciding factor is which side of the Aug. 30 close each one falls on.

Consider an album released in January and another released in December of the same year. The January album sits in a window that opened the previous late summer. The December album sits in a window that opened only months earlier. They share a release year but compete at different ceremonies.

Each window covers about a year of releases. Every ceremony draws its contenders from a single window. That pairing of one window with one ceremony is the basic unit of the Grammy calendar.

The Recording Academy ends each Grammy eligibility period in late summer, on Aug. 30. That early close leaves room to process submissions and finish the judging before a ceremony in late winter.

A January-to-December window would not fit that schedule. Grammy nominations are announced in the fall. Under a calendar-year window, eligibility would still be open on the day the nominees were named.

Music released in November or December would then arrive after the list was public. Those releases could not be weighed against work that had already been nominated.

A late-summer close moves the end of the window ahead of the fall. Every release in a given cycle is already out before the screening and voting work begins. The process runs on a fixed set of music rather than one that keeps growing.

The ceremony sits at the far end of that sequence. About five months separate the Aug. 30 close from a ceremony in late January or early February. That stretch holds every step between the final eligible release date and the trophies.

One result is a split calendar year. Music released from January through Aug. 30 belongs to one Grammy cycle. Music released later in the same calendar year belongs to the next one. A December release, for example, shares a cycle with music that comes out the following summer.

Two albums released a few months apart in the same year can therefore compete at different ceremonies. The deciding factor is which side of the late-summer cutoff each release date falls on. The year printed on the release does not settle it.

The August-to-August structure is why the Grammy year and the calendar year do not line up. Each cycle covers roughly the last four months of one calendar year. It then runs through nearly the first eight months of the next.

The year in a Grammy ceremony's name is the year the show is held, not the year the winning music was released. A trophy from the 2020 Grammys can honor work that came out in 2019.

Billie Eilish's album When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go? shows the gap. It was released on March 29, 2019. It won at the ceremony held on Jan. 26, 2020.

About 10 months separated the album's release from that ceremony. The record and its awards therefore sit in different calendar years. Nothing about that timing was unusual. A spring release lands well before the late-summer close of a Grammy eligibility period. The album competed at the first ceremony after its release.

The label on the show reflects its place on the calendar. The ceremony takes place in the first months of a year, and the event takes the name of that year. Most of the eligibility period behind it falls in the calendar year before.

This convention can trip up anyone searching for a given year's award-winning music. Someone looking for Grammy winners among 2019 releases should check the 2020 ceremony first. A release from late in 2019 may instead appear in a later year's results.

The awards also carry an edition number alongside the year, such as the 67th or the 68th. The ordinal counts how many shows have taken place. The year marks when each one happened. Neither one identifies the release year of the honored music.

A simple rule follows from the naming system. To find when most of a ceremony's honored music came out, look at the year before the one in its title. For a show held in 2026, that means looking first at 2025. Music released in the fall two years before the ceremony is the main exception to that shortcut.

The 67th Annual Grammy Awards, held on Feb. 2, 2025, covered music released from Sept. 1, 2023, through Aug. 30, 2024. That single window spanned parts of two calendar years.

The split between those years was uneven. Four months of the window came from late 2023, covering September through December. The other eight came from 2024, running from January through August. Two-thirds of the window therefore fell in 2024, the year before the show. None of it came from 2025, the year in the ceremony's name.

That structure decided which records counted as rivals. A song released in the first week of September 2023 competed against a song released in the last week of August 2024. Nearly a full year separated the two releases. Both still sat in the same pool for the same February ceremony.

A December 2023 release shows how the window differs from a calendar year. A list limited to 2024 releases would leave it off. It was still eligible at the 2025 ceremony. It came out after the Sept. 1, 2023, opening date. The same was true of releases from September, October and November 2023.

Music from early 2023 sat outside this window entirely. An album released in the spring of 2023 came out before the Sept. 1 opening date. It belonged to the previous cycle.

For the 2025 show, the final eligible release day was Aug. 30, 2024. The ceremony followed on Feb. 2, 2025, a little over five months later.

Checking whether a record could compete at the 2025 show required its exact release date, not just its release year. Any date from Sept. 1, 2023, through Aug. 30, 2024, made it eligible. Any date outside that span did not. The 2025 trophies therefore went to music from the fall of 2023 through the summer of 2024.

The 68th Grammy Awards, held on Feb. 1, 2026, covered music released from Aug. 31, 2024, through Aug. 30, 2025. A song that came out in early 2025 therefore competed at the 2026 ceremony.

The window opened in late summer 2024. About four months of eligible releases came from that year, running from Aug. 31 through Dec. 31. Nearly eight more months came from 2025, running from Jan. 1 through Aug. 30.

That split put an album released in October 2024 and a single released in June 2025 into the same pool. Both were eligible for the same awards at the February 2026 show.

The 68th window opened the day after the previous cycle closed. No release date fell into a gap between the two periods.

The last eligible release date was Aug. 30, 2025. A track that came out on that day still qualified for the 2026 ceremony.

The wait from release to ceremony varied inside this single window. A release from Jan. 1, 2025, reached the Feb. 1, 2026, show 13 months later. A release from the final eligible day waited about five months.

Grammy nominations arrive in the fall, and the ceremony follows in late winter. For the 68th Grammy Awards, the Recording Academy announced nominations on Nov. 7, 2025, ahead of a ceremony held on Feb. 1, 2026.

The clock on that schedule starts when the eligibility period closes. For the 68th awards, Aug. 30, 2025, was the last eligible release date. Nothing released after that day could compete at the February show.

The first stretch of the calendar runs from that cutoff to the nominations announcement. Entries are submitted and screened, and voting rounds then produce the nominee lists.

In the 68th cycle, that stretch lasted 69 days, from Aug. 30 to Nov. 7, 2025. That works out to just under 10 weeks between a closed window and a public list of nominees.

The second stretch runs from the nominations announcement to the ceremony itself. In the 68th cycle, it covered 86 days, from Nov. 7, 2025, to Feb. 1, 2026. That is just over 12 weeks, or nearly three months.

The post-nomination stretch was the longer of the two. It ran 17 days longer than the period between the cutoff and the nominations announcement.

Taken together, the 68th cycle spanned 155 days from the last eligible release date to the night of the show. About 45% of that time came before nominations, and about 55% came after.

The ceremony date also held close to the previous year's. The 67th awards took place on Feb. 2, 2025, so the two shows fell 364 days apart.

That gap equals exactly 52 weeks. Both ceremonies therefore landed on the same day of the week, a Sunday.

For nominees, the November announcement is the first public confirmation of which releases from the eligibility period made the nominee lists. Winners in each category are not named until the February ceremony.

Beyoncé's Cowboy Carter came out on March 29, 2024. It won Album of the Year at the Grammy ceremony held in February 2025, in the calendar year after its release.

That gap follows from the Recording Academy's late-summer cutoff. Cowboy Carter competed at the 67th Annual Grammy Awards, held on Feb. 2, 2025. A March 29 release date put it well inside the window that fed that show.

The album sat near the middle of its eligibility period. It arrived about seven months after the period opened and about five months before it closed. It was neither an early entry in the cycle nor a last-minute one.

That position also set the length of its wait. About 10 months separated the album's release from the night it won. Roughly half of that stretch came before the cutoff, while the album was still inside its eligible span. The other half, roughly five months, came after the window shut at the end of August.

The timing could have played out differently. Had Cowboy Carter arrived after Aug. 30, 2024, it would have fallen into the following cycle instead. That cycle led to the 2026 ceremony, a full year later than the show where it won.

Cowboy Carter is a 2024 album holding a 2025 Album of the Year trophy. Its path shows how a spring release and a winter ceremony fit inside one Grammy cycle. The late-summer cutoff, not the calendar year, decides which ceremony a record reaches.

Kendrick Lamar's "Not Like Us" won both Record of the Year and Song of the Year at the 2025 Grammys. The single came out on May 4, 2024. Each award recognizes a different contribution to the same track.

The release date set the timing. May 4, 2024, fell inside the eligibility window that closed on Aug. 30, 2024. That placed the single at the ceremony held on Feb. 2, 2025. About nine months passed between the release and the two wins.

The category names sound alike, but they honor different people. Song of the Year goes to a track's songwriters. It recognizes the composition — the work as written.

Record of the Year goes to a specific recording. Its honorees are the performers, producers and engineers behind that recording. The award covers the finished version listeners hear, not the underlying song.

That split determines whose names are attached to each trophy. A Song of the Year win credits the people who wrote the track. A Record of the Year win credits the team that made the recording. A track that takes both has been recognized once for its writing and once for its recording.

Both categories draw on the same eligibility window. The release date that placed "Not Like Us" in contention for Song of the Year also placed it in contention for Record of the Year. Both trophies were handed out on the same night.

The shared window means one release date governs a track's eligibility in both categories at once. A track released after the cutoff would wait for the next ceremony in each category, not in just one of them.

The distinction also explains why a winning track's credits can list more people than its performer. Songwriting and recording are separate jobs, and the Grammys reward them in separate categories. One person can hold both roles, or the two jobs can belong to entirely different people.

A song or album released after Aug. 30 skips the next Grammy ceremony and falls into the following eligibility window. A fall release can therefore wait nearly a year and a half to reach a Grammy stage.

The cutoff is a fixed line on the calendar. Music that arrives on Aug. 30 still makes the closing window. Music that arrives a day later starts the count toward a new cycle.

Release dates in late summer 2024 show how sharp that line is. A track released on Aug. 30, 2024, was eligible for the 67th Annual Grammy Awards. That ceremony took place on Feb. 2, 2025, about five months after the track came out.

A track released one day later, on Aug. 31, 2024, belonged to the next cycle. It counted toward the 68th Grammy Awards instead. That ceremony was held on Feb. 1, 2026, roughly 17 months after the release.

Two releases separated by a single day ended up a full ceremony apart. The earlier one could have collected a trophy in February 2025. The later one had to wait for the following February, one day short of a year after that show.

The delay also stretches the time before a nomination. A track released on Aug. 31, 2024, waited more than 14 months to learn whether it had been nominated. Awards night followed almost three months after that announcement.

The same logic covers the rest of that autumn. A song released in September 2024 competed at the 2026 Grammys, not the 2025 show. Its release date fell after the cutoff for the earlier ceremony.

The rule applies to every release in the same way. Songs that come out a few weeks apart in late summer and early fall can land in separate Grammy cycles. Each cycle has its own nominations announcement and its own ceremony.

The pandemic pushed two Grammy ceremonies off their usual late-winter schedule. The 2021 show was held on March 14, and the 2022 show was postponed from Jan. 31 to April 3. The logic of the eligibility window stayed the same both times.

The March 14 date placed the 2021 ceremony in mid-March. That was later than the late-winter slot the Grammys usually fill.

The 2022 ceremony already had a date on the calendar when the delay came. The move from Jan. 31 to April 3 amounted to 62 days, or roughly nine weeks. It turned a late-January show into an early-spring one.

A ceremony date and an eligibility window are two separate parts of the Grammy calendar. The window decides which releases are allowed to compete. The ceremony decides when the winners are announced on stage. Moving the second part did not reopen or extend the first.

That separation had a direct consequence for the contest. A later show did not, by itself, add music to the field. The pool of contenders was set by release dates, not by the date of the broadcast. A record that came out after its window closed did not gain entry because the show moved later.

The same rule worked in the other direction. A record that qualified did not lose its place because the ceremony moved. Its eligibility rested on when it was released, and that date did not change.

For artists and audiences, the practical effect was a longer wait. The gap between a song's release and its trophy grew by however long the show was delayed. In 2022, that added wait matched the full span of the postponement.

The two delays show that a ceremony date can move independently of the eligibility rules. The Recording Academy kept the two questions apart. When the music came out still determined eligibility. When the show happened affected only the date the results became public.
