Mortgage lock in decay is measurable, the annual unlock rate is 5.8%

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Mortgage lock in is suppressing turnover, with existing sales near 4.2 million versus a 5 million norm. The article lays out 4 life event driven channels that can unlock listings, with a cited 5.8% annual decay rate.  AI Summary

Mortgage lock-in is real and measurable. It is also decaying on its own, because death, divorce, diapers, and diplomas keep arriving on schedule. Here is how to work that schedule.

There is a number floating around this fall that explains the mood in most sales meetings. Research by Compass economist Jonah Coste calculates that mortgage rate lock-in is preventing about 870,000 home sales in 2026 that would otherwise take place, with existing home sales running near 4.2 million against a long-run norm of 5 million. With the 30-year rate at 7.35% as of the November 12, nobody in the room expects relief soon.

Read that same research one paragraph further though, and you find the sentence a real estate professional should tape to the dashboard. About 5.8% of locked-in homes get unlocked every year through what the economists call normal demographic and economic activity. That is their phrase for it. This business has always had a shorter one. Death, divorce, diapers and diplomas. The four D’s.

Those four events are the reason lock-in decays whether rates fall or not. They are not a forecast. They are a tide chart.

A charter captain does not cancel the season because the weather turned. He fishes the tide, because the tide table was printed a year ago, and the tide shows up on time no matter what the sky is doing. The rate is the weather. The four D’s are the tide. An agent who only works when the weather is pleasant will spend most of this cycle tied to the dock. The one who learns to read the table will come back with a full boat while everyone else is refreshing the forecast.

Here is the table, one ‘D’ at a time: where the list lives, and what to bring when you show up.

Every estate holding real property ends in a sale or a transfer, and the people managing it are almost always doing it for the first time.

The List: Probate court filings, which are public, county-level and frequently online.

The Better List: The attorneys who file them. Estate attorneys, elder law attorneys, trust administrators. Add the liquidation companies and the move managers who empty the house before anyone thinks to call an agent.

What to bring: A valuation as of the date of death, at no charge, on any file where the attorney needs one. Every estate requires that figure for tax basis and most agents have never thought to offer it. Be the one who does. Do not contact a family off an obituary, ever. The professionals around the family are the door. Walk through it with service in your hands rather than a business card.

A divorce splits a household in half, and the house seldom fits either half. What makes these listings difficult is not pricing or marketing. It is that both spouses must agree on you.

The List: Family law attorneys, mediators, and the planners who specialize in divorce finances. They know about the house roughly six months before the MLS does.

What to bring. Neutrality in writing. A single page the attorney can give both parties explaining how the sale works, how proceeds are typically held, and what the timeline looks like. Offer to meet each spouse on their own. An attorney’s biggest worry is an agent who picks a side. Remove that worry and you become the standing referral for the whole practice.

No rate on earth holds a family in a two-bedroom once the second child arrives. The lock-in researchers can model payment shock with precision. They cannot model a crib in the dining room.

The List: Your own past clients first: every buyer you placed in a small home or condo three to five years back. Then the places young parents already spend their Saturdays, pediatric offices, daycare centers, parent groups, library story time.

What to bring. Your presence rather than your postcard. Sponsor the daycare fundraiser. Show up for the T-ball opener. One Fair Housing note that carries real consequences: advertise the house and the service, never who ought to live there, and have your broker review any audience targeting before it runs.

The August a family’s last child leaves is the August a four-bedroom colonial becomes three empty rooms and a tax bill. Parents feel it at once. They do something about it the next spring.

The List: Graduating senior names are still printed locally every June. Match those surnames to homes bought 18 to 25 years ago in the assessor’s records. What you are holding is a roster of parents about to stand in an empty bedroom wondering what comes next.

What to bring. An empty-nest conversation. Reserve the library meeting room, invite a financial planner, and talk about what happens to a house that got too big. No slides, no listing presentation, no pressure of any kind. Treat people like people in September and they call you in April.

“I’m not reaching out because you have to sell. I’m reaching out so that when life changes, and it changes for all of us, you already know somebody. No presentation and no ask. Only a name worth holding onto.”

Then stop talking. That is a conversation rather than a sales call, and it works across all four D’s because it is honest about who you are and what you want. Serve first, and the listing takes care of itself.

The agents waiting on a rate cut are watching the weather. The agents working the four D’s are fishing the tide, and the tide, unlike the weather, has never once missed an appointment.
