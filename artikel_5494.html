From entry to trophy: 10 steps in the Grammy voting process

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

How Grammy voting works, step by step: the entry process, screening committees, nomination voting and final ballot that decide which recordings win the Recording Academy's awards

A Grammy is not decided in a single vote. Each award is the end of a sequence of stages run by the Recording Academy, the organization behind the Grammys, and carried out by its voting members. By the time a winner's name is read aloud, a recording has already passed through several checkpoints that happen out of public view.

Understanding how Grammy voting works starts with that sequence. A recording first has to be eligible. That means it must have come out within the release window the Academy sets for each awards year. It then has to be entered for consideration in a specific category. Screening committees review those entries to confirm that each one meets the rules and sits in the right place. Only after that do voting members weigh in.

Voting happens in rounds. In the nomination stage, members cast ballots on the entries that cleared screening, and those results determine the nominees. A final ballot follows, and members vote again to choose winners from those nominees. The ballots are counted confidentially, and the results stay sealed until the awards are announced.

The details inside each stage have not stayed fixed. The Academy revises its rules from time to time. Past changes have affected how many nominees a category holds, how committees operate and which categories members may vote in. The process described here is one the Academy continues to adjust.

Those mechanics shape the public conversation around the awards. Arguments over snubs and category placement often turn on a specific stage. One question might be whether an album was placed in a particular genre field. Another might be how a list of nominees was formed. Knowing where each decision happens makes those debates easier to follow.

The Grammys also differ from fan-voted awards. The people choosing nominees and winners are music professionals who belong to the Academy, not the listening public. Their ballots reflect the judgment of peers within the recording industry rather than the preferences of an audience.

The 10 slides that follow walk through the process in order. They start with the Academy's role, who is eligible to vote and the release window. They then cover submission, screening, both voting rounds, the general field review committees and how winners are tabulated and revealed.

The Recording Academy organizes the Grammy Awards. Its voting members — not network executives, sponsors or the public — choose the winners, under rules the Academy writes and enforces.

The organization's original name was the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences, often shortened to NARAS. Its founders came from the record business. They wanted an honor for achievement in recorded music, comparable to the Oscars in film.

The award takes its name from the gramophone. The trophy is a gilded gramophone statuette. It refers to the early record players that brought recorded sound into homes.

The Academy is a nonprofit membership organization. Its members work in music as singers, instrumentalists, songwriters, producers and recording engineers. The Academy decides who qualifies for membership. Those standards shape who sits behind every ballot. The Academy is based in Santa Monica, California, and has regional chapters in cities across the U.S.

The organization does more than hand out trophies. It runs MusiCares, a charity that offers health and financial help to people in the music industry. It also lobbies lawmakers in Washington, D.C., on issues that affect music creators, including copyright. The Grammy Museum in Los Angeles operates as a related nonprofit.

The Grammys also have a sibling award. The Latin Grammy Awards are run by the Latin Recording Academy, a separate body. Its members vote on Latin music through their own process.

For the main Grammys, the Academy's core job is the rulebook. It decides which categories exist and which recordings qualify. It also defines what happens at each voting stage.

Those decisions rest with the Academy rather than the television broadcaster. When a category is added, merged or retired, the Academy makes the call. The same applies to changes in eligibility and voting procedures.

The Academy publishes its rules ahead of each awards cycle. That document is the reference point for entrants, screeners and voters alike.

Grammy winners are chosen by voting members of the Recording Academy, who are working creative professionals in recorded music. Fans and the listening public do not vote.

The voting membership is built around the people who make recordings. That group includes performers, singers, songwriters, composers, producers and recording engineers. It also includes contributors whose crafts have their own Grammy categories, such as arrangers, album package designers and the writers of album notes.

Membership is not automatic. An applicant has to show creative or technical credits on commercially released recordings. The Academy reviews that record of work before it grants voting status. Recommendations from current members also form part of the application. The requirement limits voting to people with direct experience making music. Members are organized through regional chapters in cities across the U.S.

Each vote belongs to an individual. A label, management firm or music publisher cannot cast a ballot on behalf of its roster. Someone employed by one of those businesses votes only after qualifying as a voting member in their own right. The Academy's voting guidelines also tell members to fill out their own ballots rather than let anyone else do it.

The Academy also offers a separate, non-voting membership for industry professionals. These are people whose work supports music-making without the creative credits voting requires. They can take part in Academy programs and events. They do not help choose nominees or winners.

Voting members pay dues to keep their status. The Academy also expects them to stay active in the recording field. It periodically reviews whether members still qualify to vote. It has revised its membership requirements over the years.

A Grammy therefore reflects the judgment of fellow recording professionals. It is not calculated from sales, streaming totals, radio play or chart positions. The Academy instructs voters to judge entries on artistic and technical merit rather than commercial performance or popularity.

Each Grammy awards year covers a defined window of release dates, and the Recording Academy sets that window. A recording can compete only if it was released inside it. The calendar decides what can be entered before any member casts a vote.

The eligibility period is the first filter in the process. An album released just after the cutoff is not rejected. It waits and becomes eligible for the following year's awards instead.

The window has not followed the standard January-to-December calendar year. A recording released late in one calendar year can end up competing at a ceremony held well into the next year. Its rivals may include music released many months earlier.

The date that counts is the commercial release. A recording generally has to reach the public through normal distribution, such as sales or streaming. Music shared privately, played only at live shows or circulated as an unreleased demo does not establish eligibility on its own.

As a general rule, a recording is entered in only one awards year. It cannot come back in a later cycle just because it found a bigger audience after the cutoff. As a result, one release does not collect nominations at several ceremonies.

Timing also determines which competitors a project faces. Two albums released a few weeks apart can land in different awards years if the cutoff falls between them. Each one is then measured against a different pool of entries.

The release window is separate from the question of category. Eligibility by date says only that a recording may be entered this year. Where it is entered, and whether that placement holds, is handled later in the process.

The Recording Academy publishes the exact window for each awards year in its official rules, and it has revised those rules over time. Checking whether a specific album qualifies means reading the rules for that year. The dates from a previous cycle may not match.

A recording reaches the Grammys only after someone enters it. Recording Academy members and registered media companies, such as record labels, submit eligible recordings to the Academy. They also name the specific categories where each one should compete.

That entry step comes before any member casts a vote. A release that is never submitted does not appear on a ballot, however widely it sold or streamed. The Academy does not scan the market for contenders on its own. The awards depend on entrants putting work forward.

Entries are filed through the Academy's online submission system. The entrant supplies details about the recording, including the title, the performing artist, release information and the people credited on it. Those credits matter later. They help determine who receives an award if the recording wins.

The Academy publishes a rulebook for each awards year that spells out entry requirements. Entrants are expected to follow the version in force for the year they are filing. Rules that applied in one cycle may not apply in the next.

The main decision an entrant makes at this stage is the category. Grammy categories are grouped into fields. Most fields cover a genre, such as pop, rock, rap, country, jazz or classical. Others cover crafts such as production, engineering, packaging and music video. A separate general field holds the broad categories open across genres. These include Album of the Year, Record of the Year, Song of the Year and Best New Artist.

An entrant typically places a recording in the genre field that fits its style. It may also be entered in the general field. Academy rules limit how many categories a single recording may enter, and the Academy has revised those limits over time.

Correct placement shapes who judges the work. A jazz record entered in a pop category would face a different pool of voters than its makers intended. The category an entrant picks is a request, not a final placement.

Grammy screening committees review every entry before voting begins. Their job is to confirm that each recording is eligible and sits in the right category. They do not rate quality, and they do not decide who gets nominated.

The committees are made up of Recording Academy members with expertise in the genres they review. A panel focused on one field listens to the entries submitted there and compares each one with that category's definition. The question in front of them is classification, not merit.

Eligibility comes first. A screener checks whether a recording meets the Academy's rules for the awards year, including when it was released and how it was distributed. An entry that fails those checks does not advance to the ballot.

Placement is the second test. A label or member may enter an album in one genre, while the committee concludes it belongs in another. In that case, screeners can move the entry to the category that fits it better. They can also remove it from a category where it does not belong.

A placement decision determines the competition an entry faces. A recording moved out of one genre goes up against a different set of entries. It also goes before a different group of first-round voters.

The outcome of screening shapes the first-round ballot. Members see only the entries that the committees have cleared and placed. A recording that never reached a ballot in its preferred category could not be nominated there.

Genre placement has drawn public disputes. Artists and labels have objected when committees reclassified their work. Those disputes often center on music that blends styles and does not fit a single category cleanly.

The Academy revises its committee procedures and category definitions over time. As a result, the specific rules screeners apply can differ from one awards year to the next.

First-round voting is where Recording Academy voting members choose nominees from the pool of entries that cleared screening. Members do not vote in every category. They vote in the general field plus a limited number of other fields set by Academy rules.

The general field holds the awards that cut across all genres. Album of the Year, Record of the Year, Song of the Year and Best New Artist sit there. Every voting member may cast first-round votes in those categories.

Beyond the general field, members choose among the genre and craft fields. Those cover areas such as pop, rap, country, jazz and classical. The Academy caps how many of those fields a single member may vote in. It has revised that cap over the years, along with the rules on which categories count toward it.

The limit is meant to tie each vote to knowledge of the music. The Academy asks members to vote only in categories where they have expertise. A session musician who works mostly in jazz, for example, is expected to concentrate on jazz rather than spread votes across unfamiliar genres.

Members are also told to judge entries on artistic and technical merit. Sales, chart positions and streaming totals are not supposed to drive a ballot. A recording that sold modestly can still earn a nomination if voters in its field rate the work highly.

The first-round ballot lists only entries cleared by the screening committees. Each entry appears in the category where screeners placed it. A recording therefore competes only against other entries in that category, not against every submission across the awards.

Individual ballots stay private at this stage. The Academy does not publish how any member voted in the nomination round.

This round turns a large pool of entries into a short list for each category. It does not pick winners. Those are decided later, on a separate final ballot.

Grammy nominees are the entries that receive the most votes from Recording Academy voting members in the first round. In most categories, the top vote-getters become the official nominees. Only those nominees move on to the final ballot.

A few specialized craft categories follow a different path. In those categories, Academy committees with relevant expertise select the nominees rather than the full first-round vote.

The first round works as a filter, not as the contest for the trophy itself. An entry does not need to win its category at this stage. It needs to finish among the leaders in member votes for that category.

The Academy sets the cutoff. The cutoff is not a fixed formula that stays the same every year. The number of nominee slots in each category is a rule the Academy has revised over time. Each revision changes how many first-round finishers advance to the final ballot.

The cutoff applies separately in each category. When a recording is entered in more than one category, its votes are counted separately in each one. The same recording can earn a nomination in one category and miss the list in another.

Screening is already finished at this point. Each entry on the first-round ballot was checked for eligibility and placed in a category before members voted. The vote count decides which entries advance. Category placement was settled at the earlier screening stage.

The Academy does not publish first-round vote totals. The public sees which entries became nominees but not how close any other entry came.

Most nominations attach to a recording or work in its category. Academy rules decide which credited contributors, such as artists, producers or songwriters, are named alongside it. Those credit rules vary by category.

Eligibility is tied to the release window for a single awards year. An entry that misses the nominee list generally cannot be entered again in a later year.

The outcome of this step is the list of Grammy nominations. The Academy makes that list public before final-round voting begins. From then on, members choose among a small field of nominees rather than the full pool of entries.

Nomination review committees were small panels of Recording Academy members that once helped pick the Grammy nominees in the general field. Under that system, first-round vote totals did not set the shortlist on their own. The panels reviewed the leading entries and chose which ones advanced to the final ballot.

The general field is the group of awards open to recordings from every genre. It includes album of the year, record of the year, song of the year and best new artist. Entries in those categories come from across the industry rather than from a single style of music.

Members still voted first under the committee system. Their ballots produced a ranking of entries in each general field category. A committee then examined a longer list of top vote-getters rather than treating that ranking as final. Its members chose the nominees from that list. Strong first-round support therefore did not guarantee a nomination.

The Academy kept the names of committee members confidential. Neither the public nor the artists under review knew who sat on a given panel. The committees' role also extended beyond the general field. Panels operated in a number of genre categories as well.

That extra layer drew public criticism. Artists and commentators questioned why a small, anonymous group could override the result of a wider vote. Complaints centered on transparency. Outsiders could not see who made the choices or how a committee reached them.

The Academy later changed how committees operate. It removed nomination review committees from most categories, including the general field. Under that change, nominees in those categories come from the votes of eligible members rather than from a panel's selection.

Committees did not disappear entirely. The Academy kept them for certain specialized craft categories, such as best recording package and best album notes. Those awards assess work like packaging design and liner notes rather than the music itself.

The shift moved more weight onto the nomination ballot itself. In the general field, the members' own votes decide which recordings reach the final round.

The final ballot is the stage where Recording Academy voting members choose the Grammy winners. Members pick from the nominees that emerged from the nomination rounds, and in each category the nominee with the most votes wins.

By this point the field is fixed. No new recordings can join the race, and entries that missed the nomination cut are out of contention. Every recording or artist on the ballot has already cleared screening and earned a nomination.

The final round asks members a narrower question than the first round did. During nominations, voters sorted through a large pool of entries. On the final ballot, they compare a short list of finalists within each category and select one.

Which categories a member can vote in follows Academy rules. The general field, which includes Album of the Year and Record of the Year, is open to voting members across genres. Those awards are not tied to any single style of music.

Genre categories work differently. The Academy limits how many genre fields each member may vote in on the final ballot. The Academy frames that limit as a way to keep genre voting tied to members' areas of expertise. The Academy has revised these limits over the years. The allowance for any member depends on the rules in force for that awards year.

Commercial performance does not enter the count. Sales figures, streaming totals and chart positions play no part in the final tally. The outcome in each category depends only on how voting members mark their ballots.

Each category on the ballot is a separate race. A recording can be nominated in several categories and win in some, all or none of them. That structure explains why one album can lead the nominations and still leave the ceremony with fewer awards than another.

Once members submit their final ballots, the votes move on to the counting stage.

After final-round voting closes, the Recording Academy's ballots are counted confidentially. Each category's winner stays sealed until it is revealed at the Premiere Ceremony or the main televised Grammy ceremony.

The count is the last stage of a long sequence. By this point, recordings have been entered, screened, voted into nomination and placed on the final ballot. Tabulation turns those final-round ballots into a single result for each category.

In each category, the winner is the nominee who receives the most votes from eligible voting members. The Academy does not publish vote totals or margins. Fans and artists learn who won, but not how close the race was.

Keeping results private preserves the reveal. Nominees attend without knowing the outcome in advance. Presenters open sealed envelopes on stage and read the winner's name aloud.

Most Grammy categories are not handed out during the main television broadcast. They are presented earlier the same day at the Premiere Ceremony, which the Academy streams online. A smaller group of high-profile awards, including the general field categories, is held for the main telecast.

This two-part format means every category receives a formal on-stage announcement. Only a fraction of the awards appear on the network broadcast.

A nominee does not need to be in the room to win. Results for absent winners are announced the same way. The Academy records the award whether or not anyone walks to the stage to accept it.

Each winner receives the Grammy statuette, a gilded gramophone. The trophy's shape reflects the award's name. The word Grammy comes from the gramophone, an early machine for playing recorded sound.

After the ceremonies, the Academy publishes the complete list of winners on its official website, Grammy.com. That list becomes the permanent record for the awards year. When the next eligibility period opens, the cycle starts again with a new round of entries from members and registered media companies.
