Everyone has the file. Nobody has the right version

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

EVENT: Distributed teams need to collaborate, but duplicated files, delays, and competing versions keep getting in the way

Distributed teams need to collaborate on shared information, but duplicated files, delays, and competing versions keep getting in the way.

Picture a video production team with editors in different cities. One person updates a project file, another keeps working from an earlier copy, and a third waits for a large transfer to finish.

When teams need to collaborate on large files across locations, traditional storage and file-sharing arrangements introduce friction precisely where people expect technology to help.

The hard part isn't merely moving a file. It's knowing who has the authoritative copy, when changes are available, and whether somebody else is still working on an outdated version.

Consider a game studio preparing a build for review. A designer exports an updated asset, but a colleague pulls an older version from a synced folder. The mistake only becomes obvious when the pieces are assembled. The team must trace the mismatch, resend files, and repeat work.

Sync-and-download workflows depend on a chain of uploads, downloads, and decisions about which copy is current. Under deadline pressure, that chain breaks. Delayed access means missed handoffs; duplicated files mean extra checking and sometimes rework.

For IT, each workaround creates another question: where does the data live, who can access it, and how do teams maintain consistent controls? Adding another collaboration application doesn't resolve those underlying questions.

The useful starting point is the workflow. Find out where teams copy data, where they wait, and what prompts them to work outside the shared system.

These frustrations cut across industries, but the ways organizations respond aren't identical. Hearing how another team handled version confusion, access, and handoffs is often more useful than another abstract promise of seamless collaboration.

That's the focus of The Register and LucidLink’s invitation-only dinner on November 24 at Covent Garden Hotel in London. Joe Fay of The Register will moderate a peer discussion under the Chatham House Rule.
