Creativity Is Conning Us

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

We are culturally obsessed with innovation. Consider TED Talks and Apple’s “Think Different” campaign and our obsession with tech founder-philosophers. The writer Thomas Frank has. In The Creativity Con: How Innovation Became America’s Biggest Racket, the author deconstructs what he calls “our national fascination with creativity.” The book has chapters on neoliberalism, AI, and the current concept of the creative class. His research included a long trip to a music festival turned marketing love-in, South by Southwest, which he describes in his smart, dry way as a “marathon trade show with something like 40,000 attendees” and “a more inclusive, more lovable version of Davos.”

Frank, who is 61 and lives in Maryland, is best known for his bestselling books about politics, including What’s the Matter With Kansas? and Listen, Liberal. But his new book is in some ways a belated sequel to his first book, 1997’s The Conquest of Cool, about how advertising co-opted the rhetoric of the 1960s counterculture to sell us Pepsi and cars. (It’s the kind of book you’ll think of often: anytime you see the launch of a new extreme energy drink and still required reading for anyone doing a Mad Men rewatch.)

Since Frank’s newest work focuses on creativity and innovation as the drivers of American affluence, I thought meeting him on his book tour for slop bowls at Sweetgreen, a salad marketed as an efficient and innovative way to get a nutritious meal, would be apropos. But the Sweetgreen on the Upper East Side was both loud and had nowhere to sit, a sad reflection of how Americans view workday lunches. Luckily, a small Vietnamese restaurant on Lexington Avenue became a welcome space for bahn mi and a discussion of the great man theory, the midterm elections, and using AI for gardening advice.

This interview has been condensed for length and clarity.

Vanity Fair: I can’t believe you were at South by Southwest for nine whole days researching this book.

Thomas Frank: Longer. It goes for 10 days, and I was determined to see the whole thing.

Did it make you depressed, or what was your takeaway?

Well, I was fascinated. That was one of the first things I did for the book. It was March of ’23. So, you know, I—I had my theories and my ideas and my preconceived notions, and I was like, “What? What am I going to find?” I wasn’t prepared at all for what swept over me.

One of my favorite parts of the whole book is that the CIA was there at the Creative Industries Expo.

I love that when they asked you if you had any questions you asked them a spicy question about the JFK assassination. What else did you do early on for research?

I went back and read [Thomas] Carlyle, On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History. That is where he introduced the great man theory of history. He thinks the nature of humanity is to worship heroes, and that sort of gave rise to this sort of whole genius theory. So I didn’t talk about any of this in the book—it all got left on the cutting room floor—but then in the 19th century, they had this theory of the great genius, that Franz Liszt or Napoleon or Goethe had this spark and we had to sort of honor them. And they were quasi-magical, because we couldn’t really understand how they did it. And that obviously rubs Americans the wrong way. Even though our democracy is falling to pieces, we are an intensely egalitarian people.

How did you decide what to cover and keep in the book?

The way I decided to do the book was historical and then sociological. So I did the historical part, and a lot of it is [about] Obama and Reagan. And then for the sociological part, I decided I would choose a state and describe the innovation initiatives there. And I chose Maryland because I live there. I chose an industry, and the one I chose is the defense contractors.

Defense contractors love to talk about how innovative they are. There are like five industries that really abuse this language [of innovation]: defense, advertising, pharma, the tech guys, and Wall Street.

What is the creepiest thing you learned about defense contractors?

The contrast between what they make and the way they describe it, which is wholesome all-American innovation. It’s language that’s just camouflage.

You’ve written a lot about politics. Do you think there’s going to be a big midterm shift happening?

The country is definitely turning against Trump. But then again, what do I know? The polls have been so wrong all these years. I mean look, the Iran war is so unpopular, and it’s really unpopular with the very people that voted for him.

They have to have something that they stand for, and it can’t be innovation.

Oh yeah, absolutely. The Republicans used to love the language of creativity, and the Democrats liked the language of innovation. So [President] Carter used to talk about innovation all the time, and at the same time, Reagan was always talking about creativity. Nobody said at the time, “this is a battle between innovation and creativity.”

How do you define the difference between innovation and creativity?

Technically, innovation has to do with actually producing a product. Creativity means facility with art and language. But in practice, the words overlap all the time and are used as synonyms. They were basically synonyms and then split back in the ’60s when creativity was identified with the counterculture, and innovation was identified with engineers, military, industrial, and technology. And the counterculture really turned against all of that stuff.

It’s funny that after all of your political books, this one still ends with Trump.

Yeah, Trump and AI. The last chapter is all about it.

I have no technical understanding of it, so I wouldn’t know. I don’t think anybody else knows either because it’s all private. It’s proprietary. I compare it to a private sector Manhattan project. My wife is an economist. She finds it extremely useful. In my work for this book, it was not useful.

I’m really into gardening, and it’s very helpful with gardening. It’s very helpful with household chores. It knows a lot about stereo equipment.
