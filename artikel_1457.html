Pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 as a therapy against solid and blood cancers

The N7-methylguanosine (m7G) modification is one of the most prevalent tRNA modifications, mainly catalysed by the METTL1–WDR4 protein complex1,2,3,4. The m7G methyltransferase METTL1 has been linked to oncogenic transformation and the maintenance of several aggressive malignancies; however, the clinical implications of therapeutically targeting a tRNA-modifying enzyme have not been established. Here we present the development and characterization of STM9005, a highly selective and potent first-in-class tRNA methyltransferase inhibitor of METTL1. Treatment of numerous cancer cell models (including leukaemia, melanoma, pancreatic carcinoma, oesophageal cancer and small cell lung carcinoma) with STM9005 blocks cell growth, causes differentiation changes and dysregulates cell cycle progression. These cellular effects were accompanied by a selective decrease in m7G on a subset of tRNAs and a reduction in their expression levels, which impacted the translation of oncogenic mRNAs. We show that pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 in vivo impairs tumour growth and significantly prolongs survival in multiple mouse cancer models of different tissue origin. Collectively, our results provide a strong proof of concept that targeting of tRNA methylation is a promising avenue of anti-cancer therapy and illustrate that catalytic inhibition of METTL1 represents a therapeutic strategy against a number of aggressive malignancies.

Over 150 RNA modifications have been described to date, with transfer RNA (tRNA) being one of the most highly modified RNA species5,6. tRNA modifications are critical for the folding, stability and function of tRNA2,7,8. One of the most prevalent tRNA modifications is m7G1,2. It is deposited at position 46 of tRNA (m7G46) by the METTL1–WDR4 heterodimeric protein complex, with METTL1 functioning as the catalytically active component of the complex, and WDR4 having a scaffolding and RNA-binding role2,3,4. The m7G modification on tRNA has been shown to have an important role in stabilizing the tRNA structure, regulating the translation of mRNA and influencing a number of cellular processes, including stress response and oncogenesis9,10,11,12,13. Recent studies have shown that METTL1 may also deposit the m7G modification on mRNAs and microRNA precursors14,15,16, although at a substantially lower rate compared with the m7G levels on the tRNA fraction.

Many studies have implicated the role of the epitranscriptome in the development and regulation of cancer17,18,19. An increasing amount of evidence shows that dysregulation of tRNA modifications and their pathways contributes to the pathogenesis of human disease, including cancer6,18. Notably, upregulation of METTL1 and its catalytic activity has been linked to oncogenic transformation as well as the initiation and maintenance of aggressive haematological and solid malignancies, including acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), melanoma, glioblastoma, liver and lung cancers11,20,21,22. Moreover, the non-catalytic function of METTL1 has recently been linked to the development of sarcomas through its interaction with the multi-tRNA synthetase complex23.

To date, epitranscriptomic drug discovery efforts have largely focused on the targeting of the m6A mRNA modification and its regulators24,25,26,27. Early prototype inhibitors of METTL1 have recently been described and support the feasibility of pharmacologically inhibiting METTL128,29. However, their limited potency and selectivity render them unsuitable as tools to assess the molecular and therapeutic impact of targeting METTL1 catalytic inhibition. Here we present the development and characterization of STM9005, a potent and highly selective first-in-class tRNA methyltransferase inhibitor of METTL1 that demonstrates significant anti-tumour activity across diverse cancer types through the reduction in m7G on tRNA substrates of METTL1.

We performed a high-throughput screen of 250,000 diverse drug-like compounds. After initial hit triaging, STM4529 was identified as a singleton non-S-adenosyl methionine (non-SAM)-related hit that inhibited METTL1 with a half-maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) of around 15 μM (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis showed that STM4529 binding to METTL1 was competitive with the SAM analogue S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH), indicating its binding to the catalytic site of METTL1 (Extended Data Fig. 1c). After extensive chemical optimization of potency, selectivity and in vitro and in vivo absorption–distribution–metabolism–excretion properties, we identified STM9005 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Note 1a).

STM9005 inhibited recombinant METTL1 enzyme activity with an IC50 < 13 nM, a value restricted by the lower detection limit of the assay (Fig. 1b). To use a more sensitive assay system and to verify binding to METTL1 in an orthogonal system, we performed SPR analysis, which demonstrated direct stoichiometric high-affinity binding of STM9005 to METTL1 with a Kd of 2 nM (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Moreover, using a cellular target engagement assay, we confirmed cell penetration and binding of STM9005 to METTL1 in cells with an IC50 of 13 nM (Fig. 1c). STM9005 was found to be highly specific to METTL1 as it had no effect on the activity of other RNA-methyltransferases (METTL3/14, METTL16, BCDIN3D, TSG1 and NSUN2) when used at comparable concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 1e), and exhibited greater than 10,000-fold selectivity for METTL1–WDR4 when tested against a broad panel of 49 RNA-, DNA- and protein-methyltransferases (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, no significant off-target toxicity liabilities were identified when STM9005 was tested in a broad panel of 97 kinases (KINOMEscan, Eurofins DiscoverX; Supplementary Table 2) and over 70 assays testing a range of receptors and enzymes commonly associated with drug toxicity (SAFETYscan 47, Eurofins DiscoverX; Supplementary Table 3).

To further investigate the cellular activity of STM9005, we applied RNA mass spectrometry (MS) to assess the specific effect of STM9005 on the m7G modification level of tRNA from the previously known METTL1-dependent AML cell lines MOLM-13 and OCI-AML211,30,31. We observed a significant decrease in m7G levels (Fig. 1e), but not in the levels of the m1A and m5C RNA modifications (Extended Data Fig. 1f). To further quantify the cellular effect of STM9005 on m7G-modified tRNA, we developed an antibody-based m7G detection assay (Meso Scale Discovery (MSD)), which enabled us to measure the effect of STM9005 on tRNA-Val. This demonstrated a concentration-dependent decrease in tRNA-Val-m7G after treatment with STM9005 with an IC50 of 34 nM (Extended Data Fig. 1g), confirming potent inhibition of tRNA modification by STM9005 in cells at nanomolar concentrations. Moreover, we confirmed STM9005 acts specifically on the METTL1–m7G tRNA axis, as we observed no significant effect on m7G modification of 18S ribosomal RNA (Extended Data Fig. 1h).

To characterize the binding of STM9005 to METTL1 further, we performed X-ray crystallography, confirming unequivocally that STM9005 binds to the SAM-binding site of METTL1 (Fig. 1f, Extended Data Fig. 1i, Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Note 1b). Notably, part of STM9005 binds in a pocket not previously observed in METTL1 crystal structures, where it forms molecular interactions such as the hydrogen bonds between Tyr184 and the central carbonyl moiety of STM9005, and a pi-carbon interaction between Phe159 and the tetrahydroisoquinoline methyl of STM9005, providing the basis for its high affinity and selectivity. In strong support of this, STM9001, a diastereomer of STM9005 in which the tetrahydroisoquinoline methyl is in an opposite orientation, is around 100-fold less active (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1a,g), providing further evidence of the specificity and binding mode of STM9005 (Extended Data Fig. 1i and Supplementary Table 1).

The observed binding of STM9005 to the catalytic pocket of METTL1 did not impact the RNA or protein levels of METTL1 after in vitro treatment (Extended Data Fig. 1j,k), in contrast to the significant and selective effect observed on the m7G tRNA modification levels (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 1f,g). Finally, pharmacokinetic profiling of STM9005 in mice after a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) dose of either 10 or 30 mg per kg showed good bioavailability with maximal plasma exposures of 13 and 56 μM, respectively, and a half-life of around 1 h. Importantly, at the 12 h time point, STM9005 plasma levels remain above the METTL1 cellular IC50, indicating METTL1 inhibition in vivo over a period of at least 12 h (Fig. 1g).

Taken together, these data establish STM9005 as a potent, selective and bioavailable catalytic inhibitor of METTL1 that is suitable for both in vitro and in vivo investigations.

To investigate the anti-cancer potential of STM9005 in cellular models, we initially screened the impact of STM9005 treatment in three different cancer cell panels using colorimetric assays which measure metabolic activity (MTS assay). For these experiments, we used cancer cell models that were either previously reported to be sensitive to the genetic inhibition of METTL111,32,33, or their METTL1-dependency status (sensitivity or resistance) was validated here using CRISPR-based genetic disruption of METTL1/Mettl1 (human and mouse, respectively) in competitive co-culture assays (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). We observed a dose-dependent reduction in the proliferation of human leukaemic cell lines (Fig. 2a), primary mouse AML models (Fig. 2b) and human and mouse solid cancer models (Fig. 2c) from different tissue origins (skin, lung, pancreas, colorectal, oesophageal and soft tissue). Investigating the direct effect of STM9005 on cell growth using a methylene blue assay, we observed highly potent, dose-dependent effects on the fitness and growth of a subset of these cell lines (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3a). Additional reprofiling in proliferation assays using ATP quantitation under optimized culture conditions confirmed the anti-proliferative activity of STM9005 at submicromolar concentrations in these cells (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 3b). By contrast, the diastereomer control compound STM9001 was significantly less potent in these systems, consistent with its reduced activity against the catalytic activity of METTL1, providing strong evidence that the anti-proliferative effects of STM9005 are due to METTL1 catalytic inhibition (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 3b). There was also a significant dose-dependent negative effect seen in the colony forming potential of MLL-AF9/Flt3ITD/+-driven primary mouse AML cells after ex vivo treatment with STM9005 (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Notably, the cancer models that appeared to be independent of METTL1 genetic loss (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b) were also not significantly impacted after treatment with STM9005 (Fig. 2d). To confirm that the anti-proliferative effects of STM9005 are mediated specifically through METTL1, we assessed its impact in two insensitive (OE33, mPDAC2) and two sensitive (B16F10, mPDAC1) cancer cell lines with a loss of METTL1 or Mettl1 after CRISPR–Cas9 targeting. STM9005 treatment in both sensitive and insensitive cell lines resulted in no additional negative effect (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e) despite the absence of METTL1 protein (Extended Data Figs. 2a,b and 3a), consistent with the small molecule exerting its mechanism of action specifically through inhibition of METTL1’s catalytic activity.

In the context of haematological malignancies, pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 using STM9005 caused significant and dose-dependent myeloid differentiation in both human and primary mouse models of AML (Fig. 2f,g and Extended Data Fig. 3f). Moreover, we and others have previously shown that genetic inhibition of METTL1 negatively affected various cancer models by impacting their cell cycle status11,20. To examine whether the observed effect on cell cycle is dependent on the catalytic activity of METTL1 we analysed a panel of solid cancer cell lines with different tissues of origin and mutational background and showed that treatment with STM9005 causes a significant disruption of cell cycle, with a dose-dependent increase in cells in G0/G1 phase and a significantly decreased number of cells in S phase (Fig. 2h,i and Extended Data Fig. 3g,h) as the most common changes across the cell lines tested. Moreover, western blot analysis of cell-cycle-related markers confirmed the observed cell cycle dysregulation seen in the various cancer models tested here (Fig. 2j,k). Moreover, annexin V analysis showed no significant levels of apoptosis after treatment with STM9005 (Extended Data Fig. 3i).

These data confirm the importance of METTL1’s methyltransferase activity in maintaining both haematological and solid cancers, and that pharmacological inhibition of its enzymatic activity is sufficient to elicit anti-cancer cellular effects.

To assess the molecular changes caused by treatment with STM9005, we analysed its impact across different layers of the transcriptome. We first performed modification-induced misincorporation tRNA sequencing (mim-tRNA-seq) to investigate the m7G modification status on tRNA fractions from B16F10 melanoma tumours and a primary mouse MLL-AF9/Flt3ITD/+-driven AML. Analysis of the mim-tRNA-seq data revealed a highly specific and significant reduction in the m7G46 mark in known m7G-modified tRNAs—a result that was highly similar between both cancer models (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 4).

Subsequent investigation of differential tRNA expression from the same two cancer models showed selective downregulation of a subset of m7G-modified tRNAs after pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 5), which was validated using northern blotting across a panel of m7G-modified tRNA substrates (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 4a).

We previously showed that the catalytic function of METTL1 drives the translation of key oncogenic proteins, including CDK4 and KDM1A, by modifying and stabilizing a subset of tRNAs11. To explore the dependence of these outcomes on METTL1’s catalytic activity, we performed western blotting using a panel of cancer models from diverse tissues, confirming that CDK4 and KDM1A protein levels were reduced after STM9005 treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 4b), while the same was not seen with corresponding mRNA levels (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). To verify that STM9005’s mechanism of action is mediated by inhibition of the m7G–tRNA axis, we performed rescue-of-phenotype experiments by overexpressing either the established METTL1–m7G tRNA substrate Arg-TCT or the METTL1 biomarker CDK4, a known substrate of Arg-TCT11. Notably, overexpression of Arg-TCT tRNA in both a solid cancer and a leukaemia model (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 4e–g) led to a partial but significant response to STM9005 and an increase in the viability of the cancer cells. Similarly, overexpression of Cdk4 in both a solid cancer and a leukaemia model (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 4h–j) mirrored the partial rescue of phenotype seen when using Arg-TCT tRNA overexpression, supporting the conclusion that the cellular effects caused by STM9005 treatment are dependent on the m7G–tRNA molecular pathway, which is maintained by the catalytic activity of METTL1.

To further validate that STM9005 acts specifically by inhibiting the catalytic function of METTL1 we overexpressed wild-type (WT) METTL1 and mutant (MUT) METTL1 carrying two amino acid changes in the catalytic domain (L160A/D164A for human METTL1 and L154A/D157A for mouse Mettl1). Forced expression of WT METTL1/Mettl1 significantly increased cellular resistance to STM9005, whereas viability did not improve in cells overexpressing METTL1MUT/Mettl1MUT (Fig. 3g,h and Extended Data Fig. 4k,l), confirming that STM9005 exerts its effects through the inhibition of the catalytic activity of METTL1. Moreover, the reduction in the levels of known m7G-dependent protein substrates, such as CDK4 and KDM1A, is reversed when WT METTL1 is overexpressed, while the same is not seen with overexpression of METTL1MUT (Extended Data Fig. 4m). This further accentuates that STM9005’s activity is specifically driven by the catalytic inhibition of METTL1.

To assess the wider downstream effects of STM9005 treatment on global translation, we used ribosome sequencing (Ribo-seq). After treatment with STM9005, Ribo-seq and mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) changes were moderately correlated (r = 0.6368; Extended Data Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 6), suggesting that pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 affects gene expression at both the transcription and translational levels. Ribosome-protected RNA fragment (RPF) density analysis showed that, after treatment with STM9005, there was a significant increase in A-site ribosome occupancy at codons associated with m7G-modified tRNAs compared with those associated with non-m7G modified tRNAs (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c and Supplementary Table 7), suggesting ribosome stalling at sites that are dependent on m7G-modified tRNAs for decoding. Importantly, treatment did not reduce the protein levels of METTL1 (Extended Data Fig. 1k), METTL3 and WDR4 (Extended Data Fig. 5d) at any of the concentrations tested, confirming that the effects seen can be attributed to the catalytic inhibition of METTL1, rather than a global translation defect. To further validate our Ribo-seq findings, we compared genes downregulated in our Ribo-seq dataset with a published SILAC-based proteomic dataset from METTL1 knockdown and METTL1(WT)–WDR4 overexpression studies11. Genes downregulated after STM9005 treatment showed significant overlap with those reduced after short-hairpin-RNA-mediated depletion of METTL1 (shMETTL1; P < 1 × 10−5; Extended Data Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 8) and were likewise enriched among genes upregulated by METTL1(WT)–WDR4 overexpression (P < 1 × 10−5; Extended Data Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 8), further confirming on-target, catalysis-dependent effects on the METTL1-regulated translatome. This demonstrates that catalytic inhibition of METTL1 using STM9005 selectively alters the m7G-modified tRNA pool and therefore impacts translation, in concordance with our previous findings using genetic downregulation of METTL1 levels11.

To assess the molecular changes caused by treatment with STM9005 on the mRNA transcriptome, we performed mRNA-seq and differential expression analysis using three cancer models (OE21, LoVo and B16F10) treated with either vehicle or STM9005 (Supplementary Tables 9–11). Gene Ontology analysis of differentially expressed genes showed that treatment with STM9005 dysregulates several key oncogenic pathways, including pathways such as those regulated by MYC, PI3K–AKT–mTOR, p53 and, as shown earlier, the cell cycle (Extended Data Fig. 6a and Supplementary Tables 12–14).

Previous studies have associated METTL1 with the transcriptional regulation of MYC and its pathways12,34. However, there is a lack of evidence directly linking the catalytic function of METTL1 to the regulation of MYC. To directly address this, we investigated whether the observed changes in expression of MYC, caused by isolated catalytic inhibition of METTL1, were evident at the MYC protein level. Indeed, western blot analysis confirmed that treatment with STM9005 leads to a dose-dependent decrease in MYC protein levels in two independent cancer models (Extended Data Fig. 6b), while the same reduction was not seen at the mRNA level (Extended Data Fig. 6c), showing that METTL1 regulates MYC protein levels through its catalytic function. To explore the potential of STM9005 treatment impacting cancer growth through the downstream regulation of MYC levels, we performed rescue-of-phenotype experiments by overexpressing MYC in a human and a mouse solid cancer model. Our experiments showed that MYC overexpression increased viability after treatment with STM9005 compared with the empty vector control (Extended Data Fig. 6d–g), indicating that the negative impact of STM9005 on cancer cells could be partially connected to negative regulation of MYC and its pathways.

Finally, we confirmed that, despite the transcript and protein level dysregulation of several cancer-associated pathways after treatment with STM9005, transcript and protein levels of METTL1 remained unaffected (Extended Data Fig. 6h and Extended Data Fig. 1j), highlighting again that the effects observed were due to the catalytic inhibition of METTL1.

Taken together, our mechanistic analysis further confirms the specificity and selectivity of our small-molecule approach and that treatment with STM9005 affects METTL1-mediated m7G modification levels, which in turn causes defects in the translation and gene regulation of cancer-essential biomarkers.

Given our strong evidence for potency, specificity and anti-cancer efficacy of STM9005 in vitro, we performed a series of in vivo experiments using both solid cancers and leukaemia models.

Initially, we tested the in vivo activity of STM9005 using three solid tumour models from different tissues (melanoma, pancreatic ductal carcinoma (PDAC) and lung adenocarcinoma). For the first in vivo solid-tumour experiment, we transplanted immunocompetent mice subcutaneously with B16F10 mouse melanoma cells and treated them twice daily with 30 mg per kg STM9005 for 5 days (Extended Data Fig. 7a). This treatment plan led to a significant decrease in tumour size of the STM9005-treated cohort in comparison to the vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 4a,b and Extended Data Fig. 7b). To confirm on-target activity of STM9005 in vivo, we quantified m7G levels on tRNA extracted from melanoma tumours and kidney tissues of control and treated mice. Notably, we detected significantly lower levels of m7G tRNA modification in the tumours (Fig. 4c) and kidneys (Fig. 4d) of animals treated with STM9005 compared with the vehicle-treated cohort, demonstrating catalytic inhibition of METTL1 in vivo. We also confirmed a significant decrease in MYC and, simultaneously, a significant increase in p53 after in vivo treatment of melanoma tumours with STM9005 (Fig. 4e–g), providing strong proof of a mechanism consistent with reduction of cancer proliferation in vivo and complementing our previous in vitro findings (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b).

We followed up with two further in vivo experiments using solid cancer models, one using a mouse PDAC35 (mPDAC1) model driven by Kras and a second using the human lung adenocarcinoma model A549, both transplanted subcutaneously into immunocompromised mice and treated with 30 mg per kg STM9005 or vehicle twice daily for 9 days (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). As with the B16F10 model, treatment with STM9005 significantly impaired the growth of pancreatic and lung adenocarcinoma tumours compared to the control cohorts (Fig. 4h–m) and reduced tRNA m7G levels (Fig. 4n). Importantly, while STM9005 showed strong anti-cancer efficacy in vivo, it did not cause any acute or lasting signs of toxicity, and caused no measurable effects on the body weight of either immunocompromised or immunocompetent mice (Extended Data Fig. 7e–g).

Having established the anti-proliferative potential of STM9005 treatment in solid tumours, we next investigated the effect of pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 on haematological malignancies. First, we transplanted an aggressive primary mouse AML model driven by MLL-AF9/Flt3ITD/+ into NSG-SGM3 mice and treated them with 30 mg per kg STM9005 or vehicle, twice daily for 5 days (Extended Data Fig. 7h). Pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 significantly prolonged the survival of STM9005-treated mice compared with the vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4o), thereby demonstrating efficacy in both haematological and solid cancer models.

To accentuate these observations in a clinically relevant context, we next used a patient-derived AML xenograft (AML-491 PDX) and treated the mice twice daily with either vehicle or 30 mg per kg STM9005 for 10 days (Extended Data Fig. 7i). The STM9005-treated PDX cohort had a significantly longer lifespan compared with the vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 4p) and illustrated a significantly lower AML burden in the bone marrow, as measured by the presence of hCD45+ cells (Fig. 4q). Finally, analysis of weight and blood count measurements covering the duration of the AML PDX experiment (Extended Data Fig. 7i) showed no significant differences or signs of toxicity between the vehicle- and STM9005-treated cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 7j–o), consistent with our previous profiling illustrating no off-target toxicity (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

To investigate the impact of STM9005 on normal cells, we assessed the effects of STM9005 on primary cells ex vivo. STM9005 treatment did not impair the colony-forming potential of WT mouse bone marrow cells (Extended Data Fig. 8a) or primary human CD34+ cord blood cells (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Similarly, treatment of primary human hepatocytes from normal donors had no effect on cell viability, in contrast to doxorubicin-treated positive controls, which showed a significant reduction in viability (Extended Data Fig. 8c).

To assess the long-term effects of STM9005 on normal tissues in vivo, WT C57BL/6J mice were treated with vehicle or 30 mg per kg STM9005 twice daily for 12 days, and samples were collected 30 days after treatment. The organ weights of the spleen, liver and kidneys showed no differences between treatment groups (Extended Data Fig. 8e–g, respectively). Moreover, there was no differential impact observed on bone marrow cellularity (Extended Data Fig. 8h) and colony-forming potential of bone marrow cells (Extended Data Fig. 8i,j). Haematological parameters during the treatment period (day 7 after treatment initiation), including white blood cell counts, red blood cell counts, platelet counts and haemoglobin levels, were unaffected by STM9005 treatment (Extended Data Fig. 8k–n). Furthermore, haematopoietic cell composition in the bone marrow and spleen (Extended Data Fig. 8o,p), and analysis of major haematopoietic stem and progenitor compartments (Extended Data Fig. 8q–v), also revealed no significant differences between groups. Finally, histological assessment of heart, liver, kidney and spleen illustrated no morphological evidence of tissue pathology in either treatment group (Extended Data Fig. 9).

Taken together, our data show that isolated catalytic inhibition of METTL1 using STM9005 is highly efficacious in vivo, leading to detrimental effects on the proliferation of both solid and haematological malignancies, with no measurable signs of toxicity or long-term adverse effects.

The therapeutic potential of pharmacologically targeting different epitranscriptomic regulators is largely underexplored. Recently, we and others have demonstrated that small-molecule inhibition of RNA-modifying enzymes is a credible therapeutic avenue. However, most of the preclinical and clinical efforts to date have been focused on the m6A modification on poly(A)+ RNA24,25,26,27. There are numerous studies suggesting that tRNA-modifying enzymes could be therapeutic vulnerabilities in cancer6,29,36. Yet, the true therapeutic potential of targeting a tRNA-modifying enzyme pharmacologically remains unclear. Here we illustrate the development and preclinical characterization of a bona fide first-in-class tRNA methyltransferase inhibitor, STM9005—a potent, specific and bioavailable small molecule that selectively blocks the catalytic activity of the m7G-writer METTL1.

We show that pharmacological inhibition of METTL1 affects a range of aggressive malignancies through distinct cellular and molecular activity. STM9005 treatment selectively decreases the deposition of m7G on a key subgroup of tRNAs previously associated with oncogenic transformation, cancer stemness, maintenance and aggressiveness11,20,37, which in turn impacts the translation of their mRNA/protein substrates. This molecular mechanism of action affects key oncogenic and therapeutically relevant pathways, such as those related to cell cycle regulators, p53 and MYC-associated proteins, consistent with our previous findings11.

Using a specific and potent catalytic inhibitor of METTL1 enabled us to generate multiple unique datasets to examine the direct effects of inhibiting the catalytic activity of the METTL1–WDR4 complex. It also enabled us to investigate the therapeutic potential of METTL1 inhibition, illustrating robust and on-target anti-proliferative efficacy of STM9005 in vivo using a range of aggressive mouse and human cancer models, including PDXs. Although we illustrated robust tumour growth inhibition in vivo, we did not observe tumour regression, potentially due to short periods of STM9005 dosing. These findings pave the way for further investigations, including possible combinatorial treatments of STM9005 with other cancer drugs, ultimately leading to the improvement of outcomes of current anti-cancer treatments.

Overall, this study provides a strong proof of concept of the in vivo activity and therapeutic efficacy of a specific and selective tRNA-methyltransferase inhibitor against cancer, identifying this class of inhibitors as a promising opportunity for the development of therapies against malignancies with a clear unmet medical need.
