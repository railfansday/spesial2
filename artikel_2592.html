Fiorentina’s Mastantuono problem isn’t just about Mastantuono

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

As I wrote yesterday about Radu Drǎgușin, Fiorentina has a lot of problems and focusing in on a single one risks missing the bigger picture. However, I'd argue that, over the course of the season, the biggest problem has been Franco Mastantuono. The young Argentine has shown tantalizing glimpses of the player he could become but hasn't contributed to winning for a team that desperately needs winning contributions.

I want to make it very clear that it's weird to criticize a 19-year-old, even one as precocious as Mastantuono. International superstardom is a tough row to hoe and I can't imagine the weight of expectations are easily borne by any shoulders, much less a teenager's. It's worth remembering that he's in his first season as a full-time starter in Europe, in a new country, learning a new language. For the first time in his career, he isn't employed by a juggernaut but by a club that, despite its ambitions, is mid-table fodder. His head is probably spinning and he deserves credit for what he's done well.

I want to list some of the things he's done well, too, because this article is going to focus on the other stuff. Mastantuono wants to work. He covers a lot of ground and plays with some fire in his belly. Unlike many luxury players, he isn't afraid to stick his foot in. He gets booted up in the air every game and never complains; he tries to play through contact instead of going to ground every time someone breathes on him. That fits in with his on-pitch character: he wants to be the best player. He wants all the responsibility of on-ball creativity and the spotlight that comes with it. He never hides. He never drifts out of games. He's obviously got a tremendous reservoir of self-belief, a mental strength that will stand him in good stead throughout his career.

For each of those positives qualities, though, there's a negative corr0lary. For all his running around, Mastantuono doesn't really help the team. He loves charging forward to close down a defender when the rest of the team has dropped into a deep block, which deforms the shape and forces everyone to rotate around much earlier than they ought to. It's the same with his tackles: he'll challenge for the ball but he's very bad at it, usually lunging as an opponent goes by. That also puts him out of position and forces his teammates to cover him, leaving their own assignments and yeah, you know what happens next. The whole shape collapses.

Mastantuono is supposed to be an individualist, of course. Real Madrid didn't send him to Florence to learn how to be a cog in a mid-table machine; it sent him to Florence to get as many on-ball reps as he could. By pushing the boundaries of what he's able to do, he'll expand his game, even if the immediate results are often frustrating. Superstars aren't supposed to worry about consequences. That's for the water carriers. The peons. Everyone else.

In Mastantuono's case, though, I think this course is a mistake for two reasons. First, he's not that kind of player. While he's quick and slippery, he lacks the elite physical gifts to blow by defenders. He's a good dribbler but not good enough to wriggle away from multiple markers. He's clever but lacks the galaxy-brained creativity to outfox a set defense. Maybe he'll develop one of those qualities but it looks more like he's banging his head into the flimsy cement of the Franchi over and over. You can push the boundaries of your ability but only so far. There's a maximum extent beyond which you cannot cross no matter how much you want to and how much you work.

That leads me to the second reason I think he's doing himself a disservice. If Mastantuono sees his future at Real Madrid, it probably won't be as a Kylian Mbappe or a Cristiano Ronaldo or one of the other all-time greats the club builds around. No, I think Mastantuono's best chance to stick is as a more functional player. Madrid always needs one attacker who does the dirty work of tracking back and running off ball and making simple passes to his more illustrious teammates. It's the only way to balance the team. Because Mastantuono's got a good engine, his best chance to stick at the Bernabeu is to embrace the dirty work. Instead, he's doing the exact opposite in Florence.

It's a tough situation for the kid. I think he's doing exactly as he was instructed by Jose Mourinho and the Real Madrid brass. The egocentric style of play doesn't necessarily reflect him as a player. It reflects him doing what his coaches told him to do this year at some anonymous Italian team that's lucky to bask in the reflected glory of his brilliance. If that doesn't help his team, well, he'll be gone in a few months. Just endure some discomfort, then come back home and see if you're ready to play for a real team.

And there's the root of the problem. Mastantuono's purpose this season isn't aligned with Fiorentina's. His job is to get better as a player regardless of impact on the team. If his improvement helps the team, that's an incidental bonus. The Viola, on the other hand, need consistent excellence from Mastantuono because they're contractually obligated to start him in as many games as possible. They need him to maybe not prostrate himself at the altar of team success but at least bow.

It's a situation that probably hasn't helped the team spirit. Here's this kid, this famous kid who's accomplished not much in his brief career, guaranteed a starting job regardless of performance. While he seems respectful enough, at the end of the day he's not accountable to Paolo Vanoli or to his current teammates; he's accountable to Real Madrid. That preferential treatment can destroy a team's morale and thus its effectiveness. Last year's swoon was partly motivated by Moise Kean's unique ability to avoid reprimand for things his colleagues would've been pulled up for. Mastantuono has done less than Kean in Serie A. The algebra is as simple as it is inevitable.

A dry loan for a budding talent doesn't scupper other clubs, of course, and there have been plenty of similar cases that were mutually beneficial. For both player and club to succeed in these circumstances requires a unity of organizational purpose, a long-term vision for the club, and expertise in combining them. It's not Mastantuono's fault that Fiorentina hasn't shown competence in any of those areas in years. For all the money spent this summer, there's one thing that the Viola can't buy: leadership. And until they find another way to acquire it, situations like Mastantuono's will be regular symptoms of an underlying condition.
