Patient ‘horrified’ to find her sensitive discussions were shared with more than 100,000 NHS staff

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Exclusive: Investigation launched after details of grief and trauma uploaded, raising questions over extent of issue

The Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) has launched an investigation after a patient was “horrified” to discover that notes of sensitive discussions with her psychotherapist had been shared with more than 100,000 NHS workers.

Rosanna (not her real name) found out that her detailed therapist’s notes, including about her grief and trauma, had been uploaded to her patient records when she saw them on a computer screen during a visit to her GP.

Inquiries made by Rosanna under freedom of information laws revealed that her therapy notes had been accessed by staff who did not work at the hospital trust where she was being treated.

Her discovery led to her stopping the treatment she was receiving for bipolar disorder with South London and Maudsley NHS trust (SLaM). It meant she had no mental health support during a close family bereavement.

Rosanna’s case has prompted questions in parliament, and suggests many other patients’ psychotherapy notes, including those of children, could have been shared without their knowledge or consent. This week the ICO confirmed it would investigate the case after a request to expedite it by the former Labour minister Philip Hunt.

Her case also raises questions about whether the new single patient record, proposed under the health bill, will include intimate details from any psychotherapy sessions provided by the NHS.

SLaM said it was reviewing how it documented psychotherapy on electronic patient records and how it communicated how data was shared.

Rosanna’s psychotherapy notes were uploaded to the London Care Record (LCR), an electronic patient record accessible to up 140,000 NHS staff, at a time of increasing concern about health workers snooping on patients.

She said: “I felt horrified looking over my GP’s shoulder as they checked what I had told them about my therapy records being available on the LCR. It’s like discovering that your personal diary has been photocopied and placed in a library.”

SLaM said “no intimate details” from therapy sessions had been shared, but in a response to Rosanna it admitted that “key session content” from therapeutic discussions was automatically uploaded. It also told her: “Very private material can be shared in the context of treatment.”.

In September Rosanna attempted to clarify exactly what had been shared on her medical notes by submitting a subject access request to SLaM. So far SLaM has failed to respond to that request.

She is concerned that sharing therapy notes, including those of children, could present a danger to patients.

She said: “Where trauma is involved, it can create a safety risk. You don’t want the most intimate parts of your life getting into the hands of the wrong person. SLaM’s own list of what it shares includes all events from its child and adolescent mental health services.”

She added: “When I asked what was being shared, a senior therapist told me he didn’t know all the answers. SLaM now accepts staff and patients may not have been given enough information.”

Rosanna said there should have been a choice to opt out of such information being shared, but SLaM had failed to inform her that her therapy notes would be available to tens of thousands of staff.

Her MP, Bell Ribeiro-Addy, asked the government what guidance had been issued to mental health trusts on sharing therapy notes. James Frith, a junior health minister, replied that all records “relating to direct care are shared on a basis of implied consent”.

Rosanna said: “It’s implicit that therapy is confidential unless you’re told otherwise, within the normal safeguarding limits. Patients should be told where their data is going. You can’t object to something if you don’t know it’s happening and you haven’t been told.

“I paused my psychotherapy because I couldn’t be confident where what I said in treatment would end up. I’ve been without mental health secondary care support since, including while a parent was seriously ill and after they died.

“I am concerned other people receiving therapy from SLaM will see this and, like me, pause treatment. I would urge you not to, and to learn about your data rights instead.”

Rosanna supports the new planned single patient record, but thinks the focus should be on sharing medical details, not the contents of therapy sessions. “Transparency over what will be on the shared record and the option to opt out is essential,” she said.

Prof Derek Tracy, SLaM’s chief medical officer executive, said: “We apologise again to the patient for the delays in responding to her concerns.

“We put the utmost importance on patient confidentiality and the trust placed in us by our patients and service users. We have clear guidance on limiting the information recorded. This includes any significant changes or themes, risks and agreed actions.

Andy Bell, the chief executive of the Centre for Mental Health, said: “Normally when you start therapy there’s a discussion about confidentiality. The therapist will say ‘If you tell me something that creates a safeguarding risk to yourself or others I will have to tell x y and z’. Not everything can remain confidential.

“A situation like this does raise issues about the boundaries of what is shareable and what isn’t, and what is appropriate to go on someone’s notes and what absolutely shouldn’t.

“If a patient is finding out information about themselves on the system that they didn’t expect, that suggests that the system isn’t working properly.”
