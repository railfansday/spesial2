CBS’ ‘Lincoln Lawyer’ Replacement Is Officially Making a Major Change

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. CBS has moved Cupertino to a better time slot. The network announced that its new legal series, starring Mike Colter, will move to Sunday evenings starting January 17, 2027, at 10 p.m. ET — a major change for a show that debuted just a few months ago. That this format will be much more successful in terms of attracting an audience has to do with the fact that there is no better way to watch a show about corporate power, complex cases, and those who are ready to bring Silicon Valley to justice than at a time that is usually taken by the most popular dramas on TV.

The decision is also sensible as Cupertino will air right after Marshals and Tracker, which happen to be among the most-watched television programs. This is definitely a great position for a new series. CBS is giving the King team a chance to reach an audience motivated to watch on Sunday nights, as the trial in this time slot on November 8 showed Cupertino drew its biggest audience of the season after 60 Minutes.

The move comes as CBS makes several mid-season schedule adjustments. Cupertino originally premiered on Thursday, October 8, at 10 p.m., following Elsbeth. Its departure will open the door for Matlock Season 3 to take over that time slot beginning January 21.

For Cupertino, though, the Sunday placement is arguably the more interesting development. The series will have two established broadcast hits leading into its episodes, which could make it easier for curious viewers to stick around and discover something new. CBS clearly saw enough potential in the November 8 broadcast to give the experiment a more permanent home.

Television audiences have no shortage of crime procedurals and legal dramas, but Cupertino has a slightly different concern. Its cases frequently involve technology companies, artificial intelligence, and the increasingly complicated relationship between consumers and businesses collecting their personal information. These aren’t particularly farfetched subjects anymore; in fact, the series sometimes feels as though it is struggling to keep up with current developments in Silicon Valley.

Robert and Michelle King are credited with the show's concept, and they previously worked on shows like The Good Wife, The Good Fight, and Evil. Their previous projects have focused on powerful, party-centric institutions, especially when they have something to cover up. This story also brings the tech industry into focus.

Colter plays Michael Price, a lawyer on the receiving end of a Silicon Valley start-up's business tactics. After being thrown out of a company he founded, Michael learns that the stocks he expected to sell have also become a concern. The plot intensifies when Olivia Siffre (Rachel Keller), another lawyer who was fired by the same company, seeks a solution. Ultimately, they decide to open their own practice representing people mistreated by the technology industry.

Michael and Olivia aren't trying to take on Silicon Valley, but they will take on companies other lawyers won’t challenge. This difference opens up many possibilities for the storyline to explore inequality between ordinary citizens and corporations with virtually unlimited resources. Multiple references to artificial intelligence can be spotted in the series. One plot revolves around an AI model that might be harmful, while other episodes explore robo-misconduct, prediction markets, and the adverse effects of online surveillance. More importantly, the show doesn't portray the technology as dangerous in itself.

The show’s ensemble is another reason CBS should feel comfortable giving it a more prominent time slot. Colter makes for an appealing lead, bringing the same presence that helped make Evil such an enjoyable watch. Keller provides a compelling counterpart as Olivia, whose personal convictions frequently inform how she approaches her work.

The show also features other strong performances. Ella Stiller portrays Christy Palladino, a former firm member who becomes a key player in its work; Nik Dodani plays Vikram, a tech geek; and Busy Philipps addsinteresting plot twists as Natalia Boutella, a rich investor with a complicated relationship with Silicon Valley. Rounding out the cast, Renée Elise Goldsberry plays Nichole Ferebee, whose relationship with Michael is complicated by their romantic past.

The series strikes a good balance of personalities, and can get into genuinely unsettling territory when it discusses AI and corporate misconduct, but it rarely loses sight of the fact that viewers also want interesting characters and entertaining legal battles. Critics seem to agree, as Cupertino currently holds a 100% approval rating on Rotten Tomatoes, with an average score of 8.5 out of 10, an impressive reception for a new broadcast series, especially one attempting to make complicated questions about the technology industry accessible to a mainstream audience.

CBS has valid reasons to take action in this case. Moving to a new time slot may mean the network wants to capitalize on the show's early success rather than stick with Thursday. Moreover, the series could gain new audiences right after Marshals and Tracker, since viewers who like traditional procedural stories might appreciate this one as well. Changing the time period alone may not be enough to resolve all the issues a new show might have. Nevertheless, Cupertino has a strong creative team, a great cast, and a track record of delivering better material.
