Brad White challenges Florida defense to eliminate explosive runs

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Florida's defense needed more than a schematic adjustment to recover from one of its roughest halves of the season. It also needed a change in focus.

After allowing Vanderbilt to score on five of its first six possessions, the Gators limited the Commodores to three second-half points and closed the 34-27 victory with consecutive sacks. Defensive coordinator Brad White attributed the turnaround to improved communication, leverage, pad level and composure.

White also discussed the development of Ty Jackson and Jayden Woods, the importance of eliminating explosive runs and his personal history playing and coaching at Florida State.

Here's everything White had to say ahead of Week 13's rivalry game in Tallahassee.

It was nothing sort of mind-blowing from an adjustment standpoint.

It was as much about controlling the unbalanced formations, the shifting in and out and making sure the communication was clean. Then it was going back to the fundamentals.

I didn't think we played with great pad level. I didn't think we played with a great understanding of where our leverage was. I don't think we played with a great understanding of, "What is the formation? Who's in the game?"

We talked all week about some formation and personnel tells. Those things held up. We knew some of the shifting and motion were coming, but some of it got the guys a little out of whack and uncomfortable from a communication standpoint.

When you feel uncomfortable and then they start moving the ball a little bit, it really puts you on edge. You're kind of on tilt. It was just about getting settled down.``

They did a nice job of being calm in the locker room and understanding where we were in the game.

Obviously, the big play right before halftime — you never want to give one of those up. We would've loved to go get that ball and give it back to the offense on a 50-50 ball. But that's football. Those plays happen. You've got to regroup.

I was proud of their effort in the second half, but we need to start faster.

It has been a tale of two seasons. During the first half of the season, we would start the first quarter really fast, and then we'd fizzle. During the back half of the season, we've come out a little bit slowly and then picked it up.

Here we are in Game 12. Hopefully, we can put one together from start to finish. We need to come out of the gate, and then we need to sustain it.

I think it's a sign of growth for the players, and I think it's a sign of growth for the coaching staff.

For me, it's how I handle those guys at halftime. After that South Carolina game, we've talked about it, I changed how I addressed them at halftime. I think the response has been better.

That's a learned thing for me as well. It's not just them and how they handle the coaching. It's about how I present the coaching, how we get those things taught and, as a coaching staff, how we handle them at halftime.

That has been positive for all sides involved. It has led to solid results for the most part in the second half.

Obviously, it's a huge play. Sometimes, you need a little bit of luck to go your way.

You're not teaching your players, "Hey, I want you to rip the quarterback's helmet off on a sneak." Now, that may be the new way to stop the tush push. You can't grab him by the facemask, so just grab the quarterback by the back of the helmet or something.

It was a freaky, fluke play, and it went our way. I'm glad it happened.

Obviously, it created huge momentum to give the ball to the offense on a short field. But there's nothing you can teach from that as a teaching moment.

Even this late in the season, you can't lose sight of it.

You've been banging all season through a nine-game SEC schedule. You've got a huge rivalry game. Our guys understand that, even on a short week, we've got to put the work in.

If you think, "I've banged enough. I'll just show up and play with good pad level and a good strike," you're wrong. You have to do it every day.

We did it today. We banged a significant amount, emphasizing footwork, hand placement and pad level because those are things that show up on Saturday.

They also show up when you don't do them right. That's what showed against Vanderbilt in the first half.

If I wasn't vocal and didn't get guys lined up, I wouldn't have been on the field.

My job was, "If you're going to be the least athletic guy on the field, align the 10 athletes correctly, make sure they know where to go and then keep yourself out of the way."

I think I can help guys in that regard because I've been in those shoes. I understand how vital and important it is to be a communicator, especially in the middle of the defense.

I can demonstrate it for them. I can walk out and say, "Guys, this is how loud you need to be. This is how demonstrative you need to be."

If you need to walk up and tap somebody, go walk up and tap No. 3 or No. 5. If they don't hear you, those defensive linemen have an offensive lineman six inches away trying to rip their faces off. They're not listening to you. They're worried about that guy.

Sometimes, you've got to go down there and tell them. Sometimes, you've got to go down there and move them.

It starts in practice. I stand behind the offense. If I can't hear you while standing behind the offense, you didn't say it loudly enough. I want to hear that communication.

The older guys, the A.C.s and the Jaden Robinsons and Myles Grahams, you can hear them. You can hear the vocal communication.

It's the same thing with our safeties. You've got to be vocal.

If you're talking to somebody who's turned around, they can't see your face. You have to talk loudly enough to get through to them.

You have to be really loud. You have to be louder than you think you need to be out there.

Then you put that in The Swamp. I'm yelling from the sideline to a corner, and he can barely hear me. Out on the field, they have to be really loud and demonstrative and use hand signals and all that.

But even with the missed tackle, if we leverage No. 2 and then leverage with the corner, it should be an 8-yard gain.

Those are the frustrating pieces. Fundamental football is leveraging the football and understanding, "If I have to set the edge, it may not be in the box. It can be outside our slot receiver or outside the No. 1 receiver."

I've got to understand where I have to funnel the ball.

Usually, big runs happen as a result of missed tackles or misfits. When you have a missed tackle and a misfit from a leverage standpoint on the same play, it usually results in a big run.

All it takes is one. That's the frustrating piece.

Take out the sacks. They had 43 carries for 178 yards. Take out the negatives for sacks, and all of a sudden, it's 40 for 197.

Five of those runs went for 128 yards, which means 35 runs went for 69 yards. That ended up being about 1.97 yards per rush.

I told the defense, "You can play 35 really good plays. Thirty-five really good plays. But if you give up five explosives, it doesn't matter how good those 35 were."

If you get 35 out of 40 on an English test, you might be really happy with that grade. You can't do that on defense.

You get one mistake. We don't want to give up any explosives, but you can give up one.

You can't give up five runs of 13 or more yards. It can't happen.
