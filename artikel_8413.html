Te Araroa day 33: Beautiful but brutal

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

24.5km, Daly’s Clearing hut to Te Rereatukahia hut

Note: As I’ve mentioned in the past, Lindsay is also a Trek blogger. Although we’ve been covering the same ground for a while, her blog is much funnier than mine and definitely worth reading. Undoubtedly her account of the CSQ, and this day in particular, will be quite a different experience!

I was a bit nervous on this morning. As the creator of the CSQ and the one that had encouraged everyone to join, I felt very responsible for everyone else’s enjoyment of the trip. There was no TA trust to blame for poor route choices, only me! And I was beginning to suspect I might have made some poor route choices.

Back in September, I had come to the Kaimai mountains as my last overnight tramp before starting TA. I did a 46km loop from Franklin Road, along the Old North-South route to Te Rereatukahia hut, and back via the ‘new’ North-South route. DOC specifically recommends the ‘new’ route, but at the time I felt that the old route was better – a little more rugged, sure, but better views (at least if the weather had been more cooperative) and more repeated ups and downs, which I found easier than the long ups and down of the new route. But now I was dragging my friends along with me, I was starting to have my doubts. I am famously speedy, and what had been a 10 hour day for me before could easily turn into a much longer one at a slightly slower pace. Because of the route we had chosen (the easier but longer one), it would have made more sense to stay at Waitāwheta hut the previous night, rather than Daly’s Clearing. Doing that would take 2 hours off what promised to be a long day. But I hadn’t thought of that until we were all settled at the hut, and it was too late.

I tried to keep my worries to myself. I wasn’t having a great morning, though – we had agreed to leave at 6.30am, and I was struggling to get things packed up in time. The old saying ‘more haste, less speed’ definitely applied to me on this morning. Eventually I was ready and we set off. I was looking forward to the first section, which I knew would be beautiful and easy.

The Waitāwheta tramway track was just as pretty as I remembered it, and I was happy that everyone else thought so too. It runs along the river as it goes through gorges, and crosses beautiful big suspension bridges. It’s honestly quite surprising that it isn’t better known. If anyone reading this happens to be living in Auckland, Hamilton or Tauranga and wants an easy, kid-friendly overnight tramp, this would be my top recommendation. The hut is one of the big modern ones, and you can walk there in a few hours from the road end.

Annabel admiring the view from the first suspension bridge

We had a snack break at the hut, and topped up on water. In hindsight we should have filled up a bit more! We set off again, and I soon left the others behind. I felt like moving a bit faster, even though I knew that as long as the others were behind me it wouldn’t make a difference. We had agreed to wait for each other at the junctions so we could touch base as we moved through the day at our own speeds. There were an annoying number of downed trees to scramble over, and some of them were quite awkward. Having done this track several times before, I knew that there was a bit of a climb at the end that would take us up to the ridgeline we would be walking. I kept thinking I was coming up to it, but apparently I didn’t know the track as well as I thought! I also passed lots of streams, and I did think about topping my water up some more. But I didn’t really want to stop, and I usually don’t drink that much so I figured it would be fine.

Despite my eagerness to get there, I almost walked right past the junction with the old North-South route, which would make up the rest of the day. This section is only 11km but last time I did it, it took me around 6 hours. I dug out some photos from last time to see what time I had arrived back then. It seemed like a similar time – around 11.15 – but the clocks have changed since then, and I wasn’t sure if my phone had accounted for that. I decided to eat lunch while I waited for the others.

The scary sign. I agree it’s only suitable for experienced trampers, but I stand by my claim that this track is not ‘extremely rough.’ It’s actually reasonably maintained.

I waited a while for everyone else, around half an hour. Then like buses, they all turned up at once. First came Sinéad – ‘Lindsay was just behind me, but Annabel stopped for a break so I don’t know how far behind she is.’ Then a few minutes later, there was Lindsay – ‘Annabel is behind me, I could hear her moving through the trees.’ Then finally Annabel arrived. I sat around for a bit longer, then realised that I didn’t need to. I was done with lunch and only getting stiff from sitting still. I momentarily thought about asking the others how much water they had and if I could have some, but decided better of it. It would be fine!

I could immediately see that the track was much drier than the last time I walked it. That made sense, as it was late winter then and early summer now, but it was a relief as I had promised the others that it would be less muddy than the Dome and I was beginning to doubt my recollection. I was happy to be back in nicer weather – on my previous trip, a cloudy start had turned into high winds, low visibility, and dampness that would have been rain if it hadn’t been pulverised by the wind before it had a chance to fall. Ideal conditions to walk along a ridge! I probably should have turned back when it was getting worse, but I was so keen to complete my trip that I kept going. Not very good decision making, in hindsight.

Anyway, now that the weather was clear I was taking a million photos. It was beautiful! The track went up and down constantly, but I don’t mind that too much because then as soon as my uphill muscles are tired I’m going downhill again, and vice versa. I was getting a bit worried about how much water I had left though, and was trying to ration it.

About an hour in, I checked the time and my map and was pleased to see how much distance I’d covered. About 2km, which was about what I expected on this kind of terrain. Shortly afterwards I passed a DOC sign indicating that it would be 4 hours to the hut. I would be very impressed if it only took 4 hours! Normally DOC times are overly generous, but this track is one of the notable exceptions.

An hour or two passed in much the same vein. Puffing up the hills, picking my way carefully down the descents, stopping occasionally to find the way. I was in a flatter, muddier area and was getting a bit tired so I stoped for a snack break. I was very surprised to hear someone call my name, and turned to see Annabel. She said she was finding it pretty tough but she was also running out of water, and wanted to get there asap. She also seemed to think we weren’t too far away, and I was sorry to inform her it was still some way to go.

Annabel wasn’t far behind me for the rest of the day, and would often catch up when I had a break. At one point I heard some rustling in the bushes, and paused for a moment thinking it would be a deer. I waited a little longer and heard some huffing and grunting, and realised it was probably a pig. I don’t know anything about pigs, but it sounded mad. I stood for a moment, not really sure what to do and then because the huffing didn’t stop decided it was best to keep moving and hopefully get out of its territory. I thought about shouting back to Annabel, but I wasn’t sure how far away she was and didn’t know if it would help much if she knew about the pig anyway. I realised I don’t really know what to do if there are pigs. New Zealand is famous for not having dangerous animals but sometimes I think they forget to tell you about things like pigs on the land and sharks in the water! Yes, there are sharks in New Zealand, no I didn’t know that till recently either.

2 hours to the hut is believable, but 3.5 hours to Mt Te Aroha is crazy

At some point along the route, I knew that there was a sign with an estimated walking time at the old turning for the track to Cashmore’s clearing. The track is now closed but the sign is still there. As I recalled, this sign was about halfway through the day so when I was 3-4 hours in and I still hadn’t seen it, I started getting pretty worried. But then I looked at the map and realised that the sign was in fact only 4 km from the end of the day, and I was actually quite nearly there. I was very relieved! When I arrived, it said it was two hours to the end, which I remembered being accurate for me. And the weather had got pretty bad by that point too.

There are more exposed ridgeline sections later in the route, and it was nice this time to not be worried every time I came around the corner and saw an exposed bit. The wind was so strong last time that I was a bit concerned for my safety in some of these sections. Again no, I probably should not have been out there! But this time I was able to enjoy the views and take some photos.

I also got to properly see Wāhine rock. Last time it was wreathed in shifting clouds, which was very atmospheric but not conducive to a good view. By this point though I was pretty pooped. The last few kilometres stretched on forever, and I was nearly out of water. At last I came to nearly the final hill. The track went down a fair bit, and I suddenly remembered that this must be the scrambly uphill bit. I had remembered a very steep, hands-required section somewhere on the route, but thought it was much earlier on. But apparently it was here, right at the end. I made it up with several sweetie breaks. And then it was the final hill. It’s grassy rather than bushy, so has great views from the top. I drank my last couple of mouthfuls of water, and made my tired legs begin the climb. Partway up, I turned and saw Annabel. I yelled to her that it wasn’t far now. At the top, I saw a nice rock and collapsed on it to admire the view.

As I started walking down the hill, I saw the hut shining below me. What a glorious sight. There’s nothing better at the end of a long day! Annabel was a bit behind me still, but shouted to me when she saw it too, and I waited for her. We walked the last bit down together.

Arriving at the hut, we both dumped our packs on the porch and grabbed our filters. Water was the priority here! As we re-hydrated ourselves, we sat on the porch in a bit of a daze. We had arrived a bit after 6, making this an 11.5 hour day. I’m pretty sure this is my longest ever in terms of time, although it was only 25km. Eventually we pulled ourselves together to go inside and cook dinner. Also staying that night at the hut were three teenage girls who were making up characters for a game of D&D. They were clearly learning about respiration and photosynthesis in biology at school, and I smiled to myself as they made their characters’ part of the ‘Rubisco army’ that fought in the ‘Kreb’s war.’

As we are dinner, Annabel and I became a bit worried about the others. It was getting on for 7pm and they hadn’t appeared yet. I decided to send them both an InReach message. After waiting a bit, I decided to sit and eat my dinner outside where I could see the final descent to the hut. And then I heard some voices! It sounded like Sinéad. I walked up the track a bit to meet them, and was glad to see they were both still smiling, although obviously tired.

As it turned out, Lindsay and Sinéad had also run out of water. As Lindsay said; ‘At one point I was jealous of the mud, because it was wet.’ I felt the need to apologise to everyone for vastly underselling the difficulty of this day. I had genuinely thought it wasn’t bad when I had done it before. I still had fun this time around, but it would have been nice if it was an hour shorter. I don’t think I’ll be allowed to organise any more side quests…
