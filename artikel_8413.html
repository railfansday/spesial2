Week 19 on the CDT

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Total elevation gain so far: 121,562m/398,825ft (equivalent of 13.7 Everests)

I’m writing this update from what I expect to be my final resupply town, as we plan the last few sections to take us through to the border with Mexico.

Progress through New Mexico remains swift, with the flatter terrain allowing for big miles each day. The elevation is also dropping, from 8000ft to 5000ft which brings warmer temperatures and more pleasant hiking.

There is one final twist in this journey however, as we have just been notified that the final 2 miles as you approach the border (and terminus monument) is temporarily closed for construction. This means that some alternative options are needed: finish the hike early before reaching the terminus, find an alternative finishing point somewhere else along the border, wait it out until the construction work has finished and the area is reopened. None of these options are ideal, and may extend the hike for another week.

Of all the wildlife I had on my list to see during the CDT, there was one still remaining that was proving elusive – a big cat. These are notoriously rare to see, with both mountain lions and cougars wary of people and hide during the day. For the last couple of days we had seen occasional paw prints in the sand that looked feline, and about 4 inches wide.

The following evening I was hiking late and just after dusk I saw a pair of eyes shining back at me from the bushes. A deer, I thought, as is normal on trail. But instead of darting off, the eyes stayed focussed on me and slowly moving toward me. I could just about make out the outline of its back and shoulders, prowling forward with its head lowered, perfectly still. A cougar. And it was stalking me! Making loud noises and banging my hiking poles together did nothing to deter it, so I backed up along the trail, all the time keeping my headlamp focussed on the eyes shining back at me. It matched my movement step for step, moving silently through the bushes about 30 yards away from me. I continued along the trail, as at least it was no longer advancing towards me, and instead just moving sideways keeping track of me. After a few minutes (which felt much longer) it paused its stalking and just watched me as I put some distance between us. I was doing continuous 360 degree checks for the next few miles!

This was then contrasted by the overly friendly welcome I received at Doc Campbell’s Post, which has 3 young cats (and a very cute dog) to welcome hikers, demand to be fussed over, and try to take some of your food if you leave it unattended for even a second.

Road walks continue to be a feature of this section of the CDT in New Mexico, partly due to the amount of private land, and partly due to having to avoid deep water river crossings. While these are relatively easy and fast, they are also tough on the feet and monotonous. It is hard to stay motivated when you have a 20 or 30 mile walk ahead of you on the same road, which stretches off into the distance.

Over the course of the CDT you meet many hikers time and again, as you cross paths, take different alternates, and have different resupply strategies. I started in Montana in a group of 4, but over the course of time we became separated, only seeing each other occasionally at town stops. During this section however I reconnected with Banshee and BumbleBerry, and is great to be hiking again with them.

The weather has also bunched people up, and it’s been fun to be at a location sheltering from the rain, welcoming them in as they arrive wet and bedraggled, sharing stories of the trail conditions. By the end of the day we had 13 hikers at Doc Campbell’s!

One feature we were all looking forward to was the Gila river, famous for it’s winding path through the valley that it has etched out among the volcanic rock. It features over 200 river crossings along its course, but after recent rains and with more on the way, we chose the safer option and remained on the high route using mainly roads. In some parts, the water crossings were chest deep!

There is also lots of history in this area, with petroglyphs and cave dwellings from the 1200s to explore.
