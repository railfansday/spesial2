Spotify’s founder wants to scan you

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Neko Health brings AI-powered body scans to the U.S. for $499, but its expansion raises a bigger question

A version of this article originally appeared in Quartz’s members-only Weekend Brief newsletter. Quartz members get access to exclusive newsletters and more. Sign up here.

Two decades ago, Daniel Ek got people to pay for music they used to pirate. Now the Spotify $SPOT co-founder is asking them to pay $499 to get a look at their own bodies.

Neko Health, the startup Daniel Ek co-founded in 2018, opened its first U.S. clinic in New York on Sept. 24. More than 50,000 New Yorkers had joined the waitlist before the doors opened, and the company said more than 350,000 people worldwide had signed up for a scan or a place in line.

The hour-long visit includes a 53-marker blood panel, more than 6,000 high-resolution photos of the skin, a look at heart and circulation, and a grip strength test. AI flags suspicious moles for a human clinician to review.

Insurance does not cover it, though the scans are eligible for health savings accounts. Ek said Miami and Washington, DC would follow, with more cities over the next one to two years. Until then, the waitlist is the closest most people could get.

Neko arrived in a crowded health tracker market, and not a particularly successful one. Fitbit launched its first tracker in 2009, and Jawbone, once valued at $3 billion, followed with the UP band in 2011. Jawbone liquidated in 2017, and Google $GOOGL later bought Fitbit for $2.1 billion.

Still, the wearable refused to die — a 2024 JAMA study found that 40 percent of Americans wore a tracker — whether or not it ever made the money its makers wanted.

Even the latest AI-backed attempt with Oura, a ring that tracks 50 health metrics and is backed by Salesforce $CRM chief executive Marc Benioff and Dell $DELL founder Michael Dell, had to postpone its IPO last month. Even with a reported 5.7 million paying customers, it cited market uncertainty after planning to go public at a valuation near $15 billion.

Lab tests have been an even harder sell. Theranos promised answers from a finger prick and 23andMe promised health insights from a tube of spit, and both collapsed. Newer companies tried again, with Function Health selling annual blood panels for $365 and landing $450 million in growth financing in July, while Superpower charged $199 a year for more than 100 biomarkers.

Then came the whole body. DEXA scans, X $TWTR-ray tests that measured fat, muscle and bone, had been sold in gyms and offices for about a decade, with BodySpec charging around $50. Prenuvo charged $2,499 for a whole-body MRI that took it further, and Kim Kardashian posted about hers in 2023. Neko’s scan is not an MRI, but it sold for a fifth of the price.

American healthcare was built to treat infectious disease, Ek told the All-In podcast, so it rewarded fixing people once they were sick. Employer-based insurance made it worse, he said, because workers changed jobs every few years. Why would an insurer pay for a prevention bet that took 10 or 20 years to come good?

Neko’s answer was to skip the insurer and ask the individual to pay. But the company’s own numbers showed the limits of that bet.

After more than 100,000 scans, Ek said about 1% of members had a serious undiagnosed condition. The company presented that as evidence the product worked. But finding a condition is not the same as demonstrating that the scan improved someone's health.

Some of that 1% were real saves. The founder of the meditation app Calm said the scan caught a malignant mole on his back, and catching skin cancer early is a good outcome for anyone who can afford the visit.

For a worse result, the earlier catch was only the beginning. Neko's U.S. clinic provides screening and clinical review, but members who need further diagnosis or treatment may have to seek additional care. The $499 bought a head start on a system where the costs could dwarf it.

The harder problem was what happened after the scan. Heart disease remained the leading cause of death in America, and spotting risk was only one part of preventing it. People also needed regular access to doctors, affordable medication and the time and resources to follow medical advice.

A primary care doctor who works in a clinic for homeless patients said none of her patients wore trackers. She told the New York Times that data only mattered if someone had the agency and money to act on it. Her patients lived in circumstances “so out of their control” that more information would not help them.

That was the gap the quantified self movement keeps stepping over. Measuring was the cheap part. The follow-up, the medication and the time off work stayed out of reach for the people with the most to gain.
