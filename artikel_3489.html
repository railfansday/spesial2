The End Of Oak Street Ending And Time Travel Explained: What Really Happened & Who Survives

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Warning: This post contains spoilers for The End of Oak StreetThere has been a long line of dinosaur-centered movies in the last few decades. Jurassic Park kicked off a years-long fascination that audiences have collectively had with the prehistoric animals. Now, there’s a new sci-fi movie that joins the growing list of dinosaur movies.

The End of Oak Street, which is streaming on HBO Max now, tells the story of a family who are put in a terrifying situation when their neighborhood is suddenly surrounded by Earth as it was millions of years ago. The family must not only survive, but to somehow escape from the dinosaurs as well.

The End of Oak Street features an all-star cast, led by Ewan McGregor and Anne Hathaway. Their star power helps elevate the Twilight Zone-like story above some of its sillier moments (including the now notorious sight of two giant dinosaurs mating over a house). They're joined by support from Maisy Stella and Christian Convery as their kids, and Jordan Alexa Davis and P.J. Byrne as neighbors. The End of Oak Street is a lot of fun, doesn't demand a lot from the audience, and plays with high-concept sci-fi in an accessible way Spielberg and JJ Abrams would be proud of.

Now that its theatrical run has come to an end, The End of Oak Street has made its streaming debut exclusively on HBO Max and can be watched on the platform now. It will also be released on HBO linear on November 14, at 8 p.m. ET. And since the ending of The End of Oak Street’s story leaves some pretty major questions unanswered, now is the perfect time to rewatch and analyze exactly what happened, and what it all means.

The End Of Oak Street begins with a simple family, with a father who’s struggling to make ends meet, a son who is having trouble with some of the other kids his age, and a mother who is keeping secrets from her family, as the parents' marriage unravels and they head towards divorce. That may all sound like a setup for a typical drama, but that's obviously not this movie, which quickly reveals itself to be a time-travel infused dinosaur caper with heavy Amblin vibes.

At first, the Platt family (understandably) have no idea their neighborhood is suddenly in prehistoric times, Anne Hathaway’s character pieces things together quickly. Each of the family members begins to piece things together until all-out dinosaur attacks begin to happen and the neighborhood is quickly wiped out.

Thanks to the lightning-like flashes and tremors the family experience one night, their entire neighborhood is teleported back, with a giant portal enveloping the few blocks around the family's home. At the same time, the same area of land from millions of years ago is teleported to the future.

Because they reappeared back in the 1980s about twenty minutes before the neighborhood disappears, Denise is able to save some of the townsfolk. She uses a phone to call her husband, who then arrives a short time later, avoiding the mysterious blast radius of the portal.

The film’s final moments also reveal that Denise saved more than just her husband. A news report shows that Denise also saves the librarian, who talks to reporters about how Hathaway’s character called her and warned her of what’s to come. The antagonistic neighbor played by P.J. Byrne is also interviewed, and says that he owes Denise his life. Jeanette's family also survives, including the version of her from earlier in the timeline, meaning there's now two Jeanettes.

Ultimately, what happened to other members of the neighborhood remains unclear. The news report still calls the event a tragedy, which implies that many likely didn’t make it in the end, and it's pretty obvious that Denise didn't warn herself or her kids, because they don't jpin them at the barbecue at the end. Denise was still able to save some of the people in her immediate vicinity, making her a hero in the eyes of the town.

When talking about the ending director David Robert Mitchell revealed that they initially entertained the idea of not going with a happy ending, but that it felt right:

"Everything was debated, but really, what was important to us was that when we go through this journey, we have these moments of suspense. There are moments of danger, there are moments of terror, but then there's also joy and humor. The audience, along with the family, go through this ride and these sometimes darker experiences. It's really about being able to take the audience to these darker places, but know that we're ultimately [within] the safety of a movie."

Ewan McGregor also revealed that there was also a darker version of the ending in which Denise, Audrey and Brian jumped too far back in time and ended up with early settlers. He says that was dropped because they wanted The End of Oak Street to close on a more hopeful note. Which JJ Abrams also echoed:

"David was the one who brought up the idea, so any ideas for the Platt family and beyond would have to come from this guy. I will say that the ending went through a few different iterations, but every time it felt like it was the proper end of this story, and that could be it."

The End of Oak Street actually does something quite novel with its approach to time travel, ignoring the rules of lots of other time-travel centered films. The David Robert Mitchell-directed film doesn’t use time machine devices, despite the portals initially appearing to be the result of some unknown tech, it's brave enough to avoid explaining every intricate detail of what’s happening.

Instead, the broad concept of mysterious portals is put forth to the audience, and that’s all that the story requires. Signs of time travel first crop up when small prehistoric plants begin growing at rapid rates in neighborhood back gardens, suggesting a secondary type of time travel is at play before the neighborhood moves in time. It's an interesting approach, that focuses more on time travel as a means for the family to come back together - admittedly through some pretty harrowing trauma.

If you pick at it too much, it may be frustrating for hard sci-fi fans, but that's not really the point here. The story also isn't bogged down in the idea of paradoxes, because if it were, the ending wouldn't actually make a lot of sense. We don't fully know if what we're viewing is a time loop, or an alternate reality, or straight time jumping, because nothing is outright stated.

It's clear that when the surviving Platts jump back through the portal, they get back to the night of the initial time jump, and Denise is able to call Greg up beyond the edge of the portal, saving him from being teleported back in time and dying. But because she saves him, she presumably dooms a version of herself and her kids to dying, because her husband saved her in the past, so she couldn't be alive to come back and save him. That's probably why hard rules aren't presented.

One thing that isn’t explicitly stated is why these tremors, portals, and wormholes started appearing in the neighborhood. It’s heavily implied that these events are the result of some sort of mysterious phenomenon, as opposed to being caused by humans but that's mostly just speculation. The news reports not revealing anything leans towards that explanation, but then, anything shady wouldn't be broadcast in the news by the government, so it's still possible.

For JJ Abrams, the not knowing was sort of the point, which he confirmed to ScreenRant exclusively: "It also undeniably felt like, "Yeah. But then what happens?" That's my favorite kind of ending, and I feel like our job is just to make a movie right now that is worth people's time. And I think Dave has done that." His goal, and that of the director, was just entertainment.

Interestingly, the existence of these types of wormholes is (somewhat) plausible. Author Carl Sagan proposed the general idea of a traversable wormhole (aka a black hole that wouldn’t immediately destroy whatever entered it), and physicists Kip Thorne and Michael Morris used that to prove that, theoretically, a wormhole like this is actually plausible (and rooted in what Albert Einstein worked on during his illustrious career).

The news report also mentions something interesting — many of the dinosaurs that came in the other direction of the wormhole were killed by humans, but some were captured for scientists to study. This highlights that, in the world of The End of Oak Street, dinosaurs exist in the 1980s. A Jurassic Park-type situation would be very possible in this world as a result of this revelation, which also sets up potential sequels for The End of Oak Street.

With the central time travel mystery unsolved and some dinosaurs left in modern times, there certainly could be potential for a sequel, though you'd have to suspect it would follow the Cloverfield model of not being directly tied to the events of the first movie. There's been no official announcement of a sequel, nor any quotes from either Mitchell or producer Abrams - though the latter did confirm this absolutely isn't another Cloverfield movie, despite the theories - so it seems unlikely that we'll get another entry.

In all likelihood, The End of Oak Street simply wasn't enough of a success for a sequel to happen. Costing an estimated $80-92 million, it made just $122 million, and Variety estimated it would lose Warner Bros. $25–40 million with marketing costs taken into account. With the new era of Warner Bros. ownership now in place, it's hard to imagine the Ellisons will be looking to push for a franchise. Still, now that it's on HBO Max, more fans should come to enjoy it for what it is: a fun dinosaur sci-fi that doesn't take itself too seriously.

The End of Oak Street is streaming on HBO Max now.
