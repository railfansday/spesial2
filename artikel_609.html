‘Bridgerton’ and ‘Mad Men’ Costumers on the ‘Magic of Costume Design’ and Why the ‘Script Is Our Bible’

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

For costume designers Janie Bryant and John Glaser, outfitting a character is another form of storytelling.

“When an actor appears on screen, you see the costume before they even say a word,” says Bryant, an Emmy winner whose credits include “Mad Men,” “Deadwood” and Taylor Sheridan shows including “Dutton Ranch” and other “Yellowstone” spinoffs. “That’s the magic of costume design.” Related Stories 'Chasing Summer' Director Josephine Decker Says That ‘Filmmaking Is Lonelier Than Ever,’ So It’s Time to Build a Community Awkwafina's ‘Kate Might Die,’ ‘Outside of Cleveland’ With Alex Wolff Among Projects Selected for U.S. in Progress

Bryant and Glaser, an Emmy winner who has worked on “Bridgerton,” “Gotham” and “Harry Potter 20th Anniversary: Return to Hogwarts,” joined Variety’s senior film reporter Rebecca Rubin in partnership with the Round Top Film Festival for a panel titled, “Who, What, Wear: The Importance of Costume in Storytelling.”

For both, their work starts with the script.

“It’s kind of our bible,” Bryant said. “We can see the period. We can read how these characters are speaking to one another, the mood, the feeling. The script is everything.”

Adds Glaser, “The script is our bible, but you have to dissect it.”

Once they have a sense of the characters, they can start to build out their looks. That involves extensive research, especially if they are curating a wardrobe from decades (or centuries) ago, rather than a modern story. In some instances, they are laboring over individually tailored items, dyeing pieces to achieve the perfect shade or sourcing the perfect item. Each costume requires multiple fittings on the actor, as well as creating duplicates for stunt doubles.

“Doing a contemporary show is a little different from doing a period show,” Bryant said. “[A period piece] is more collaborative with actors because most of the actors don’t know the period very well, so you are guiding them in the direction you want them to go.”

Glaser was a little more blunt, noting that costume designers have more control with period pieces.

“[Actors] can’t have an opinion because they don’t know what they’re talking about, which makes a big difference,” Glaser says. “Modern clothes, everybody has an opinion about everything. ‘I saw this skirt at this store and blah blah blah.’”

The costume designers have mixed feelings about incorporating actors’ input into their looks. Bryant is open to collaboration, especially when working on movies. “Film is a collaborative art, and everybody wants to feel included,” she said. “I feel like I am a good politician because I do like to hear them and then have another suggestion,” Bryant said.

Glaser has more reservations about feedback, likening a costume fitting to therapy.

“You have to find out who you’re talking to: the person, the actor or the character,” Glaser says. “I always listen if it’s coming from a place that they want to make their character better. But if it’s coming from their insecurities, you have to become the therapist.”

That aspect of the job — serving as a support system for actors — is one that Glaser and Bryant say they take seriously.

“What’s very important is that you gain the confidence of the actor in the fitting room,” Glaser says. “You have to be there when that costume is first seen to give them that support and confidence.”

Bryant adds, “When the actors come to see us, we’re the first person that they see. A lot of times they’re really scared and they don’t want to be that vulnerable, so I think having that understanding and compassion is so important.”
