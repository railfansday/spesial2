Exercise is good for your brain, but too much may alter its chemistry

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Excessive exercise may alter brain chemistry, raising dopamine levels during rest and offering clues to the sleep problems linked to overtraining.

Skipping a rest day might seem like a good way to get more out of a workout. But pushing the body too hard may also change what happens in the brain.

Mice that completed eight weeks of increasingly demanding treadmill running had higher dopamine levels in a brain region involved in memory and mood than untrained mice.

The difference appeared during their usual rest period, when this chemical, which helps regulate alertness and motivation, might affect their ability to settle down.

Serotonin, another brain chemical associated with exercise fatigue, showed no significant difference between the groups.

The finding offers a possible clue to why some overtrained athletes report poor sleep and anxiety, although the researchers measured neither.

Alisson Luiz da Rocha carried out the experiments as a doctoral student at the University of São Paulo‘s School of Physical Education and Sport of Ribeirão Preto.

“Our findings show that excessive physical exercise affects not only skeletal muscle but also has a pronounced impact on the brain,” said da Rocha.

The mice first spent five days getting used to a treadmill. They ran for 10 minutes a day at about 20 feet per minute (six meters per minute).

In the first week, they ran for 15 minutes at 60% of their top speed. By week five, the treadmill sloped 14% downhill, which puts more strain on muscles than running on level ground.

The sessions grew longer, and recovery time shrank. By week eight, the mice ran twice daily for 75 minutes each time, with only four hours between sessions.

Running tests at the start, at four weeks and at the end showed what this schedule did to their fitness. The mice improved halfway through the program, then lost those gains by the end.

Two days after the final running test, the researchers collected a brain region called the hippocampus. It helps with memory and mood and responds to exercise, including by growing new cells.

They collected samples at 6 p.m., midnight, 6 a.m. and noon, using four mice from each group at each time.

The lab lights stayed on from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., covering the daytime hours when these nocturnal animals normally rest.

At noon, the overtrained mice had more dopamine in the hippocampus than the untrained mice. They also had more tyrosine hydroxylase, an enzyme that helps make dopamine.

Serotonin remained similar between the groups, so the balance between the two chemicals shifted toward dopamine.

That noon measurement showed a difference 48 hours after the final test, but it didn’t show that dopamine stayed high throughout those two days.

A separate group of 20 mice completed just one run in the early evening. They ran at 60% of their top speed until they could no longer keep up with the treadmill.

Their dopamine levels were also higher immediately afterward.

However, levels of the enzyme that helps produce dopamine fell, along with activity in the gene responsible for making it.

The researchers think this combination might reflect fatigue, with demand for dopamine getting ahead of the machinery that makes it.

They couldn’t confirm that explanation because they measured the amount in brain tissue, not how quickly the brain produced it.

After eight weeks of overload, both dopamine and the enzyme were higher two days after testing. The authors see this as a possible sign of a longer-lasting change, beyond the immediate effects of one demanding run.

The idea that serotonin drives exercise fatigue dates to 1987. A British biochemist proposed that rising serotonin during long exercise makes the effort feel harder and encourages an athlete to stop.

The team expected serotonin to rise, or at least increase relative to dopamine. Neither happened in the single-run experiment or the eight-week program.

To reduce the chance that the chemical broke down during handling, the researchers froze each hippocampus within five minutes. A 1989 rat study using a similar method also found no change in serotonin.

Research in people has raised questions, too. When Finnish researchers scanned severely overtrained athletes a year apart, they found similar patterns in how their brains handled serotonin.

The team also examined public genetic data from 71 strains of laboratory mice. They looked for patterns linked to the gene that helps produce the dopamine-making enzyme.

Strains with more activity in that gene also showed more activity in genes involved in inflammation.

Mice from those strains froze longer during a fear test and spent less time in exposed parts of a maze, behaviors researchers associate with anxiety.

These mice also performed worse on a rotating rod used to test balance. That result can point to poorer coordination.

But these data came from other mice at rest. The team did not measure inflammation or behavior in its trained mice, so this analysis cannot show that overtraining caused those problems.

The researchers did not measure sleep, anxiety or alertness in the overtrained mice.

Higher dopamine during their usual rest period may offer a clue to athletes’ symptoms, but that link still needs testing.

The groups were small, with four to five animals at each sampling time. All were male, and sex can affect both dopamine regulation and the body clock.

The chemical tests also had limits. They measured dopamine in tissue, without showing how much passed between brain cells or how quickly the brain broke it down.

Overtraining syndrome is a condition diagnosed in people. The mouse running program models some aspects of it, but it cannot fully reproduce the condition.

Serotonin has many jobs in the brain, including helping regulate sleep. This study suggests researchers should also examine its balance with dopamine when looking at the effects of excessive training.

Other research has linked brain signals to how long people can keep exercising, often over minutes. This work raises a different question: what happens to brain chemistry after weeks of training without enough recovery?

Study coordinator Adelino Sanchez Ramos da Silva said similar changes in humans could help explain links between excessive training, brain regulation and sleep problems.

His group is now working on an international project to identify which biological systems first shift away from exercise’s usual benefits.

The study is published in the journal Neuroscience.
