Mitchell targets Worlds success and Paralympic future

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

"There's something just freeing about getting on the ice in a sled."

That feeling has carried Shannon Mitchell to her second World Para Ice Hockey Women's World Championship, where she will represent Great Britain in the 2026 tournament from 24-29 November in Newport, Australia.

For the 26-year-old from Caerleon, this chapter is another milestone in a sporting journey that began long before she entered the frozen arena.

"I started out with taekwondo as throughout school I've always been super sporty, football, that kind of thing," Mitchell told BBC Sport Wales.

"When I was 14 or 15, I had an injury, which then led me to become disabled and eventually I lost my leg in 2019."

Shortly after, she was encouraged to try wheelchair basketball, and in what could be described as a twist of fate, one of her teammates recommended Para-ice hockey, a sport she immediately fell in love with.

"It would have been the 2020 season I started playing for the Cardiff Huskies, or maybe a little bit earlier, and I've played for them ever since," she said.

"It's a real adrenaline, physical, fast game.

"The rules for Para-ice hockey are exactly the same as stand-up, with the addition of a few extra rules, like teeing, which means you can't hit someone's feet first."

The Para-athlete has witnessed the rapid growth of the women's game first-hand.

Mitchell has been involved with the women's programme in the UK since its inception, representing her country at international competitions including the Women's World Challenge and the inaugural Women's World Championship in Slovakia in 2025.

Now she is one of 12 athletes selected by Great Britain for the second edition of the tournament, which pits them against reigning champions USA and hosts Australia in Group A.

Great Britain finished fourth of the five teams that competed in Slovakia, but Mitchell believes the squad has developed significantly over the past 12 months.

"The step up that we've made as a team between the last world champs and this world champs has been very intentional," she said.

"We're all getting excited about playing together and seeing the level of improvement in skill, in speed and in intent in terms of what we want to do."

For Mitchell, however, the tournament represents something far greater than results on the ice.

As women's Para-ice hockey continues to grow, athletes and governing bodies are striving to secure a dedicated competition at the Winter Paralympics, and Mitchell is determined to play her part.

"The aim within the sport is to get that women's category at the Paralympic level. The goal is 2030 in the Winter Paralympics," she said.

"At the moment the sport is mixed, which means that women can roster to traditionally men's teams.

"Great Britain are an example where there are a handful of women who roster onto the men's team."

Currently, women's Para-ice hockey struggles to meet the International Paralympic Committee (IPC) criteria for a separate medal event.

Eligibility requires a minimum of two world championships and sustained participation from at least eight nations, along with representation from at least three global regions.

Beyond promoting the sport, defender Mitchell is equally passionate about ensuring more people feel represented within it.

"I think representation is incredibly important, particularly in disability sports, because it's about more than just seeing someone like yourself on the ice," she said.

"It's about feeling that you belong, that there's a space for you, and that you don't have to leave parts of yourself behind to be part of a team.

"As a female and a member of the LGBTQ+ community, being able to show up as my whole self in Para-ice hockey means a lot to me.

"I'm so lucky to be part of a sport where the culture and community are so inclusive. When my wife is there supporting me, it doesn't feel any different to having any other team-mate's partner there.

Shannon Mitchell plays for Cardiff Huskies who were first established more than 30 years ago [BBC Sport]

Like many athletes, Mitchell's journey to achieve elite status involved overcoming several challenges beyond the rink.

Equipment is often bespoke and costly, while access to training can require lengthy travel commitments.

"As a person with a disability and also being an athlete you travel twice as far for training, you pay twice as much for equipment - whether that be sports wheelchairs or sleds - for the privilege of being able to play a sport," she said.

"A non-disabled person maybe wouldn't have as many barriers in place and I think it's just the harsh reality of disabled sports."

Earlier in her career, Mitchell regularly travelled four-hour round trips to attend training sessions while studying biomedical science at the University of Birmingham.

Now working as a full-time science teacher, she continues to balance her sport with professional responsibilities.

"It has definitely been a juggling act between work and sport and life but I wouldn't change it," Mitchell said.

"I feel very lucky to have found what I consider my people, like the GB women and even the guys, they are kind of the support that's been there over the past five years and I've met and made some friends for life that were at my wedding recently."

That sense of belonging is one of the reasons Mitchell remains committed to helping the sport thrive both on and off the ice.

For Mitchell, the goal is to help Great Britain challenge the world's best today while building for something even bigger for the future.
