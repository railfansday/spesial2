First Guidelines for Cancer Caregivers Address Key Information Void in Care

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

— Information and advice about how to be a caregiver and attend to caregiver needs

First-ever guidelines for cancer caregivers offer information and advice on topics ranging from medical management and emotional support to finances and legal issues.

Published by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the "NCCN Guidelines for Patients: Caregiving for People with Cancer" helps fill an information void created by the growing number of new cancer diagnoses and cancer survivors.

"When a loved one is diagnosed with cancer, naturally, most people want to show up for them, however they can," said Anthea A. Gray, PsyD, of the Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center in Columbus, in an NCCN announcement. "But pretty quickly, many realize it's not just about cancer. They're also juggling everything else in their lives. In moments when things feel scary or uncertain, or when everything starts to feel too much, having trusted, easy-to-find information matters a lot."

The timing of the guideline development and publication reflects the evolving landscape of oncology care.

"More and more people are living longer with cancer and many of them are older," Gray told MedPage Today. "There are more needs that come with that. As people age, comorbidities start to increase and so do the needs. Another factor is that more younger people are getting cancers than in the past, and people who are younger have different challenges. They might have young children or they might be earlier in their careers. The need for caregiving support has just become more evident."

According to the guideline, at least 70% of caregivers have jobs and other personal responsibilities, and 60% live separate from the patient. Each section of the guideline includes "Caregiver's Corner," which includes suggestions and advice about caregiver self-care. Additionally, the guideline has a dedicated chapter on caregiver well-being.

Individual chapters in the guideline cover definitions and explanations related to caregiving, basic tenets of caregiving (including medical and cancer-specific terminology), being part of a patient's cancer care team, and providing patient care and support (including non-medical issues). A separate chapter provides information on resources for caregiving and caregivers. The booklet also includes a glossary of key terms for caregivers.

Gray offered a couple of examples of issues that caregivers commonly encounter but might not have an adequate understanding of how to deal with.

"Feeling prepared for appointments is a big issue for many people," she said. "They have the best of intentions and want to help, but they don't know the questions to ask. The guidelines do an excellent job of articulating some of the questions for a patient's care team. Understanding the side effects of cancer treatment is another common issue, and the guidelines offer some particularly useful information."

The information on financial aspects of cancer care can help patients and caregivers avoid being blindsided by the cost.

"A person could work for 20 or 30 years and have a very prudent reserve for rainy days or health issues, and very quickly, they can see their savings just go out the door," she said. "It's heartbreaking because with all the advancements in healthcare and cancer treatment, people often have a long life to live [after cancer diagnosis and treatment] but now they have minimal or strained resources because of the treatment."

Caregivers and patients alike will find the information about survivorship useful and perhaps thought provoking.

"Survivorship is an understudied or misunderstood area, particularly as it pertains to caregivers," said Gray. "I think a lot of people have the expectation that once somebody finishes treatment, they ring the bell and think, 'OK, it's back to life as usual.' For many people, that's not how it goes. Maybe they go on to long-term hormonal treatment or have ongoing physical and emotional effects. That's really hard to understand, so this resource really helps coach them through that."

Though the guidelines address issues specific to cancer care, some of the topics and information could readily apply to caregivers for patients with other types of chronic illness.

"Issues related to fatigue or nausea, for example, could be helpful for caregivers for patients with other conditions," said Gray.

The guidelines can be downloaded at no cost from NCCN or from an associated mobile app.
