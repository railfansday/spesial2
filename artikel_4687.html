How Do People Think About Vices and Virtues?

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

As we head into the end of the year, people start thinking about New Year’s resolutions and begin to contemplate how they might improve themselves in the next year. Often, that involves thinking about the vices and virtues that characterize their lives. A vice is a negative moral trait like greed or arrogance. A virtue is a positive moral trait like generosity or loyalty. People may think about eliminating a vice or enhancing a virtue.

If you were to try to improve yourself by eliminating one of your vices or strengthening a virtue, what impact to you think that would have on other positive or negative traits you have? This question was studied by Benjamin Hardin, Sydney Scott, and Jessie Sun in a 2026 paper in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General.

They were curious about how people thought about vices and virtues in themselves and others. The central questions were straightforward: If someone tries to strengthen one of their virtues, what impact would that have on other virtues? One possibility is that people see virtues as connected, so that strengthening one strengthens them all. Trying to be more humble might be seen as improving other virtues like compassion or tolerance. Of course, it is also possible that people see the virtues as trading off, so that strengthening one actually weakens others (or perhaps has no impact).

The same question can be asked of vices: Do people think that when someone tries to combat one vice they decrease the strength of all of them? Is the effect specific? Perhaps people believe that combatting one vice strengthens others.

The studies also explored the relationship between vices and virtues. Do people believe that strengthening a virtue has any impact on that individual’s vices? Likewise, do people believe that someone pursuing a vice affects their virtues?

Across several studies, people were asked about either themselves or a hypothetical person who was trying to strengthen one of their virtues or started to display more of one of their vices and were asked what impact they thought that would have on a variety of other traits. The study made sure that people knew the definition of all of the vices and virtues before doing this task.

In general, people felt that strengthening a virtue would strengthen other virtues and also decrease the strength of other vices. Similarly, displaying more of one vice led people to think that the person would display more of all of their vices and less of their virtues. The effect for vices was stronger overall, meaning that displaying more of one vice tended to lead to larger judgments of the degree to which someone would display other vices.

One interesting difference between vices and virtues was that the vices were a little more differentiated. That is, strengthening one vice didn’t increase people’s judgments of the strength of all vices. Indeed, in some cases, vices worked against each other. For example, people believed that someone who began to display more cowardice would actually be less likely to be arrogant, cruel, or manipulative.

The researchers also added some questions to help them understand where the effects might be coming from. For example, one reason why becoming stronger in one virtue might increase people’s beliefs that other virtues would also strengthen is a halo effect in which people believed that increasing in one virtue made every quality of that person better. To address this question, the surveys also asked some questions about physical outcomes (like becoming more athletic or more attractive). Increasing the strength of a virtue did lead participants to think that these physical traits would also improve, but the effects were much smaller—suggesting that a halo effect could not explain the entire pattern of data.

One final result of interest had to do with people’s desire to change their own characteristics. People were asked about their desire to improve their virtues or address their vices. The more that people tended to think that improving any particular virtue would lead to positive changes in other virtues, the more they also wanted to improve their virtues. Likewise, the more that people thought a change in one vice would affect all vices, they were interested in decreasing the strength of their vices. That is, thinking that vices and virtues are dependent on each other led people to want to improve themselves, because of a belief that you could make yourself more virtuous overall by strengthening a specific virtue or weakening a vice.

This pattern of results is interesting, but there is much work that remains to be done. These studies focused on people’s beliefs about vices and virtues. It is a separate question whether strengthening a particular virtue or vice actually influences other virtues or vices. Research on other skills suggests that changes in behavior are often domain-specific, suggesting that the kind of general change in virtue or vice is probably unlikely. If so, it would also be interesting to understand why people believe that virtues and vices are interdependent.
