Skift Aviation Forum 2026: Five Decisions in the Room

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Jet fuel prices are still rising, and just about every variable affecting air travel is in flux — aircraft supply, air traffic volume, global partnerships, input costs. Decisions made today will determine who succeeds in aviation.

Passenger demand and airline revenues seem to be holding at a macro level. For now. Jet fuel prices continue to rise, aircraft remain scarce, and labor and airport costs keep rising. Meanwhile, global air traffic has been unpredictable throughout 2026, with wide regional differences.

Airlines at all levels of the market are shifting toward premium offerings to offset these headwinds and keep their revenues afloat. They’re redesigning cabins, loyalty programs, networks, ancillaries, and customer experiences. Skift Research describes the premium push as a normalizing trend: it’s now a baseline offering underpinning revenue expectations, rather than a stopgap strategy. [1]

The decisions industry leaders have to make today center on how to yield a competitive advantage within this industry-wide shift. The decisions underpinning every conversation at the 2026 Skift Aviation Forum will provide foundational evidence for these answers.

Fuel has become the industry’s biggest near-term margin test. Airlines entered the year with strong passenger revenue and high load factors, but the economics changed quickly as jet fuel prices climbed.

The impact is not uniform. Some airlines have more pricing power, stronger premium demand, better hedging positions, or networks that can absorb a shock more easily. Others compete in markets where travelers are more price-sensitive or where adding a fuel surcharge risks pushing demand to a rival.

The decision is how much of the fuel increase airlines can absorb before fares have to rise.

IATA expected the airline industry’s net profit to halve in 2026, a forecast made months before jet fuel prices peaked above $200 a barrel in October. [2] [3]

Airlines have spent the past several years reallocating space toward travelers who generate more revenue per seat. It’s not just major international carriers, either. Low-cost airlines have nearly doubled premium capacity since 2019.

Demand has kept up so far. More than 70% of travelers surveyed by Skift Research said they would consider paying for a premium cabin. [1]

The decision is how much further to push. Premium now affects cabin configuration, route planning, revenue management, airport strategy, and loyalty. Airlines need to know where demand still supports expansion and where the market is beginning to crowd.

U.S. domestic premium-seat capacity has grown 33% since 2020, roughly twice the 16% growth in economy seats. Premium revenues are significantly outpacing overall revenue growth and driving disproportionate value within airline revenue streams. [1]

The global traffic story has fractured by region. Middle East passenger traffic is expected to contract by 11.4% in 2026 amid airspace closures and reduced transfer demand, while Africa and Asia-Pacific will benefit from the shift. [4] Global load factors are reaching record levels because capacity remains constrained even as demand slows.

The decision is how to create the strongest return if capacity becomes more scarce. Slower global growth raises the cost of getting that call wrong.

Global air traffic growth is expected to slow to 2.1% in 2026 from 5.3% in 2025, while capacity increase is expected to be even lower at 1.6%. [4]

For many travelers, the ease of knowing they’ll get some miles and a few perks outweighs the headache of price comparison. AI changes this calculation, even for those who consider themselves somewhat loyal. Shopping tools can compare fares and products across airlines immediately. A traveler carrying points or status can still be presented with a better-priced or better-matched option somewhere else.

The decision for airlines is what loyalty has to deliver beyond points. Service, recognition, card economics, access, flexibility, and personalization can all strengthen the relationship. To avoid commoditization, airlines need to prove to travelers at every level of their programs that they can offer differentiated value.

The shift to premium is buoying revenue numbers, but AI platforms may not-so-quietly siphon off loyalty value from otherwise brand-agnostic travelers.

The industry has to make long-term commitments while near-term conditions remain volatile. Those investments have to make some assumptions about how the industry will operate years from now.

The decision is which bets deserve capital allocation. Growth alone is no longer enough justification. Airlines, airports, and manufacturers need a clear view of what each investment changes — capacity, economics, reliability, service, or competitive position.

Global airline passenger revenue is expected to approach $883 billion in 2027, but annual growth is projected to slow to about 3%. [5]

The airline industry’s next set of decisions centers on where to commit.

Airlines need to decide how much further to push premium, where capacity belongs, which rising costs customers can absorb, what keeps loyalty valuable, and which long-term investments deserve capital now.

Each decision comes back to the same constraint: airlines cannot pursue every opportunity at once. Skift Aviation Forum is focused on helping industry leaders identify where the economics still justify the strategy.
