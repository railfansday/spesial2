Reverse-scoring a personality quiz so agree-with-everything does not break it

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Most personality quizzes end the same way: they pick your highest trait and hand you a label. That works, but it throws away the interesting part — how close your top two were, and whether any of the answers were actually yours or just a response pattern.

I built a seven-trait quiz (seven deadly sins, played for laughs) and had to solve a few scoring problems that turned out to be more interesting than the UI.

21 statements, three per trait, each rated on a 7-point scale from disagree to agree. Three per trait is a compromise: fewer and one sloppy answer swings the result; more and the thing stops being fun to finish. 21 items takes about three minutes.

Every trait ends up with a score from 0 to 100, and all seven are drawn on a radar chart so you can see the shape, not just the peak.

The first version had a bug that is not really a bug: people who agreed with everything scored high on all seven traits. The radar chart came out as a near-perfect circle, which looked broken but was an honest reflection of the input.

This is acquiescence bias — the tendency to agree with statements regardless of content. You can't fix it by telling people "answer honestly". You fix it in the instrument.

The fix: one of the three statements per trait is reverse-worded. If the trait is sloth, two statements describe avoiding effort and one describes the opposite. Agreement with the third one pulls the score down.

The radar chart is plain SVG — a polygon grid, one axis per trait, and a for the result shape. No chart library: at seven points the maths is a loop over angles, and a library would have cost more than it saved.

The one design decision that mattered: label placement. Seven labels around a circle collide on narrow screens. Rotating the label text with the axis looked tidy on desktop and unreadable on a phone, so the labels stay horizontal and the chart shrinks instead.

The most-used feature is not the result — it's sending the result to a friend.

The compare link carries the score vector in the URL fragment, not the query string:

A fragment means the server never sees it, it is not logged, and it does not end up in a referrer header. When a friend opens the link and finishes the test, the two vectors are rendered side by side with an overlap percentage — the mean absolute difference converted to a similarity score.

It is crude, and it works better than any "share your result" button I tried, because it gives the second person something to do rather than something to look at.

The result page is dressed as a mock classified file: a primary deviation, a stamp for the top score band, a short write-up, a tongue-in-cheek fix. That framing is doing real work — a bare bar chart reads as a survey, while a case file reads as a joke about yourself, which is what people are actually there for.
