Valve recently admitted that it has a problem with AI slop games and clones flooding Steam—it doesn't know what to do about it just yet, but it at least acknowledges that it's an issue. Now we have Roblox, speaking from the other side of that coin in its Q3 2026 shareholder letter, in which it bragged that the July release of Build, its AI-powered "prompt-to-game creation tool," users have published more than 70,000 games.

"During the last three months, we have witnessed an inflection point in AI adoption and content generation in the gaming industry overall, and on Roblox in particular," the letter states. "AI-enabled game creation, automation in code generation and AI supported asset creation are leading to an explosion of content. The time it takes to go from an idea to a playable game is compressing dramatically, and so is the time it takes to adapt a game for new devices."

The "explosion of content" remark is what jumps out at me first and foremost. Is that really a good thing—content, in explosive quantities? That is essentially what Valve is facing on Steam that's widely considered a problem—an ever-growing mountain of content—yet in the world of Roblox, it's a feature, not a bug. And Roblox is not looking to stem the tide.

"We have been embracing AI to accelerate creation since the first release of Roblox Studio Assistant in December 2023," the letter continues. "Over the past month, more than 80% of the top 100 games on Roblox (ranked by hours engaged) used AI tools to accelerate creation, and the number of daily asset uploads to the Roblox cloud tripled month-over-month.

"Since Q2, time spent using AI tools (including Build, Studio Assistant, and external AI via MCP) tripled among our top creators, and those using our MCP server daily grew nearly 5x. In Q3, Break and Steal an Egg and Huss Valley were both created almost entirely with AI and both broke into the Roblox top 20 (ranked by concurrent players). Build is our new prompt-to-game creation tool. Since its alpha launch in New Zealand, Singapore, Serbia and Thailand, more than 500,000 users have used Build—and have published over 70,000 games."

As content becomes abundant, attention and connection become scarce. What sets our distribution apart is that it runs on social connections.

Take a moment to think about that: Build launched in July, five months ago, which means we're looking at a rough average of 14,000 games per month. And that's with Build still not fully launched yet, so only a fairly small portion of the Roblox userbase has access to it. I can't help but wonder, with a certain sense of alarm, what will happen when the doors are opened to more populous parts of the world.

Roblox, however, says it is "uniquely positioned to capture this moment," which is seeing "traditional barriers to software and content creation" giving way to a focus on "distribution, audience aggregation, monetization, discovery, and safety."

To further capitalize on that, Roblox has kicked off two new initiatives: Roblox Everywhere, revealed in September as a way to make Roblox games playable outside of the Roblox platform, and the Universal Player, which is basically the reverse of that: "AI is used to seamlessly import any existing game into the Roblox cloud platform while transforming it to run multiplayer, on any device (at the upper edge of the device's performance capabilities), with instant join, instant UGC updates, local 3D, and access to Roblox services for discovery, safety, communication, and monetization."

That's interesting: Details are scant but the idea seems to be to make Roblox a platform for all game developers, akin to Steam, GOG, or the PlayStation Store, with minimal fuss or muss: Just make your game, then zippity-pow it through the AI machine, and suddenly you've got a full-on Roblox game and access to a massive audience that previously would've been much more of a headache to reach. And Roblox gets basically the same: Its goal "is to capture 10% of the global gaming market and an even greater share in the US."

"As content becomes abundant, attention and connection become scarce," the letter says, extolling the virtues of Roblox's platform. "What sets our distribution apart is that it runs on social connections: people come to Roblox to play with their friends, so every new game can reach players through an existing social graph, through friends inviting friends, and through social influencers, which we believe enables great content to find its ideal audience faster than on any other platform."

Maybe Roblox is right. Hell, maybe EA chief Andrew Wilson was right—maybe the future really is all about billions of people cranking out content while corporations fight over where to put it all. It's hard to argue that Roblox hasn't put up serious shareholder value so far, after all, and while Steam doesn't have shareholders it's still making bank, AI slop problem or no. Still, I can't say that making speed and quantity the key indicators of success really represents the kind of future for videogaming that I'd like.
