First Nvidia, Now AMD: A Short Seller Just Put Its China Business Under the Microscope

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A short report claims AMD’s firmware keeps turning up in updates from a blacklisted Chinese server maker, and the stock is falling.

By Johnny Rice – Nov 19, 2026 at 2:55PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Advanced Micro Devices ›

Advanced Micro Devices (AMD -9.25%) shares were down about 9% as of 1:48 p.m. ET on Nov. 19 after Hunterbrook Media published an investigation alleging that AMD's software keeps turning up in servers made by a blacklisted Chinese company.

The short report lands as the market is still reeling from Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia CEO in the wake of reports he was romantically involved with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, and on the same day new U.S. rules tightening advanced chip exports take effect. So, what should investors take from this?

While the allegations are narrower than some of the headlines make it seem, I think they're another sign that the AI trade has more cracks than bulls would like to admit.

The report centers on a Chinese server maker named IEIT (SZSE: 000977). Hunterbrook says the company was added in March 2025 to the Commerce Department's Entity List -- the list of foreign companies that U.S. firms cannot export to without a special license -- and that since then, IEIT has built at least six newer versions of AMD's firmware -- the base layer software that allows chips to run inside a server -- into its own updates. The report also alleges that three separate server companies that aren't on the Entity List post firmware that is more than 99% identical to IEIT's.

To be clear, Hunterbrook says it doesn't know how IEIT got the code or if anyone held a license and that its records don't directly establish any export violation, and AMD has firmly denied any wrongdoing, telling Bloomberg that the report misrepresents its business and that "AMD complies with applicable US export control laws and regulations."

Software might sound minor next to shipping actual chips, but Hunterbrook argues that updates count as exports. The report points to a settlement last month in which Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) counted each of seven software updates sent after a listing as a violation. And Washington is obviously watching this stuff very closely at the moment.

By Hunterbrook's count, AMD billed $7.75 billion to customers in China and Hong Kong in fiscal 2025 -- about 22% of its $34.6 billion in revenue -- and another $4.28 billion to Singapore, one of five countries where the BIS rule that took effect today requires licenses for advanced chips. Those numbers are pulled directly from AMD's own filings, so they're no secret.

However, Hunterbrook seems to believe some material portion of this could be at risk, and that perhaps the software updates, while potentially violations in and of themselves, suggest AMD chips were still flowing to IEIT even after it was added to Commerce's blacklist.

To be very clear, these are allegations from the media wing of a fund that is short the stock. It stands to gain financially from AMD shares falling. That's a real conflict of interest.

A short report should always be taken with a heavy grain of salt. That said, they shouldn't be dismissed either. A short-seller has an incentive for their research to be taken seriously, and therefore, an incentive to tell the truth.
