The automaker must now obtain express consent before collecting or sharing driver data.

GM has settled a multistate suit over selling customer data without consent.

In 2024, The New York Times published a damning investigation into the program, revealing that, in some cases, data on drivers’ habits could be used by insurance companies to set rates. Some drivers saw significant premium increases after GM sold their driving history, and one owner was told a 22% premium increase resulted from driving information supplied by a data broker, according to a statement from the New York attorney general’s office.

The data collection was described as “spying,” and GM quickly ended the program that year. In 2025, GM settled with the Federal Trade Commission over Smart Driver and OnStar location data, agreeing to a five-year ban on selling driver data.

Attorneys general in many of the states involved in the $220 million settlement posted announcements about it, including Anthony G. Brown of Maryland.

The settlement requires GM to obtain affirmative express consent from consumers before collecting or disclosing consumer driving and location data.

“Safer, more helpful and personalized experiences are enabled by connected vehicle data, and GM is committed to being clear about what information is used, how it’s protected, and the choices customers have about what they share,” the spokesperson said.

Automobiles have evolved into computers on wheels over the last few decades, collecting vast amounts of data on drivers, including contacts and song lists, location data and diagnostic information. For GM, the issue was less about collecting data than about breaking its promise to customers that it wouldn’t sell it.

There may be more action to come on illegal or unethical data-collection practices in the auto industry.

“GM was the biggest case and, in some ways, presented the most egregious set of facts. But collecting and selling driving data is an industry norm, not an aberration,” said Peter Cassat, technology partner at CM Law and a former general counsel for Cox Automotive and New Country Motor Group.

“The difference now is that regulators have a template, and every state attorney general has seen a $220 million settlement, so I think we can expect more enforcement actions and settlements,” Cassat said.

“Vehicle providers should ask for driver consent feature by feature in plain English,” he said, not simply prompt buyers to check a box.

Beyond the touchscreen on your dashboard, modern vehicles run background systems that transmit data straight to the manufacturer, and drivers have little to no control over what gets collected or who buys it.

“Unfortunately, there isn’t a lot consumers can do,” he said. “Opt out wherever possible and don’t agree to unnecessary data sharing. Review your own Customer Disclosure Report to see whether driving data appears. And if your car has a modem that can be disabled, consider disabling it.”
