10 common prenup mistakes that give courts grounds to refuse enforcement

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

What makes a prenup invalid often comes down to avoidable errors, and these 10 common mistakes, from hidden assets to last-minute signatures, explain why courts set some agreements aside

A prenuptial agreement is a contract, but courts do not treat it like an ordinary one. Two people about to marry are not bargaining at arm's length. One may depend on the other financially. One may have a lawyer while the other does not. The wedding date itself can create pressure to sign. Judges reviewing a challenged prenup look closely at how the deal was made, not only at what it says.

Prenups in the U.S. are governed by state law, and there is no single national rule. Many states built their statutes on one of two model laws. The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act was drafted in 1983. The Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act followed in 2012. Both set out baseline requirements for a valid agreement. State rules still differ, and a clause that holds up in one state can fail in another.

Because of that variation, the question of what makes a prenup invalid has no single answer. The same question matters to a spouse weighing a challenge to a prenuptial agreement when a marriage ends. Courts do not set agreements aside at random. The grounds for challenge tend to fall into two groups.

The first group concerns process. Was the premarital agreement put in writing and signed by both spouses? Did each spouse disclose assets and debts? Was there enough time to review it without pressure? Did each side have independent legal advice? Could both people read and understand the document?

The second group concerns substance. Some subjects sit outside what a prenup can control, such as child support and custody. Some terms are so one-sided that a court can refuse to enforce them. Spousal support waivers can run into limits when they would leave a spouse in need of public assistance. Personal lifestyle clauses, such as rules about housework or visits from in-laws, are generally not enforced. State formalities and the validity of the marriage itself matter as well.

A prenup that is unenforceable on one of these grounds is not always thrown out in full. In some cases a court strikes a single clause and keeps the rest. The outcome depends on the problem and on the state.

The 10 mistakes below are common grounds for challenge, not a complete list. Each one explains a separate way an agreement can fail.

A spoken promise about property generally cannot serve as a prenup. Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, a premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties. An oral deal made before the wedding is generally unenforceable.

That act is a model law, not a binding statute on its own. It carries legal force only where a state has adopted it, and adopting states often adjust the details.

The writing requirement has deep roots. The English Statute of Frauds of 1677 listed agreements made in consideration of marriage among the contracts that needed a signed writing. State statutes of frauds in the U.S. commonly include the same category.

The requirement shuts down a familiar kind of dispute. One spouse recalls a conversation before the wedding. In it, the other agreed that a business, a house or an inheritance would stay separate. Without a signed document, that memory generally cannot override the state's default rules for dividing property at divorce.

Both signatures matter. A draft that only one partner signed does not meet the model act's standard. Neither does a final version that one partner reviewed but never signed.

The 2012 successor, the Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act, keeps the same core idea. It requires the agreement to be in a record and signed by both parties. Under that act, a record can be electronic as well as on paper.

The 1983 act also settles two technical points. The agreement takes effect on marriage, not on the date it is signed. A couple who sign a prenup and then call off the wedding have no agreement in force.

The second point concerns consideration. In ordinary contract law, each side usually has to give something of value for a promise to be binding. The model act says no separate consideration is required for a premarital agreement.

The writing rule follows the agreement after the wedding. Under the 1983 act, a couple can amend or revoke a prenup after marriage only through a written agreement signed by both spouses. An informal understanding to drop or change terms generally does not alter the signed document.

Concealing property or debts from a future spouse is a common basis for challenging a prenup. A spouse who signed without a fair view of the other's finances may be able to ask a court to refuse enforcement.

The reasoning follows from what a prenup does. Each person agrees to give up financial rights they might otherwise claim if the marriage ends. That trade only holds up if the person giving something up knows what is on the table.

The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, a 1983 model law that many states have drawn on, sets out a specific test. Under that act, missing disclosure is not enough on its own. The challenging spouse must also show that the agreement was unconscionable when it was signed.

The disclosure half of the test has three parts, and all of them must be met. The challenging spouse was not given fair and reasonable disclosure of the other's property and finances. That spouse did not waive disclosure in writing. That spouse also had no adequate knowledge of the other's finances from any other source.

Each part closes off a route for the challenger. A spouse who received a full accounting has little basis to claim being kept in the dark. A spouse who signed a written waiver gave up the right to that information on paper. A spouse who already understood the other's finances cannot easily argue that missing paperwork changed anything.

Debts belong in the picture as well as assets. An undisclosed loan or tax liability leaves a future spouse with an incomplete view, just as a hidden bank account or business interest does.

A written financial statement attached to the agreement creates a record of what each spouse was shown before signing. If a challenge comes years later, that record can help answer the question of what the challenging spouse knew at the time.

Courts in states that drew on the model act do not all apply its disclosure test in the same way.

A prenup must be signed voluntarily, and a spouse who proves duress or coercion can have it set aside. A signature collected days before the wedding is not automatically invalid, though. Courts weigh the timing alongside everything else that happened.

Voluntariness is one of the basic tests for any premarital agreement. A contract signed under threat or heavy pressure does not reflect a real meeting of the minds.

Judges look at the full circumstances rather than a single date on the calendar. One factor is how many days passed between the first draft and the final signature. Another is whether earlier drafts circulated, which would show the terms were discussed over time. A third is whether the wedding invitations had gone out and travel had been booked when the document first appeared.

Those details shape the picture a court sees. A spouse who saw the terms weeks in advance and traded several versions with the other side looks like a willing participant. A spouse handed a finished agreement with guests already booked faces a harder choice. Signing may feel like the only way to avoid canceling the wedding. That is the kind of pressure a challenge tends to focus on.

California addresses the timing question in statute. Family Code section 1615 presumes a premarital agreement was not signed voluntarily unless certain conditions were met. One condition is that the final agreement was presented at least seven calendar days before signing. Another involves independent legal counsel. When those conditions are missing, the spouse defending the agreement starts at a disadvantage.

Section 1615 measures from the final agreement. An earlier draft does not satisfy the rule on its own if the terms later change. Outside a statute like California's, timing stays one factor among several rather than a bright line.

Sharing drafts before invitations go out leaves a record that the terms were negotiated. Noting when each version changed hands adds to that record.

The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act does not strictly require each spouse to hire a lawyer. Courts still weigh the absence of independent counsel when deciding whether a spouse agreed to a prenup voluntarily and with a clear sense of what it meant.

Independent counsel means a lawyer who represents only one spouse. That lawyer advises that person alone on what the agreement gives up and what it protects. A spouse who signed after that kind of advice has a harder time later arguing that the terms came as a surprise.

The factor carries more weight in some situations than in others. One is a gap in sophistication between the spouses. A spouse who owns a business, manages investments or has negotiated contracts before may follow the terms easily. A partner with no financial or legal background may not grasp what a waiver of property or support rights means in practice.

Another is a single lawyer handling the agreement for both parties. The two spouses have opposing interests in a premarital agreement. Terms that protect one spouse's assets often limit the other spouse's claims. A lawyer drafting on behalf of one side cannot also give the other side independent advice on whether to accept those terms.

California sets a firmer rule for one type of term. Under California Family Code section 1612, a waiver of spousal support is unenforceable if the spouse giving up support did not have independent legal counsel at signing.

That California rule targets spousal support, not the whole agreement. The property terms in the same document are judged under the state's other requirements. A California prenup can therefore keep its property provisions while losing a support waiver signed without a lawyer.

Outside rules like California's, the picture depends on each state's law. Where no specific counsel requirement exists, the lack of a lawyer is one piece of evidence among several. A court considers it alongside the other circumstances surrounding the signing.

A prenup signed by someone who could not read its language, and who got no translation or explanation, can be challenged for lack of knowing consent. A signature alone does not prove the signer understood the terms.

This ground concerns comprehension of the document itself. A disclosure challenge asks what one spouse knew about the other's money. A spouse can have a full picture of the other's finances and still not grasp what the agreement does with them.

Ordinary contract law starts from a firm default. A person who signs a contract is generally bound by it, whether or not they read it. Premarital agreements get closer review. They can reshape property rights and support if the marriage ends, and courts expect consent to those terms to be informed.

The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act of 1983 does not list comprehension as its own ground. A challenge based on a language barrier is usually framed as a question of whether the spouse signed voluntarily. Under the 1983 act, the spouse resisting enforcement carries the burden of proving the grounds for setting the agreement aside.

The facts that matter tend to be practical. Was the agreement written in a language the signer reads? Was a translation provided? Did anyone walk the signer through the terms before signing? When the answer to all three is no, a court can find the signer never knowingly agreed.

This ground sits close to voluntariness and disclosure, and a challenge may raise them together. A spouse who could not read the agreement may also have signed in a hurry, or without knowing the other's finances. Courts weigh the whole situation rather than a single fact.

A translation or a full explanation of the terms closes this gap. A spouse who does not read the drafting language fluently can ask for a version they can read before signing. A written record of who translated or explained the agreement, and when, gives a court something concrete to review if the prenup is later challenged.

A prenup cannot lock in child support or custody ahead of time. Courts keep the final say on both and decide them based on the child's best interests. A clause that waives or caps child support does not bind the judge.

The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act addresses this directly. Under that model law, a premarital agreement cannot adversely affect a child's right to support.

The reasoning turns on who holds the right. Child support belongs to the child, not to either parent. Two adults planning a marriage cannot bargain away money owed to someone who is not a party to the contract. In many cases, that child has not yet been born when the agreement is signed.

Custody follows similar logic. A judge decides where a child lives and how parenting time is divided based on the family's circumstances at the time of the separation. Those circumstances cannot be known when a couple signs before the wedding. A clause promising sole custody to one parent, or fixing a visitation schedule years in advance, does not override the best-interests standard.

Some agreements try indirect routes. A provision might cap support at a set monthly amount. Another might say one parent will cover all of a child's costs in exchange for a larger share of marital property. A court can disregard the child support portion of that bargain and set support under the state's own guidelines.

A child support clause does not usually sink the whole agreement. Courts typically treat the offending provision as severable. The judge strikes the clause that touches the child's support or custody and enforces the remaining terms. Property division, the treatment of premarital assets and other financial provisions can stand on their own. Many prenups include a severability clause that states this intent in writing. That clause tells a court the spouses wanted the rest of the contract to survive if one part fails.

The effect cuts both ways for a divorcing spouse. A parent who signed a prenup containing a child support waiver is not held to that waiver. That same parent generally cannot point to the invalid clause as a reason to escape the agreement's property terms. The court keeps control of the child's support and custody, while the financial arrangements between the spouses can remain in force.

A prenup whose terms are grossly unfair to one spouse can be refused enforcement as unconscionable. Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act of 1983, though, harsh terms alone are not enough to defeat the agreement.

Unconscionability is a long-standing contract law doctrine. It lets a court decline to enforce a bargain so unfair to one side that holding the parties to it would be unjust. In a prenup, the challenge might target an agreement in which one spouse gives up nearly every claim to property built during the marriage.

The 1983 model act ties this ground to a specific moment. The court asks whether the agreement was unconscionable when it was executed. It does not ask whether the terms look unfair years later at divorce.

Unfairness at signing is not enough by itself under the model act. It has to be paired with a disclosure failure, meaning the challenging spouse got no fair disclosure, signed no written waiver and had no adequate knowledge of the other's finances.

The practical result is that a lopsided deal can hold up under the model act. A spouse who knew the full financial picture and accepted unequal terms anyway is treated as having made an informed choice. The same is true of a spouse who signed a written waiver of disclosure. The spouse resisting enforcement carries the burden of proving these grounds.

Under the model act, the court decides whether a premarital agreement is unconscionable as a matter of law.

Timing is where approaches diverge. Some states, and the Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act drafted in 2012, also let a court consider hardship at the time of enforcement. The 2012 act describes this as substantial hardship caused by a material change in circumstances arising after signing. It also lets a court refuse to enforce a single term, judged in the context of the agreement as a whole.

Which standard applies depends on the state whose law governs the agreement. State rules on unconscionability differ. The approach in force in that state shapes how much scrutiny one-sided terms receive.

A prenup can waive or limit spousal support, but that waiver has a floor. Under the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act of 1983, a court may order support if the waiver would leave a spouse eligible for public assistance.

Spousal support goes by different names depending on the state. Alimony and maintenance are common alternatives. Whatever the label, it means payments one spouse makes to the other after a separation or divorce.

The 1983 model act lists the modification or elimination of spousal support among the subjects a premarital agreement may cover. A support waiver is not invalid on its face. Couples are generally free to agree that neither will pay the other, or to set a cap or a time limit.

The limit is tied to a specific moment. The model act asks whether reducing or eliminating support makes a spouse eligible for a public assistance program at the time of separation or divorce. If it does, the court may require the other spouse to provide support despite the agreement's terms.

The remedy is narrow. A court can order only the support needed to keep the spouse from qualifying for public assistance. It does not rewrite the waiver. It also does not award the amount a spouse might have received with no prenup at all.

The power is discretionary. The act says a court may order support in this situation, not that it must.

The effect is that a private contract between two spouses cannot shift one spouse's support onto public programs. The other spouse, who agreed to the waiver, remains responsible up to that minimum.

This safeguard is separate from a claim that the whole agreement was unconscionable. A spouse does not need to prove the prenup was unfair when it was signed. The trigger is the financial outcome when the marriage ends.

The rule also sits apart from the property terms. An order for support under this provision concerns support only. It does not by itself undo the division of assets the couple agreed to.

State law can go further than the model act. Some states review spousal support terms at divorce under a fairness test, separately from the property provisions. The standard that applies depends on which state's law controls the agreement.

Courts generally refuse to enforce prenup terms that govern personal behavior rather than money. A clause that assigns housework, limits how often in-laws may visit or sets a spouse's weight is unlikely to bind either party in court.

A premarital agreement is built to settle financial questions. The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act lists the subjects a couple may address. Those include each spouse's rights in property, the power to buy, sell or use it, and how it is divided on separation, divorce or death.

The same list reaches the modification or elimination of spousal support. It also covers making wills and trusts, ownership of life insurance death benefits, and which state's law governs the agreement.

That act also leaves some room for personal rights and obligations. That room has a limit. Such terms cannot violate public policy or a statute imposing a criminal penalty. Clauses about daily household life tend to fall outside what a judge will back with a court order.

The practical obstacle is enforcement. A clause requiring one spouse to cook dinner five nights a week offers no property to transfer and no clear sum to award. A cap on holiday visits from a mother-in-law raises the same problem. So does a requirement about weight or appearance.

The Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act goes further and names several terms that cannot be enforced. One is a term that limits the remedies available to a victim of domestic violence. Another is a term that penalizes a spouse for filing for divorce or legal separation. A third is a term that changes the grounds on which a court may grant a divorce or separation.

A lifestyle clause rarely sinks the whole prenup. Judges usually sever the unenforceable term and enforce the remaining financial provisions. Couples can still write down an understanding about chores or family visits. It works as a private arrangement between spouses, not as a contract a court will enforce.

A prenup has to meet the requirements of the state whose law governs it. It also depends on a valid marriage. If the marriage is void, the agreement generally never takes effect.

No single national statute in the U.S. sets out how a premarital agreement must be made or when a court will enforce it. Each state writes its own rules, and each state's courts apply them.

Model laws give many states a common starting point, but they do not make the rules uniform. A state that bases its statute on a uniform act can change the text when it enacts it. Its judges also interpret that text on their own. Two states working from the same model can still reach different results on the same facts.

The practical result is that a prenup can satisfy one state's requirements and fall short of another's. An agreement drafted to one state's standards may be tested under a different set of rules later.

California shows how specific these rules can get. Its Family Code sets a seven-calendar-day window for presenting the final agreement before signing. It also restricts spousal support waivers signed without independent counsel. Neither requirement appears in that form in the 1983 model act.

Moving raises the issue directly. A couple may sign a prenup in one state and later settle in another. The agreement may then be judged under rules nobody considered when it was drafted. Couples who relocate should have the agreement reviewed against the law of their new home state.

The second issue sits underneath every other term. A premarital agreement is made in contemplation of a marriage. It takes effect on the wedding, not on signing. Until then, its terms have nothing to attach to.

That makes the agreement conditional on a valid marriage. A void marriage is treated in law as though it never existed. If the marriage is void, the prenup generally does not take effect either. Property division terms, support waivers and every other provision depend on that condition being met.

A void marriage differs from a divorce. In a divorce, the marriage was valid and the prenup governs how it ends. When the marriage was never valid, the agreement built on it usually falls away with it.
