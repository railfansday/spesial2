SpaceX Has 33 Analysts Covering It and Just 1 “Strong Sell.” Here’s What That Analyst Sees That the Other 32 Don’t.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Wall Street remains upbeat about SpaceX's future.

SpaceX (SPCX -1.26%) stock has been a wild ride for investors since going public earlier this summer.

The space company initially set an initial public offering (IPO) price of $135 per share. Within a few days, however, shares surged above $200.

Since those highs, shares have slumped lower and now hover around $140 -- just above its initial public offering (IPO) price.

Surprisingly, Wall Street has mostly been unperturbed. The consensus price target is currently around $246, implying 76% upside potential. Of the 33 analysts that actively cover the stock, 27 maintain "buy" ratings, five consider the stock a "hold," and just one recommends that investors sell the company.

What does that one bearish analyst understand that the others don't?

Keith Snyder of CFRA Research initiated coverage on SpaceX stock in June, just before the company went public. His price target of $115 resulted in a "sell" rating, considering SpaceX stock began with a $135 per-share valuation.

Snyder has yet to shift on his position, maintaining his rating and price target throughout SpaceX's share price fluctuations. Many other analysts have upped or trimmed their price targets as SpaceX shares have swooned. But not Snyder.

Why is Snyder so bearish? Fortunately, investors can access his research directly to understand his exact position.

Snyder called SpaceX's debut "the most complex, high-stakes IPO in a generation." He was impressed with SpaceX's technology and vision, but he just had a tough time justifying the company's claimed total addressable market -- a major assumption underpinning SpaceX's steep valuation.

Snyder correctly notes that for SpaceX's valuation to make sense, it must execute on its artificial intelligence (AI) roadmap. After all, AI accounts for $26.5 trillion of SpaceX's total claimed $28.5 trillion addressable market.

But capturing that value requires success in other segments, including space-enabled solutions and Starlink. Starlink is needed to provide inbound cash flow to support AI infrastructure construction. Space-enabled solutions -- which mostly comprises rocket launches -- is required to successfully commercialize Starship, which can dramatically lower launch costs and support the deployment of space-based data centers.

To be clear, Snyder is impressed with SpaceX as a business. And so am I. I believe the company's prospects are bright. But that doesn't make it a wise investment at any price: "The company has already demonstrated disruptive innovation in launch economics through reusable rockets and has established a dominant position in global mass-to-orbit capacity," Snyder correctly points out.

Snyder's concerns are the same as my own. SpaceX is very much a long-term story. And many things must go right for its valuation to be justified. A challenge as simple as restricted access to capital -- which a market downturn could quickly present -- could stretch the company's growth journey out several years, weakening today's investment case.

As Snyder has shown, it is not a very popular stance, but I'm sticking on the sidelines when it comes to SpaceX stock. The story is simply far too complex, reliant on the execution of several high-risk initiatives, to justify an investment at today's valuation.
