Pay to Play: 2026 Audi A6 Tested

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

More performance, refinement, and tech come to Audi’s redesigned luxury sedan, but its coolest features are locked behind a sizable paywall.

10/29/26 UPDATE: This review has been updated with instrumented test results.

Push the redesigned 2026 Audi A6 into a corner, and it will rotate around the apex like a well-tuned sports sedan, its revised torque-vectoring rear differential and newly available rear-axle steering helping to redirect the car’s momentum. Grippy summer tires claw at the pavement, and the chassis feels hunkered down on a new sport-tuned suspension. There’s a caveat to its enhanced athleticism: You need to spring for the top Prestige trim level to unlock the A6’s optional Sport Plus package and its driver-focused hardware—as well as some other desirable features. And that makes the new A6 a pricey proposition.

The new gas-burning A6 is built on Audi’s latest Premium Platform Combustion architecture, which also underpins the A5 models, as well as the Q5 and SQ5 SUVs. Prices start at a reasonable $65,395 for the base Premium trim, with all versions gaining more standard luxuries and safety tech, plus additional sound-deadening measures. Think better door seals, revisions to the seven-speed dual-clutch automatic transmission, and foam inserts in the tires.

The previous generation’s four-cylinder variants are no more, but output from the familiar turbocharged 3.0-liter V-6 has swelled by 27 horsepower and 37 pound-feet of torque, now to 362 and 406, respectively. Combined with standard all-wheel drive and launch control, that was enough to get our luxury-focused test car to 60 mph in a solid 4.3 seconds and through the quarter-mile in 12.9 seconds at 108 mph. We also tested a Sport Plus variant, which needed a tenth of a second or two longer to reach those same marks—a difference that likely comes down to production variances and probably a little more rotating mass from the sportier car’s larger wheels. Either way, both vehicles are quicker to 60 mph than the last A6 we tested (4.7 seconds), yet still trail the outgoing S6 model (4.0 seconds). What’s more, our more luxurious example averaged a big 38 mpg on our 75-mph highway fuel-economy test, beating its EPA estimate by 9 mpg.

Attractively reserved and closely resembling the outgoing car, the new A6’s exterior design is unmistakably Audi, with its latest revisions accentuating the sculpting of its bumpers and flanks. Optional black wheels and accents diminish some of the body’s newfound definition, but it maintains a strong presence in person, especially from the rear. Save for a 2.4-inch length increase and a half-inch narrower width, its exterior dimensions carry over.

Slip behind the wheel, and the A6’s dash now frames Audi’s latest “digital stage” interface: a vibrant 11.9-inch driver display and 14.5-inch touchscreen, complete with intuitive menus and standard wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto. Audi has yet to get the memo about piano-black trim being a pain to keep clean and looking less upscale than intended. But there’s enough ambient lighting, including directional elements below the windshield, to enhance the ambiance. An available Bang & Olufsen 3D sound system sports up to 20 speakers, including two in each of the front-seat headrests. And a new panoramic glass roof with six individually dimmable segments can flood the cabin with natural light while providing noticeably more headroom up front. An adult-friendly back seat remains, and the trunk will swallow a healthy 14 cubic feet of stuff.

However, you’ll need to step up to the $69,295 Premium Plus trim, if not the $73,495 Prestige, to get the headlining goodies—a decision Audi says was made primarily to reduce model complexity. Want the new head-up display, which, in a brand first, can now be used to adjust vehicle and infotainment settings? Pony up for at least the Premium Plus. The same goes for four-zone automatic climate control (three-zone is standard), the 10.9-inch passenger display, and the S Line Black Optic package with its more aggressive bumpers. Want soft-close doors or the excellent massaging sport seats that are part of both the Sport Plus bundle and the indulgent $9400 Audi Exclusive Design package with its tan and navy two-tone upholstery? Now you’re looking at the Prestige. Similarly, only the A6’s top two trims get animated LED headlights with configurable DRL signatures, but the similarly adjustable OLED taillights are a Prestige exclusive.

Audi (unsurprisingly) limited our drive time to Prestige models, which come standard with adaptive dampers and height-adjustable air springs that reduce the ride height in Normal mode over the Premium and Premium Plus trims by 0.8 inch. From there, the air springs can lower the car up to 0.4 inch in Dynamic mode or raise it 0.8 inch at speeds up to 53 mph—via voice commands, no less (Premium and Premium Plus trims get coil springs with passive dampers). The third setup is the aforementioned Sport suspension, part of the Prestige-exclusive $5850 Sport Plus package that effectively transforms the A6 into a quasi-S6, a model that is now effectively dead. Along with firmer coils and passive dampers that together also drop the car 0.8 inch over stock, this package also brings black-painted 21-inch wheels (19s are standard, 20s are available), summer tires, the clever rear differential, rear-wheel steering with up to five degrees of swivel, the sportier S Line exterior getup, red-painted brake calipers, and a squared-off steering wheel.

All A6 trims benefit from enhanced variable-ratio steering that provides sharper responses than we remember in the outgoing car, even if it’s still less than chatty about what’s happening at the tires’ contact patches. Fitted with the air-spring setup, the A6 does a commendable impression of a larger A8 sedan, almost wafting down the road with a controlled cushiness. It still handles quite well, but there’s a more pronounced sense of mass and refinement in this configuration. Select Comfort mode and cocoon yourself in the almost eerily quiet interior (a mere 67 decibels and 20 sones at 70 mph, by our measure), and the outside world fades into the background.

The Sport Plus package–equipped car, on the other hand, is noticeably more agile and responsive the harder you push it down twisty roads. This is still a substantial luxury sedan with an unobtrusive engine that requires some prodding to fully motivate, but it’s a far more willing driver’s companion in Sport Plus guise. Surprisingly, it was also a tad quieter inside at speed in our testing. Strong brakes, greater grip, and better maneuverability all pay real dividends here. We recorded a healthy 0.97 g of skidpad grip in the sport-tuned car, versus 0.87 g for the air-spring setup on all-season rubber, and the Sport Plus model’s 146-foot stop from 70 mph is a vast 25 feet shorter than its sibling’s.

Ride quality over Southern California’s mountainous two-lanes was taut but never harsh, and the sportiest A6 even traversed the Midwest’s hellscape of frost heaves and potholes without shaking us silly. The Prestige’s standard adaptive setup is significantly more compliant in its softer settings, though we found the Sport Plus’s coils more comfortable than the air springs in their firmest Dynamic mode.

In addition to an entry point only $1500 higher than last year’s base V-6 model, the case for the new A6 has gotten even stronger with Audi’s mid-year update that added a new steering wheel with physical scrolling wheels in place of the current capacitive sliders, plus new graphics and functionality for the infotainment system. We just wish the best-driving version didn’t ultimately cost more than the outgoing S6’s $77,195 base price. At least its improvements don’t come with a subscription.
