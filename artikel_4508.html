The First Way of Not Being There: Detachment

Posted November 14, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

The term dissociation, as we use it today, carries so much in just one word that it has become hard to understand what people are referring to when they say they are dissociating. This confusion exists not only in pop culture but also in the professional field. The term's popularity has made it a common protagonist in the lives of many people who use it to describe all sorts of sensations that scare them to the point of alarm. The literature also uses the term to name several mechanisms and a large number of manifestations. So, I'm trying to help separate them, as I have always been very interested in precision. I believe that if we want to understand ourselves and find solutions to our emotional problems, it's better to know what we are treating.

And it's not only me. Many people in the field have become active in developing better explanations and new ways to identify differences among dissociative experiences.

My last post was the first in a new series on dissociation where I described four concrete and different mechanisms that cause dissociative experiences. This post explains one of the four in detail: Detachment.

To explain detachment, let me start with an example. Lisa got a call from the hospital informing her that her father had had a stroke. She rushed to the hospital and remained calm while talking to the doctors, signing forms, calling her brother, and arranging for someone to pick up her kids. She was a little surprised by how little emotion she felt, but three days later, standing in her kitchen, she started sobbing and couldn't stop. She called me because she wanted to work on what she experienced.

Detachment is one of the main mechanisms behind dissociative experiences, and it's the one that situations like Lisa's may recruit to help deal with them. In detachment, the person remains aware of what is happening and what it means. Lisa knew the gravity of her father's situation. What the mechanism did was to manage how strongly she felt her emotions. Research suggests that the prefrontal cortex, which normally helps regulate emotion, applies the brakes harder on the emotional system, turning down the volume of the experience. That’s why Lisa was able to be present to handle the situation without becoming overwhelmed.

Just think about it. If she had to deal with what she was feeling and also with all the other tasks the situation required, she probably wouldn't have been able to concentrate or make the decisions she had to make. That's why the brain “lowers the volume” of the emotions. Our own sedatives also help. Under this type of intense stress, the brain releases natural opioids that reduce pain and blunt feelings.

Detachment shows up in different ways, depending on what the brain is turning down.

Emotional numbing. What Lisa experienced belongs here. Feeling is turned down. The person knows that something matters but can't really feel it. When people detach this way, they often say, “I know I should feel something.”

Physical numbness. This type is similar, but it's more somatic. What gets turned down are bodily sensations and pain. Soldiers and accident victims sometimes don't notice serious injuries until hours later.

Depersonalization. This is one version of detachment that scares many people because, when the volume is turned down, they feel as if the experience doesn't belong to them. Signals from inside the body, which normally tell us “this is me, this is happening to me,” seem to carry less weight. Brain studies have linked depersonalization to reduced activity in the insula, a region that processes those inner signals. People may feel like observers of their own lives: Their hands look like someone else's, their voice sounds strange, or they watch themselves from a few feet away. The process is meant to be temporary, and it's not dangerous on its own. It feels weird, but if the person doesn’t fight it or keep checking whether it’s gone, it may subside relatively quickly. It can be problematic if it becomes chronic, and fear of the experience can contribute to its persistence. What I mean is that fearing it may be one reason it keeps happening, and if it keeps happening, it can become a regular response of the system when there is a threat.

Derealization. This version of detachment works in a similar way to depersonalization, but what is turned down is the felt sense of the outside world instead of the felt sense of the self. Everything looks right, but it doesn't feel familiar or real. A person may say their own street looks like a movie set, or that there is glass between them and everything else. What's missing is the sense of reality that usually comes with what we see. Like depersonalization, it can be temporary and is not dangerous on its own. It doesn't necessarily cause harm to the system or leave consequences once it's over. If the brain doesn't recover its baseline, the person may become more sensitive, feel overwhelmed more easily, and predict that the detachment will return. That can make the mechanism more likely to recur, causing fear and maintaining the cycle of increasing sensitivity.

To address the mechanism, in all four versions, there are a couple of steps to keep in mind:

If you notice yourself going numb or feeling unreal, it may help to ask what your system is turning down, and why now. If it’s helping, stay there and give it time to find the assurance that you don’t need it anymore.
