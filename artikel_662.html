Luke Bryan & Luke Combs Channel Old-School Influences in ‘Finders Keepers’: ‘I Want to Put Songs Out That Could Go Right Into The Set List’

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Billboard's Makin' Tracks column looks at the writing and recording behind the Luke Bryan and Luke Combs collaboration "Finders Keepers."

“Luke-ing” back across the years, Luke Bryan didn’t have much country competition in branding his name.

Hank Williams recorded a series of recitations under the pseudonym Luke the Drifter, and stretching the boundaries a bit, Waylon Jennings had a landmark hit with “Luckenbach, Texas (Back to the Basics of Love).” Bryan got used to being known in the genre by a single syllable until a “Hurricane” hit in 2016.

“I thought I had the Luke name,” Bryan says. “I thought I had that solidly before Luke Combs. Not that Bryan has a problem with the situation. In fact, he enlisted Combs as a feature voice on “Finders Keepers,” released by Row Crop/MCA Nashville to country radio via PlayMPE on Oct. 12. It lists on Billboard’s Country Airplay as “Luke Bryan & Luke Combs,” though Bryan doesn’t particularly care how people refer to them.

“Luke Squared,” he says, “whatever you want to call it.”

The song’s writers were just happy they got the call. Bryan heard it — and recorded it — 15 days after its official date of creation when his manager, KP Entertainment founder/president Kerri Edwards, was going through unopened emails during Bryan’s session at Nashville’s Ocean Way on March 6. She played it for Bryan and his producers, Jeff and Jody Stevens, during their drive to Dicey’s Tavern on a lunch break, and they added it to that afternoon’s material. The musicians picked it up easily.

“They ran through it one time, and ran through it another time, and that was it,” Jeff recalls.

Songwriter Sam Ellis (“What If I Never Get Over You,” “More Hearts Than Mine”) hosted the writing session that spawned “Finders Keepers” on Feb. 19 at his East Nashville studio. When the initial song ideas fell flat for Ellis and his co-writers, Jimi Bell (“Backup Plan,” “Last Night Lonely”) and Beau Bailey (“Stay Country or Die Tryin’,” “Favorite Country Song”), Bailey apologetically mentioned a hook he’d concocted during a brainstorming exercise: “Finders keepers, losers drinkers.” No apology was necessary; his co-writers loved the wordplay.

“They just went to town,” Bailey says, “They’re lyrical and musical assassins.”

They had chatted up their passion for Dwight Yoakam at the start of the appointment, and they used the pace of Yoakam’s “Suspicious Minds” cover, from the 1992 Honeymoon in Vegas soundtrack, as a template for “Finders Keepers.”

“I’m a huge Dwight Yoakam fan, so that was 100% channeled that day,” Bell says. “As soon as he said that title, I kind of heard a Dwight Yoakam thing in my mind.”

They sketched out the musical framework for it, ensuring that the chorus would start in an energetic place. It was obvious that the plot needed to address a broken heart, and the song’s second line, “Made a home in that hole you dug,” acknowledged the protagonist blew it. “You do know it’s his fault,” Ellis says, “and you’re kind of on his side.”

They doubled down on the breakup a few lines later with “She took the ‘U’ right out of ‘us,’” though the twist in that phrase may escape most listeners until they see the lyrics in print.

“It’s always fun when you can sneak a little bit of smartness into a happy, full-on, go-lucky drinking song,” Bailey notes. “Then when you dissect it, you’re like, ‘Wait, this actually makes sense. We haven’t heard it said like this.’”

They introduced the “Finders keepers, losers drinkers” hook in amped-up fashion to start the chorus, though returning to that hook for the payoff presented a challenge. Those four words worked better as a set up for the last line, which ultimately became a consolation prize for the guy on his barstool: “Hey man, at least the beer is cold.”

The chorus ended that way for a bit, but as they worked with it further, it called out for more. So they tacked a post-chorus line onto the cold beer: “So grab you one, the night is good, and the night is young.” That phrase came with three-part harmony, performed mostly over a heavy kick drum, a la Alabama’s “Mountain Music.” The second time around, the post-chorus grew even longer, as the Alabama section focused on the hot girls dancing: “Don’t look now, one’s lookin’ your way.”

“I love just having those dualities,” Bell says, “turning what otherwise would have been a kind of a sad situation into a party song.”

Bell sang lead for the demo, produced by Ellis with an instrumental break featuring an electric guitar that handed off the back half of the solo to a steel guitar. They thought the song was a winner — that Alabama-styled post-chorus cinched it — and Bell’s publisher, Worktape Music owner/operator Travis Gordon, envisioned it for Bryan. When Edwards played it for her artist, Bryan was won over by the Alabama sound. “It’s kind of a little cherry on top at the end of the chorus,” he says.

Returning from lunch, Bryan’s creative team made some small changes to the demo’s arrangement, particularly for the final chorus, where Jeff Stevens reworked the chord structure. “They had gone to a chord progression that, to me, didn’t feel like it contributed to the energy level that it should be at that point in the song,” he says.

They went back to the original chords and had drummer Miles McPherson — working his first Bryan session — deaden cymbal crashes with his hands to create a power sound. They brought fiddler Joe Spivey into Sound Emporium to enhance the old-school country sound, though Spivey was hesitant to play anything outside of long notes, since the original band had already filled up much of the space.

“It just felt like it was slowing it down,” Jody says. “It needed some fiddle parts that moved with the rhythm of the song.”

Jody dictated some simmering passages, and Spivey nailed it within 10 minutes. Bryan’s tracking-day vocals weren’t where he wanted them — “I had come off the road,” he says, “and I sounded like a coyote caught in a bear trap” — but he knocked out the final version in a date at Blackbird Studios.

Next up, background vocalist Trey Keller mirrored the demo’s original harmonies, but Jeff had him redo the part, creating more of a Ricky Skaggs bluegrass tone. All of those pieces were in place when Bryan sent “Finders Keepers” to Combs, who agreed immediately to sing on it. Jeff and Jody recorded him at producer-engineer Chip Matthews’ studio in three short takes.

“We couldn’t have been in there more than about 30 minutes for vocals,” Jody says.

Edwards later played it for Bell and Ellis in person, and they flipped out when they heard Combs emerge on verse two — they’d not projected it as a collaboration. The Luke Squared dueling voices made it a likely radio release, but Ellis also hoped to hear it outside the bars on Nashville’s Lower Broadway.

“I wanted this thing to sound like downtown, where you’re walking by and you can hear a band in the distance playing it,” he says. “That’s something you want to hear cruising down Broadway, you know, chicken picking and all that stuff.”

It’s working well on radio, positioned at No. 30 on the Country Airplay chart dated Nov. 28. And Bryan is focused on its concert benefits, too.

“I’m really in an uptempo mood these days,” he says. “I want to put songs out that could go right into the set list for my tours and really add a lot of energy.”
