The Thing Meets Brokeback Mountain In This Award-Winning Sci-Fi Author's 2027 Book

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Since John Carpenter’s The Thing (1982), few works of horror media have been quite so successful at capturing the uncanny terror of being isolated on icy terrain.

Adapted from the 1932 sci-fi novella Who Goes There? by John W. Campbell, that filmmade full use of its Antarctic setting, turning the mere possibility of what lives out on the ice into the ultimate boogeyman. With nowhere to run and nothing on the horizon but more snow, the tight-knit group of researchers at the center of The Thing became strangers as paranoia—and a malevolent entity—invaded their base, turning colleague against colleague in a fight to stay alive.

In all great works of horror-on-ice fiction, the frozen landscape has a way of becoming a character in and of itself, one that is as much of a villain to the characters as any supernatural entity. Luckily, the reigning queen of literary sci-fi is following in that tradition with her next novel.

Julia Armfield’s Up to the Light, which releases on March 30, 2027, will be a treat for anyone who loved her debut novel, Our Wives Under the Sea (2022). This one is similar in both premise and structure: it’s told in two timelines, predominantly by two soulmate narrators, in the period leading up to and after a potentially supernatural disaster.

In 1994, best friends and expert mountain climbers Petal and Liam join an expedition led by Jim, a wealthy, vain documentarian, to scale Mount Varda, Western Europe's highest summit. If successful, their eight-person crew would be the first to reach the summit. In 2005, only Petal, Liam, and Jim remain—as does Jim’s best-selling, highly disputed memoir about their doomed expedition, excerpts of which are interspersed between Liam and Petal’s narration.

In true Armfield fashion, however, the supernatural disaster is only one part of the story. It takes nearly two-thirds of the novel for her mountain climbers to actually begin their climb, but the delay doesn’t feel like a letdown. Long before a single character sets foot at the base, the mountain is already a character in its own right. It produces strange visions, emits phantom sounds, and even changes shape overnight. There’s also the looming phantom of previous failed expeditions, particularly a doomed climb from 21 years earlier that resulted in the deaths of its entire crew. The mountain, like a haunted mansion, seems to cast out unwanted visitors.

Up to the Light was pitched as "Into Thin Air meets The Haunting of Hill House by way of Brokeback Mountain." Though that last comparison seems out of left field, it's a welcome twist as the story takes shape.

It may take most of the novel for Armfield to set up the disastrous climb, but she wastes no time building out a slow-burn romance. (So slow-burn that revealing the two characters involved would involve a mild spoiler.) Somewhere, amid all the haunted mountaineering, Armfield finds time to craft an achingly romantic rivals-to-lovers arc that, along with another beautifully written platonic relationship, gives the book its emotional core.

Armfield’s sci-fi has always been horror-adjacent, but Up to the Light is perhaps her only novel that truly belongs in that category. She reworks some of her favorite tropes from Our Wives Under the Sea and Private Rites—shared hallucinations, characters whose physical bodies seem to be disappearing bit by bit, an entity too cosmic for any one narrator to fully articulate—but dials up the scares to a new level.

The horror in this novel feels akin to Mike Flanagan’s in The Haunting of Bly Manor—chilling, but also so emotionally resonant that the worst terror comes not from jump scares, but from anticipating the next devastating loss.

Still, this book is terrifying. Among the climbers, there’s a constant, looming memory of the lost crew from 20 years earlier, and a creeping feeling that somehow, they’ve each been here before. Where the researchers in The Thing grew openly suspicious of each other, these climbers are too nervous to even mention the strange happenings that abound on this mountain.

But there is also real environmental terror to contend with: the increasing risk of freezing to death or running out of oxygen, the constant prospect of avalanches and storms, the looming threat of death-by-falling, and the very real possibility of simply getting lost in the snow and never returning. When it all comes to a head, Up to the Light transforms itself in a second from slow-paced literary fiction to an all-out bloodbath.
