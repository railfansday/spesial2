Blue Jays add entertaining mystery box Kaima Taira to their rotation on five-year, $80 million deal

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

The Toronto Blue Jays, perpetual free-agency bridesmaids, did not reel in Tarik Skubal earlier this week, despite offering the two-time Cy Young winner $420 million over nine years. Thus marked another instance, another winter of Canada's club finishing second on a top-tier free agent. It happened with Juan Soto, with Shohei Ohtani, with Kyle Tucker. For all the commendable aggression shown by Toronto's ownership group over the past few years, for all the dollars offered and committed, it has proven somewhat difficult for the Jays to woo the biggest fish to come north of the border.

But that frustrating state of play hasn't yet squashed Toronto's willingness and ability to spend.

On Tuesday, the Blue Jays agreed to terms with Japanese right-hander Kaima Taira on a five-year, $80 million contract. The deal includes an opt-out after the third season and contains a $5 million signing bonus. Because Taira is headed stateside from Nippon Professional Baseball, Toronto must also pay a $13.875 million posting fee to Taira's now-former NPB team, the Seibu Lions.

Taira, a 27-year-old right-hander who grew up in Ishigaki, Japan's southernmost city, is not Skubal. But he might be just as interesting. Listed at 5-foot-8 and 220 pounds, Taira is built like a dutch oven, albeit one with a triple-digit fastball. The undersized hurler matriculated into NPB out of high school as a fourth-round pick. Debuting at 19 years old in 2019, Taira initially worked as a reliever before transitioning to Seibu's rotation for the 2023 campaign. He showed well that season, posting a 2.40 ERA across 150 innings. A forearm issue derailed his 2024 season and eventually bumped him back into the bullpen, where he stayed for 2025.

This past year, Seibu gave Taira another chance to start, and the stout flamethrower took the opportunity by the horns. Taira threw 146 innings across 22 starts with a 1.36 ERA, a 24.8% strikeout rate and a 7.9% walk rate. Yuri Karasawa of Japan Ball, the industry's premier English-language NPB resource, mentioned on a recent episode of "Baseball Bar-B-Cast" that continuing as a starter was an absolute must for Taira as he made the transition to MLB.

He seems primed to get that opportunity in Toronto.

Taira will join a rotation helmed by AL Cy Young runner-up Dylan Cease, whose first season in Toronto proved to be a magnificent success. Midseason trade addition José Soriano and 2025 postseason hero Trey Yesavage also figure to retain rotation spots, barring injury. Cody Ponce and Spencer Arrighetti will battle it out for the No. 5 spot, with the other joining a long list of upper-level prospect depth options such as Ricky Tiedemann, Jake Bloss, Johnny King and Nolan Perry.

Accordingly, expect Toronto to now turn its offseason focus to its offense, a unit that disappointed massively last season after a rip-roaring 2025. Seiya Suzuki, Randy Arozarena and Jazz Chisholm Jr. are all reasonable fits still available on the market.

Exactly how well Taira's stuff and build will translate to the stateside game is impossible to predict, but the diminutive righty immediately becomes something of a statistical outlier. San Diego Padres reliever Yuki Matsui was the only pitcher in MLB last season to measure in at 5-foot-8 or shorter. In fact, the only pitcher that short who recorded double-digit starts over the past decade is former Blue Jay Marcus Stroman.

In that sense, the odds are stacked against Toronto's $80 million fire hydrant, who also lacks a track record of shouldering a full starter's workload.

But like Stroman, Taira is an atypical athlete. He's remarkably athletic on the mound and when fielding his position. His lower-half strength and flexibility are downright preposterous. Taira's "drop-and-drive" mechanics also mean he gets an outrageous amount of extension for his size and is ridiculously low to the ground at pitch release, both of which create an extreme, difficult-to-hit, flat angle on his heater. At the top of its velocity range, Taira's heater should miss tons of bats in and above the zone, a la peak Craig Kimbrel, Spencer Strider, Sonny Gray or Yoshinobu Yamamoto.

There is, however, a concern that Taira, like some Japanese pitchers before him, might struggle in transitioning from the smaller, tackier and higher-seamed NPB ball to the larger, less-tacky and flatter-seamed MLB ball. As noted by Marquee's Lance Brozdowski in his recent deep dive on Taira, Japanese pitchers tend to lose a noticeable amount of horizontal and vertical movement on their fastballs after making the jump.

Taira's deep array of secondary offerings should help mitigate that problem, as should his incredibly unique release point. His splitter, an offering he leaned on heavily in NPB, will help him neutralize left-handed big leaguers and avoid a massive platoon split. But fastball command and velocity will likely determine how successful Taira is with Toronto.

His heater crept up to triple digits at points during his time with the Lions but sat more in the 94-95 range. If he can sustain or increase that velocity while maintaining his command, he should be capable of sticking in the rotation. It's also worth noting that Taira is a dedicated student of the game and runs his own YouTube channel in which he discusses pitching theory, approach, physics and grips.

In total, Taira is a sure-to-be-entertaining mystery box. Nothing else like this exists in MLB right now, and that should quickly make Taira a fan favorite for Jays fans and neutrals alike.
