Who needs a measles booster: 10 groups the CDC says may need an MMR dose

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Most fully vaccinated adults don't need a measles booster, but 10 groups may need an extra or catch-up MMR dose, and some people should skip it

Most adults who received two documented doses of the MMR vaccine as children are not advised to get another shot against measles. U.S. health authorities treat that two-dose record as lifelong protection. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention does not recommend a routine adult booster for people who have it. The more useful question is not whether everyone needs a measles booster. It is who lacks proof of immunity in the first place.

In the U.S., the measles vaccine comes as part of the combined MMR shot. That shot also protects against mumps and rubella. Children get it in two doses. The first is given at 12 to 15 months of age, and the second at four to six years. A person who completed that schedule and can show it on paper is considered covered.

The CDC recognizes four forms of evidence of measles immunity. Any one of them is enough. They are written documentation of vaccination, a lab test showing immunity, lab-confirmed measles in the past, or birth before 1957. Someone who has none of these may be advised to get vaccinated, even as an adult.

That is where the gaps appear. Paper records can go missing after moves, job changes and years with different doctors. Some people vaccinated in the 1960s received a version of the vaccine that was later replaced. Others reached adulthood with only one dose, or never had their status checked at all. Travel abroad, college housing and work in hospitals or clinics each come with their own standards for proof.

Measles spreads easily from person to person. Public health agencies track who is covered and who is not, especially when cases appear in a community. For most people, the answer to "do I need another MMR shot" depends on birth year, records and circumstances. It does not depend on a fixed booster schedule.

The 10 groups below cover the situations in which an extra, catch-up or replacement dose of the measles vaccine for adults or children may apply. They also cover adults generally presumed immune and the people who should not receive the shot at all. Each case has its own rules for timing and number of doses. Anyone unsure of their status should confirm the right course with a clinician or their local health department.

Adults born in 1957 or later who have no evidence of measles immunity should get at least one dose of the MMR vaccine, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. That single shot is the baseline catch-up recommendation for this group.

The MMR shot is the combined measles, mumps and rubella vaccine used in the U.S.

Whether an adult belongs in this group depends on a specific term: evidence of immunity. The CDC recognizes four forms of it. The first is written documentation of vaccination. The second is a lab test showing immunity. The third is lab-confirmed past measles. The fourth is birth before 1957.

An adult who can show any one of these is not part of this group. An adult who can show none of them is, regardless of age or general health.

The list leaves out some things people often rely on. A parent's memory of a childhood shot is not written documentation. A recalled bout of measles is not the same as a lab-confirmed case. Neither counts on its own.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) generally treats adults born before 1957 as immune to measles. Most people in this age group do not need a measles booster.

The reasoning rests on how widely the virus circulated before vaccines existed. Most people of that generation caught measles as children. A person who recovers from measles generally keeps immunity for life. That lasting protection is what the presumption depends on.

That birth year is more than a rule of thumb. The CDC counts birth before 1957 as accepted evidence of measles immunity on its own. For most people in this group, no vaccination card or blood test is needed to meet that standard.

The cutoff reflects the history of the disease in the U.S. Measles vaccines did not come into use there until 1963. Anyone born in the mid-1950s or earlier spent childhood in an era when the virus moved freely through households and classrooms.

A child who has had only one MMR dose by school age should get the second dose as a catch-up. The second shot is part of the routine U.S. childhood schedule, not an optional add-on.

The MMR vaccine covers measles, mumps and rubella in one injection. Under the standard schedule, the first dose comes in the second year of life. The second is timed to land around the start of school.

Children fall behind for ordinary reasons. A family may move, switch health systems or change pediatricians. Appointments can slip during those changes. A child can then reach kindergarten or the early grades with just one recorded dose.

Schools often ask for vaccination records at enrollment. That check is frequently the point when a missing dose comes to light.

Health care workers should have documented proof of measles immunity, according to the CDC. That means two recorded doses of the MMR vaccine or a lab test showing immunity.

This standard is stricter than the one for most adults. An adult outside health care who was born in 1957 or later and lacks evidence of immunity is advised to get at least one dose. A health care worker in the same position needs two documented doses or lab confirmation instead.

The word "documented" carries weight here. A memory of childhood shots or a parent's recollection is not a record. Proof means a written vaccination record or a lab result. A worker who has neither can close the gap with MMR doses or with a blood test.

The reason for the higher bar lies in who health care workers meet. Measles spreads through the air. A patient can be contagious before anyone has diagnosed the illness. The same buildings also serve patients who cannot receive the vaccine themselves, including pregnant people and those with severe immunocompromise.

Adults who cannot find proof of their MMR shots have two options. They can get another dose, which the CDC states does no harm when records are missing, or they can have a blood test for measles antibodies.

Childhood vaccinations were often logged on paper cards, in a pediatrician's files or in school records. Some of those files no longer exist. A remembered shot does not count on its own. The CDC looks for written documentation of vaccination, not recollection.

A short search can sometimes turn up the missing paperwork. Parents or other relatives may have kept a childhood immunization card. Former schools, colleges and employers sometimes held copies. Many U.S. states run immunization information systems, also called registries, that store vaccination data reported by providers. Military service records may list shots given during service. A longtime family doctor's office may also have older entries on file.

Pregnant people and anyone with severe immunocompromise should not get the MMR vaccine, the CDC advises. The shot is a live-virus vaccine, and that is why these two groups are excluded.

A live-virus vaccine contains a weakened form of the virus rather than a killed one. That design separates today's MMR shot from the inactivated measles vaccine used in the U.S. in the 1960s. It also shapes the short list of people who should not receive it.

Pregnancy is the first exclusion. The CDC advises against giving MMR to anyone who is pregnant. The rule applies to a measles booster, a catch-up dose and a first dose alike. A person who finds a gap in their records during pregnancy can discuss the timing of a later dose with a clinician.

The 1957 cutoff reflects the history of the disease. Measles circulated widely before the vaccine was available. That history is why birth before 1957 counts as evidence of immunity on its own. Adults born in 1957 or later get no such presumption. They need proof from a record or a lab test instead.

One dose is the minimum, not the ceiling. Some adults in this group face more exposure and are advised to get two doses. That includes international travelers, health care workers and college students, each of whom has separate guidance.

This catch-up dose is not a general booster. It is meant for adults whose vaccination records are missing or were never created, and who have no other form of evidence.

A clinician or local health department can help an adult work out which category applies. A lab test showing immunity is one way to settle the question without another shot.

The 1957 line is a presumption about a generation, not a finding about any one person. It assumes past infection based on how common measles was, not on a medical record. An adult in this age group who never had measles as a child would not gain immunity from a birth date.

Adults born in 1957 or later do not get the same presumption. For them, birth year alone is not proof. They need one of the other accepted forms of evidence, or vaccination.

Health care workers are the main exception to the rule. The CDC recommends that health care personnel born before 1957 who lack laboratory evidence of immunity consider two MMR doses. That guidance becomes stronger during a measles outbreak.

Older adults who are unsure about their own history can ask a clinician whether a blood test for measles antibodies makes sense. A local health department can also say whether any extra guidance applies in their area, particularly when cases are being reported nearby.

Anyone who received the inactivated, or killed, measles vaccine between 1963 and 1967 should be revaccinated, according to the CDC. The same advice covers people vaccinated during those years who cannot tell which type of vaccine they got.

The problem is the vaccine itself. The inactivated version was less effective than the live vaccine that replaced it after 1967. A record of a measles shot from that window does not settle the question of protection on its own. For this group, the type of vaccine matters as much as the date.

Many adults vaccinated in the mid-1960s may not know they fall into this group. They got a shot as children, and it may appear on an old card or school form. The gap shows up only when someone checks which product was used.

The "unknown type" category has a practical basis. An old record may note a measles vaccination without naming the product. Some paper cards from that era have since been lost or thrown away. When the type cannot be confirmed, the CDC treats the dose the same way as a killed-vaccine dose. Revaccination is the recommended course in both cases.

Checking takes some legwork. A childhood vaccination card kept by a parent is one place to start. Records from a family doctor or pediatric practice are another. School health files can also hold vaccination details. Some states keep immunization registries, though records from that era may never have been entered. A clinician can help interpret what an old record does or does not show.

Revaccination today means a dose of the MMR vaccine. That is the same live-virus shot used in the routine childhood schedule.

The date range is narrow. People vaccinated after 1967 received the live vaccine and follow the standard two-dose guidance instead. Anyone unsure where they fit can ask a clinician or local health department to review their records.

The remedy is to give the missing dose once the gap is noticed. The series does not need to start over. The first dose still counts, and the catch-up shot completes the pair.

The second dose also does not have to wait for the usual school-age visit. It can be given as early as 28 days after the first. That short minimum interval lets a clinician close the gap at the next available appointment.

Parents often hear the second dose described as a booster. Its main purpose is a little different. A small share of children do not develop protection from the first dose. The second dose gives them another chance to respond.

The same rule applies to a child who got the first dose late. A toddler vaccinated after the usual window still needs a second dose. The 28-day minimum sets how soon that second shot can follow.

Parents unsure of a child's status can start with the record kept by the child's pediatrician. State immunization registries and school health offices may also hold copies. If the record shows one dose, the child's clinician or local health department can arrange the second.

Adults without evidence of measles immunity should get two MMR doses at least 28 days apart before traveling outside the U.S., according to the CDC. Babies aged six to 11 months should get one early dose before an international trip.

The adult advice applies to travelers who cannot show they are already protected. It sets a higher bar than the general guidance for adults who lack evidence of immunity but are not traveling. Before a trip abroad, the CDC calls for the complete two-dose series.

The spacing rule sets the timeline. The second dose cannot be given sooner than 28 days after the first. A traveler who needs both doses before departure has to get the first one at least 28 days before leaving.

The infant recommendation moves the first shot much earlier than usual. A baby aged six to 11 months who is going abroad should get one MMR dose before the trip, the CDC advises.

For a baby, the departure timeline is simpler than for an adult. Only one dose is needed before the trip, so the 28-day spacing rule does not set an early deadline.

That early dose does not count toward the routine series. A baby who receives it still needs two more doses, starting at 12 months. A child vaccinated early for travel therefore ends up with three MMR doses in total instead of the usual two.

The travel dose is an extra shot tied to the trip, not a head start on the childhood schedule. Skipping either of the later doses would leave the routine series incomplete.

Travelers $TRV who are unsure whether they need one dose, two or none should check with a clinician or their local health department well before departure. For adults who need both shots, the 28-day gap between doses means that check cannot wait until the final days before a flight.

Birth year works differently for this group. For most adults, birth before 1957 counts as evidence of immunity on its own. Health care workers born before 1957 who lack lab evidence of immunity or lab confirmation of past measles are a separate case. Under CDC guidance, they should consider getting two MMR doses.

That advice becomes firmer during an outbreak. When measles is spreading in a community, the guidance for unprotected health care workers is stronger than in ordinary times. Public health authorities direct the response in each outbreak.

The guidance reaches beyond doctors and nurses. The CDC's term is health care personnel. It covers paid and unpaid workers in health care settings who could be exposed to patients or infectious material.

A worker unsure of their status can start by looking for old school, military or employer records. Where none turn up, an occupational health office, clinician or local health department can advise on doses or testing.

The CDC recommends two doses of MMR vaccine for students at colleges, universities and other post-secondary schools who lack evidence of measles immunity. For these students, a single catch-up dose does not meet the recommendation.

Campus life puts many people in shared space. Students often share dorm rooms, bathrooms and dining halls. They also sit together in lecture halls, labs and libraries for hours at a time.

Measles spreads through the air. An infected person releases the virus by coughing or sneezing, and people nearby can breathe it in. The CDC also notes that the virus can linger in a room after the infected person has left. In a shared building, one case can expose people who never met the sick student.

A person with measles can pass it on before the telltale rash appears. Early signs resemble a cold, with fever, cough, runny nose and red, watery eyes. A roommate or lab partner may already have been exposed by the time anyone suspects measles.

The routine U.S. childhood schedule already includes two doses. A student who completed it generally meets the standard. A student falls short of it after receiving only one dose, or when records cannot show a second. A student with no record of any dose would need both. Students who grew up under another country's vaccine schedule may also need to check their records.

Documentation is the practical step. Many U.S. colleges and universities ask incoming students for a written immunization record before enrollment or move-in. A student with only one documented dose can close the gap with a second MMR shot.

The recommendation is not limited to four-year schools. It covers students at any post-secondary institution, including community colleges, graduate programs and trade schools, who lack evidence of immunity.

A student unsure about eligibility or past doses can check with a campus health center, a clinician or the local health department.

When no record turns up, another MMR dose is one route. It takes a single clinic visit and does not depend on a lab result. A dose given as an adult also creates a new written record. That record can be kept for future school, job or travel checks.

The second option is a measles titer test. This blood test looks for measles IgG antibodies. The immune system makes these after vaccination or a past infection. A health care provider draws a blood sample, and a lab analyzes it. A positive result serves as lab evidence of immunity in place of vaccination records.

The test has a limit. A negative or equivocal result does not always mean a person is unprotected. Someone who is still protected can return a low or unclear reading. That makes a negative result harder to interpret than a positive one. It is a point to raise with whoever ordered the test.

The better fit depends on individual circumstances, such as travel plans, job requirements or school rules. A clinician or local health department can review what records exist and recommend the next step.

Local health departments may recommend an extra MMR dose during a measles outbreak. In some outbreak settings, a third dose has been recommended for people at increased risk. Those decisions belong to public health authorities, not to individuals acting on their own.

The routine schedule gives most people two doses of MMR in childhood. An outbreak changes the picture for a specific place and time. Health officials look at who has been exposed, where cases are clustered and which groups face the highest risk. They then decide whether an extra shot makes sense for that community.

A third dose is not part of the standard schedule. It has been recommended only in certain outbreak situations. Even then, it applies only to people whom public health authorities identify as being at increased risk. News of an outbreak in another city does not by itself call for a third dose. People who live or work inside an affected setting should follow the instructions their local health department issues.

Timing matters after a known exposure. An MMR dose given within 72 hours of exposure may provide some protection against measles. That window is short. A person who learns they shared a room, a flight or a waiting area with an infected person should contact a clinician or the health department quickly. Waiting for symptoms can mean missing the window.

The vaccine is not the only option after exposure. Immune globulin, a product made from antibodies, may be given within six days of exposure to certain people. It offers a second route when the 72-hour window for the vaccine has passed. It also offers one when the vaccine itself is not appropriate for the exposed person. Health officials decide case by case who should receive it.

People caught up in an outbreak investigation may be asked about their vaccine history. Written records of past doses give health departments a clear starting point. Anyone unsure of their status after an exposure should call a clinician or the local health department before showing up at a clinic. Calling ahead lets staff plan the visit and limit the chance of exposing others in a waiting room.

Timing also matters before a pregnancy begins. People planning to conceive should wait four weeks after an MMR dose before trying to become pregnant, according to CDC guidance. Anyone planning a pregnancy who is unsure of their measles immunity can build that four-week window into their plans.

Severe immunocompromise is the second exclusion. The CDC advises against MMR for people whose immune systems are severely weakened. The guidance refers to severe cases specifically. Whether a particular condition or treatment rules out the shot is a question for a clinician who knows the patient's medical history.

The third exclusion involves allergies. Anyone who had a severe allergic reaction to a previous MMR dose should not receive another one. The same applies to anyone who had a severe allergic reaction to a component of the vaccine. The guidance is written around severe reactions. A clinician can review the details of any past reaction.

These exclusions apply to the whole shot. MMR protects against measles, mumps and rubella in a single injection. A person who should not receive it goes without the vaccine's protection against all three diseases.

Deciding who should get a measles booster starts with checking these exclusions. Someone in a group that would otherwise qualify — an international traveler, a college student or a health care worker — still should not receive MMR while pregnant or severely immunocompromised. A clinician or local health department can confirm whether the shot is appropriate in an individual case.
