4 factors behind California's quakes, from the San Andreas to the Ring of Fire

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Four factors explain California's constant shaking and why clusters of quakes can feel like a spike, from fault lines to depth.

Many Californians have felt the ground move more often lately, and a string of tremors has left them wondering whether something has changed beneath the state. Shaking is the ordinary condition of the region, and residents never notice nearly all of it. Southern California alone records about 10,000 earthquakes in a typical year, according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), and only several hundred of them are stronger than magnitude 3.0. Most of the movement there is too faint for anyone to feel. A single quake that people do feel therefore stands out against a background that only seems quiet. The useful question is not whether California is shaking, because it always is. The question is what makes a particular run of quakes noticeable enough to feel like a surge.

Even the quakes strong enough to make the news are a regular part of life in Southern California. USGS estimates that the region has about 15 to 20 quakes above magnitude 4.0 in a typical year. A burst of such quakes also cannot tell anyone when a large one will strike. Pinning down the timing of a quake is “one of the most difficult unsolved problems in science,” Lillian Burkhard, lead author and a research affiliate at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa, said, as reported by TIME.

USA TODAY set out to explain what was behind California’s recent jump in earthquake activity. Its reasons include the San Andreas Fault, the Pacific’s Ring of Fire, and the depth at which a quake begins. Some are permanent features of the land that explain why California always shakes. Others explain how one quake becomes a cluster, or why a whole city feels it. None of them shows that the ground under California has become more active.

Central California's recent cluster of tremors happened along the state's best-known fault line. Those tremors struck a stretch of ground directly next to the San Andreas Fault, close to Highway 25, according to USA TODAY. Much of California lies along that fault, which, TIME reports, extends from the Salton Sea to San Francisco. With a fault that long under so much of the state, quakes beside it are an ordinary part of life there and not a sign of change. The fault explains why the ground moved in that spot, but it does not show that a large rupture is coming.

The part of the fault that concerns seismologists is the southern section, where the San Andreas meets the San Jacinto Fault and where strain has been accumulating for generations. TIME reports that the two faults are under more stress than at any time since the 11th century, and that the last major quake to ease stress in the system they share came 160 years ago. The 20 million people who live along the two faults face that stored strain, and it deserves far more of their attention than any single cluster of tremors.

The worst case is a rupture on the San Andreas that continues into the San Jacinto Fault, and that could happen at only one spot. Cajon Pass is the only point where the two faults intersect, TIME reports, and the faults cross there far below the surface. “A rupture crossing from one region to the other requires favorable conditions at depth,” Burkhard said. Tremors felt elsewhere along the fault cannot show whether a rupture will get through that crossing point, or when.

The San Andreas is a long-term danger to prepare for, not a reason to panic over every new tremor.

Much of what feels like a run of separate quakes is often one rupture and the smaller quakes around it. Near Pinnacles in central California, two smaller quakes came before a magnitude 5.0 mainshock, and more tremors came after it, according to USA TODAY. A news feed may list each of those tremors as its own event, but all of them trace back to a single break in the ground. Counted one at a time, they suggest a fault that is getting busier.

The label “foreshock” is given only after a larger quake arrives. The two early quakes near Pinnacles looked like any other minor quake at the time. The familiar shape of a sequence, with small quakes building to a large one and then tapering off, can be seen only after it has ended. That shape is a description of the past, not a forecast.

The size of the cluster depends on who counted the tremors and over what stretch of time. One tally, from Fox Weather, puts the tremors after the mainshock at more than a dozen. KSBW reports only six aftershocks, along with a mainshock that the U.S. Geological Survey first measured at magnitude 5.1 and later revised down to 5.0. Early numbers in any sequence are provisional and settle only as reports come in. A spike built from these counts is partly a matter of bookkeeping, and the same rupture can be logged as a handful of quakes or as more than a dozen.

Before reading a burst of quakes as a trend, a reader should ask how many separate ruptures it contains, and often the answer is one.

The recent quake in Los Angeles began only about 10 miles below the surface, and that shallow start is why people throughout the city felt it, according to USA TODAY. A shallow source of this kind is part of the area's usual pattern. In 2024, a different quake near Malibu, Calif., started at a similar depth of about 8 miles underground, according to U.S. Geological Survey figures reported by The Associated Press. Depth explains why so many people noticed the quake, not whether the ground has grown more active. When a shallow source puts a routine quake in front of an entire city, a few in a row are enough to make ordinary activity feel like a spike.

A quake that people across a whole region feel can still be ordinary to the scientists who study the ground. "It's sort of run of the mill for earthquake country," seismologist Lucy Jones said of the 2024 Malibu quake, as reported by The Associated Press. The number of people who notice a quake tells no one whether it will do harm. Real harm in Los Angeles has come from much larger quakes, and 64 people died in the magnitude 6.6 San Fernando earthquake in 1971. The danger of a quake depends on its size, not on how far across a city people feel it.

A shallow quake that everyone feels is a sign of where it began, not a sign that a bigger one is coming.

California shakes first because of where it sits on the planet. Of all the earthquakes on Earth, 90% occur along the Ring of Fire, a band that stretches 25,000 miles around the edge of the Pacific Ocean and takes in California, according to USA TODAY. Only 5% to 6% of the world’s quakes happen along the Alpide belt, the next most active zone, according to USGS. California’s quakes belong to a pattern far larger than the state itself.

The belt is the reason California always shakes, and for that reason it cannot explain a sudden rise. Instruments around the world pick up an estimated 500,000 earthquakes in a year, according to USGS, and most happen on the belt that runs through California. The San Andreas Fault is this same belt seen up close in one state, so the two are one explanation at two scales, not separate causes.

The Ring of Fire will remain long after any burst of quakes fades, which makes it a fact about where Californians live, not about what is changing.
