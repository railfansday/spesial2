From the 1935 act to Medicare: 10 milestones in the history of Social Security

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Trace the history of Social Security through 10 turning points, from Roosevelt's 1935 law and the first checks to Medicare, the 1983 reforms and today's trust fund outlook

Social Security began as a narrow answer to old-age poverty during the Great Depression. The Social Security Act of 1935 created federal old-age benefits for retired workers, paid for by a tax on wages. Over the following decades, Congress turned that retirement program into something much broader. It came to cover spouses, young children, survivors and disabled workers. It also became the legal home of Medicare.

The idea was not new. Germany had adopted a national old-age insurance system in the 1880s under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck. That system served as a precedent for the American program, which arrived decades later.

The history of Social Security explains much of how the program works now. Many of the rules that workers and retirees deal with today trace back to a specific law passed in a specific year. The payroll tax on wages took effect in 1937. Benefits for spouses, children and survivors arrived with the 1939 amendments. Disability insurance followed in 1956. Automatic inflation adjustments were written into law in 1972 and began in 1975.

Each of those changes filled a gap in the original design. The 1935 law paid no monthly benefits to the spouses or survivors of workers. It offered no insurance for workers who became disabled before retirement age. Until the mid-1970s, any increase in benefits depended on Congress passing a new law.

The 10 slides that follow trace that arc in order. They begin with Franklin D. Roosevelt's signature on the 1935 act and the committee chaired by Labor Secretary Frances Perkins that drafted it. They move through the first lump-sum payment to Ernest Ackerman of Cleveland and the first monthly check to Ida May Fuller of Vermont. Next come the family and disability expansions, the creation of Medicare in 1965 and the launch of Supplemental Security Income under the 1972 amendments. The series closes with the 1983 law that raised the full retirement age and with the program's current funding and trust fund outlook.

Each slide covers one turning point and can be read on its own. Taken together, they form a Social Security timeline that runs from the first checks to today.

President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935. The law created federal old-age benefits for retired workers and became the legal foundation of today's Social Security program.

Roosevelt signed the bill during the Great Depression. Mass unemployment had cut off wages for millions of workers, and bank failures had erased many families' savings. Before this law, older Americans who could no longer work had no national retirement benefit to fall back on.

Retirement income was only one part of the act. The law also set up an unemployment insurance system run jointly by the federal government and the states. Under that structure, states operated their own programs within a national framework. Unemployment insurance gave laid-off workers temporary income, a direct response to the joblessness of the era.

A third piece of the law sent federal grants to the states. That money supported aid to dependent children, aid to the blind and public health programs. It also funded old-age assistance, a separate need-based program for older people with little or no income. The mix of insurance and grants made the act a broad social welfare law, not just a pension bill.

The old-age program was designed around work. Benefits were reserved for retired workers who had earned wages in jobs the law covered. Payroll taxes paid by workers and their employers would fund them. The self-employed were among the groups left outside the system at first.

The law created a three-member Social Security Board to administer the new programs. John G. Winant, a former Republican governor of New Hampshire, became its first chairman. The board was the forerunner of today's Social Security Administration.

The act set 65 as the age when retirement benefits could begin. That age remained the full retirement age for decades, until later legislation raised it in stages.

The 1935 statute remains in force today. Each major expansion since then has come as an amendment to it rather than as a separate law.

Labor Secretary Frances Perkins chaired the Committee on Economic Security, the panel that drafted the proposals behind the Social Security Act of 1935. President Franklin D. Roosevelt created the committee in 1934.

Perkins was the first woman to serve in a U.S. cabinet. She led the committee while holding the post of labor secretary.

The committee's job was to turn the Roosevelt administration's policy goals into specific legislative proposals. Those proposals became the basis of the law Congress passed in 1935.

The committee's work is the link between the administration's plans and the statute that created Social Security. Its drafts shaped the program in the form that took effect.

Social Security payroll taxes took effect in January 1937. Workers and employers each paid 1% on the first $3,000 of a worker's annual wages.

The cost was split evenly between the employee and the employer. That shared arrangement became the program's basic funding model.

The $3,000 figure worked as a ceiling. Wages above that amount in a given year were not taxed. A worker who earned more than $3,000 paid the same tax as one who earned exactly $3,000.

The tax depended on a way to track each person's wages. The government issued Social Security account numbers so employers could report every worker's covered earnings. Those earnings records later determined who qualified for benefits and how much each person received.

Collection began before regular benefits did. Payroll deductions came out of workers' wages starting in 1937. Monthly retirement checks did not go out until 1940.

Lump-sum payments filled that gap. The original 1935 law provided one-time payments to covered workers who reached 65 before they could qualify for monthly benefits. It also provided them to the estates of covered workers who died. In both cases, the payment gave something back for the taxes the worker had already paid.

Ernest Ackerman of Cleveland received the first of these lump-sum payments. It marked the first time the new system paid money out instead of only taking it in.

The lump-sum stage was temporary. Monthly benefits replaced it as the program's main form of payment.

Ida May Fuller of Vermont received the first ongoing monthly Social Security retirement check on January 31, 1940. Her payment marked the program's shift from one-time lump sums to the regular monthly income it pays today.

Fuller lived in Ludlow, Vermont, and worked as a legal secretary. Her employer was a law practice that included John G. Sargent, who later served as U.S. attorney general under President Calvin Coolidge.

She retired in November 1939. Around that time, she stopped by the Social Security office in Rutland, Vermont, to ask about benefits while she was in town on an errand.

Her claim put her among the first retirees to collect monthly benefits, which the 1939 amendments had moved up to 1940.

Her record as a contributor was short. Payroll taxes for the program began in January 1937. Fuller paid them for only a few years before she stopped working.

Her time as a beneficiary was far longer. Fuller lived to age 100 and collected a monthly check for the rest of her life. She died in January 1975.

Her payment did not stay fixed over those decades. Congress raised benefits a number of times starting in 1950. Those increases applied to retirees already on the rolls, including Fuller.

The gap between her brief contributions and her long years of benefits made her a frequently cited example of how the early program worked. The first generation of retirees received far more in benefits than they had paid in through payroll taxes.

Fuller was not unusual in that respect. Workers who were already near retirement age in 1937 had little time to build a long contribution record. They could still qualify for monthly checks once payments began.

Her benefit was not capped at the taxes she had paid. It was set by a formula in the law based on her covered earnings, not by the balance of a personal account. That design let the program pay monthly income to people with short work records under the system.

The 1939 amendments to the Social Security Act extended benefits beyond the retired worker. They added monthly payments for the wives and young children of retirees and for the families of insured workers who died.

That change turned Social Security into protection for households, not just individuals. Before 1939, the monthly retirement benefit belonged to the worker alone. A family that had depended on that worker's wages lost the income entirely once the worker died.

Congress addressed that gap in two ways. The first was a set of dependent benefits. A retired worker's wife and young children could now draw monthly payments based on the worker's earnings record.

The second was survivor protection. When an insured worker died, eligible family members could receive monthly benefits that replaced part of the lost income. Those provisions covered widows who had reached retirement age. They also covered younger widows caring for the worker's children, along with the children themselves. A deceased worker's dependent parents could qualify when no widow or child was eligible.

The amendments also changed the timetable. The 1935 law had scheduled monthly retirement payments to begin in 1942. The 1939 legislation moved that start to 1940.

The benefit formula changed as well. The 1935 law had based benefits on a worker's total lifetime covered wages. The 1939 version based them on average monthly wages instead. That shift allowed larger payments for people who retired in the program's early years.

The financing structure gained a formal home too. The amendments created the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund to hold payroll tax revenue not needed for current benefits. The retirement program itself took the name Old-Age and Survivors Insurance, often shortened to OASI. The label reflected the program's wider purpose. It insured a worker against the loss of earnings in old age. It also insured a family against the loss of a breadwinner.

The family-based design from 1939 still shapes Social Security. Benefits for spouses, children and survivors continue to be calculated from one worker's earnings record. The relatives collecting them did not pay into the system on that record. Later laws widened eligibility, but they built on this structure rather than replacing it.

The 1956 amendments created Social Security Disability Insurance, which paid monthly benefits to disabled workers aged 50 to 64. A 1960 law removed that age floor. It opened disability benefits to disabled workers of any age.

The disability program changed what Social Security insured against. Until 1956, the system paid out when a worker reached the benefit age of 65 or died. A worker forced out of the labor force by a disability before 65 had no monthly benefit of their own.

The 1956 law closed that gap, but only for older workers at first. Someone disabled at 45 still fell outside the program. The 1960 law ended that cutoff. A younger worker with a qualifying disability could now draw benefits too.

The 1956 amendments made a second change to eligibility. Women workers and wives could claim reduced benefits as early as age 62.

President Dwight D. Eisenhower was in office for both disability laws. He also held office for the coverage expansion of 1954.

The decade opened under President Harry S. Truman with the 1950 amendments. That law brought a cost-of-living adjustment to benefits. It was the first increase since monthly payments began in 1940.

That 1950 increase was the first of several ad hoc adjustments. Each one was a separate decision by Congress rather than an automatic formula. Retirees had no guarantee that their checks would keep pace with prices from one year to the next.

The 1954 amendments widened the program in a different direction. They extended coverage to groups of workers the original law had left out.

Coverage decides who pays Social Security payroll taxes and who earns credit toward benefits. Workers outside covered jobs could not build a record for retirement, survivor or disability protection. Bringing more occupations into the system enlarged both its tax base and the number of families it protected.

By 1960, the program reached a wider share of the workforce. It also protected against disability at any age, alongside retirement and death.

President Lyndon B. Johnson signed Medicare into law on July 30, 1965, as amendments to the Social Security Act. The law gave Americans aged 65 and older federal health insurance tied to the same system that paid their retirement benefits.

Johnson held the signing ceremony at the Harry S. Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, rather than in Washington, D.C. He picked the site to honor Truman, who had backed national health insurance as president.

Truman sent Congress a proposal for a national health program in 1945. It stalled and never passed during his time in office. At the 1965 ceremony, Truman and his wife, Bess, received the first Medicare cards.

The 1965 law built Medicare in two main parts. Part A, hospital insurance, covers inpatient hospital stays and related care. Part B, medical insurance, covers doctor visits and other outpatient services. Enrollees pay a monthly premium for Part B. Benefits began on July 1, 1966.

The same legislation also created Medicaid. That program is run jointly by the federal government and the states. It covers people with low incomes rather than people defined by age.

Medicare's hospital coverage borrows the payroll tax model that funds Social Security retirement benefits. Workers and employers each pay 1.45% of wages toward Medicare's hospital insurance trust fund. Unlike the Social Security portion of the payroll tax, the Medicare tax has no annual wage cap.

Coverage later reached beyond older Americans. In 1972, Congress extended Medicare to some younger people with disabilities, including those who had received Social Security disability benefits for two years. People with end-stage kidney disease became eligible at the same time.

The program has kept adding benefits since then. The Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 created Part D, a prescription drug benefit that took effect in 2006. Today the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services runs the program. The Social Security Administration still handles much of the enrollment for Parts A and B.

The 1972 amendments to the Social Security Act tied benefit increases automatically to inflation, and the first automatic adjustments took effect in 1975. The same legislation created Supplemental Security Income, a needs-based program for elderly, blind and disabled Americans.

The automatic cost-of-living adjustment, usually shortened to COLA, replaced raises that each required a separate act of Congress. Benefits now rise with consumer prices without a new vote.

The adjustment is based on the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers, known as the CPI-W. The percentage increase in the CPI-W's average for July through September sets the size of the raise. The agency announces each year's COLA in October, and it first appears in benefits paid in January. If the index does not rise, benefits stay the same rather than falling.

President Richard Nixon signed the 1972 amendments into law. The original indexing formula contained a technical flaw that could push benefits up faster than intended. Congress corrected it in the 1977 amendments.

SSI payments began in January 1974. The new program replaced a patchwork of state-run assistance programs for older, blind and disabled adults, which the federal government had helped fund. SSI set a national minimum payment level, and many states add their own supplements on top of it.

SSI differs from Social Security retirement benefits in how it is paid for. It draws on general tax revenues from the U.S. Treasury, not on payroll taxes or the Social Security trust funds. Eligibility depends on income and assets rather than work history. A person who never held a job covered by Social Security can still qualify. Adults must be 65 or older, blind or disabled, and fall under the program's financial limits. Children with disabilities can qualify as well.

The Social Security Administration runs both programs. A person can receive SSI alongside Social Security retirement or disability benefits if their total income stays low enough. Federal SSI payments rise each year by the same COLA percentage as Social Security benefits.

President Ronald Reagan signed the Social Security Amendments of 1983 on April 20, 1983. The law raised the full retirement age in stages from 65 to 67 for people born in 1960 or later.

The changes came from a bipartisan commission chaired by Alan Greenspan. The panel's formal name was the National Commission on Social Security Reform. Its job was to propose fixes for a short-term funding shortfall in the program. Its recommendations became the basis of the bill Congress sent to Reagan that spring.

Greenspan was an economist who had chaired the Council of Economic Advisers under President Gerald Ford $F. He became chair of the Federal Reserve in 1987.

The retirement age increase was phased in gradually. It did not affect anyone who was already close to retirement in 1983. The first affected group was people born in 1938, whose full retirement age rose to 65 and two months. The age then rose by two months for each later birth year. It reached 66 for people born from 1943 through 1954. A second phase brought back the two-month steps for people born in 1955 and later. It ended at 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.

Full retirement age is the age at which a worker can claim an unreduced retirement benefit. Raising it meant that a worker who claims at a given age before 67 receives a smaller monthly benefit than under the old rules. Workers can still start retirement benefits as early as 62, but at a reduced rate.

The law also made part of Social Security benefits subject to federal income tax for the first time. The tax applied only to higher-income recipients. Under the 1983 rules, up to half of benefits could be taxed once a recipient's income passed a threshold. That threshold was $25,000 for single filers and $32,000 for married couples filing jointly. The revenue from the tax is credited to the Social Security trust funds.

A third major change widened the program's coverage. New federal employees hired after 1983 were brought into Social Security. Before that, most federal workers had been covered by the separate Civil Service Retirement System.

Social Security is paid for mainly through a payroll tax of 12.4% on wages. Workers and employers each contribute 6.2%, and the tax applies only to earnings up to an annual cap.

That structure follows the program's original design, in which both sides of the paycheck shared the cost. The rate and the wage ceiling are far higher now. The even split between worker and employer has not changed.

Self-employed people pay both halves themselves. Under the Self-Employment Contributions Act, they owe the full 12.4% on net self-employment earnings up to the same cap.

The cap is formally called the contribution and benefit base. It is adjusted each year to track growth in average wages across the economy. Earnings above it are not subject to the Social Security tax. Those earnings also do not count when a worker's future benefit is calculated.

Medicare's hospital insurance tax is collected separately from the Social Security tax. Workers and employers each pay 1.45% toward it. Unlike the Social Security tax, it has no wage cap.

Payroll tax revenue flows into two trust funds. One pays retirement and survivor benefits. The other pays disability benefits.

The Social Security Board of Trustees reviews the finances of both funds. Each year it publishes a report projecting how long the funds can keep paying scheduled benefits in full. Those projections shape the debate in Congress over the program's future.

Depletion of a trust fund would not end benefit payments. Payroll taxes would keep arriving from current workers. That revenue would cover a portion of scheduled benefits rather than the full amount.

The main options lawmakers discuss fall into a few categories. They include raising the payroll tax rate, lifting or removing the wage cap and changing the benefit formula. Each choice shifts the cost among current workers, employers and future retirees.
