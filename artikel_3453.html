10 TV Show Remakes Better Than The Original

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

These 10 TV shows were actually remakes of older shows and movies, and they managed to improve upon what came before. TV remakes may not be quite as ubiquitous as movie remakes, but there are still plenty to go around. Some are made better than others, of course.

These 10 shows are some of the best in their genres, and a few rank among the greatest TV shows ever. Some are remakes of successful shows, others of not so successful shows. Some began their lives as movies with big fanbases, and others adapted an unknown film for wider audiences.

Before Joss Whedon developed Buffy the Vampire Slayer for television, he wrote a version of the story that was turned into a film in 1992, directed by Fran Rubel Kuzui and starring Kristy Swanson as the titular vampire slayer. Though not a critical hit or huge commercial hit, it's retained a strong cult following.

However, the TV series is leaps and bounds better. Whedon's storytelling style has always been more effective on TV, and Buffy the Vampire Slayer the series gives Whedon the time and room to flesh out the ever widening world of Buffy. It's a horror/comedy/action staple that is far better than the movie.

The 2003 Battlestar Galactica is just one piece of a large franchise, but if anyone ever mentions the word "Battlestar Galactica", you can be pretty sure they are talking about the 2003 reboot. The original series premiered in 1978 on ABC but budget cuts led to it being dropped.

Galactica 1980 was an attempt at a low-budget remake, but that didn't work either. It wasn't until 2003 that Battlestar Galactic morphed into one of the greatest sci-fi TV shows ever. With modern special effects and the backing of Sci-Fi, the bleak, military sci-fi series got the attention it deserved.

Ricky Gervais' original The Office on BBC Two is still one of the funniest sitcoms ever, and deserves more praise than it gets, but the American remake took the series into another stratosphere. That's what happens when you have Steve Carell in the lead role, and a writing team led by one of the most decorated The Simpsons writers.

The American The Office wisely pivoted after a first season which was sometimes a shot-for-shot remake of the British version, and became a consistently hilarious, surprisingly thoughtful show, with characters you wanted to see grow, but which never traded jokes for story arcs.

There's a group of people who consider Friday Night Lights the film a superior product to the TV show, and I suspect those people have never actually watched the show. The 2004 film does get some things right, including its cast, many of whom also appear in the TV series, sometimes in the same role.

The series is much stronger, with two incredible lead performances from Kyle Chandler and Connie Britton as Coach Eric Taylor and his wife Tami Taylor, respectively. Friday Night Lights brings Dillon, Texas to life, painting it as a town that is so desperate, the success of the local football team really is life and death.

Hawaii Five-0 may have sounded like a cash grab when it was first pitched, but the crime procedural ended up being an improvement on the 1968 original, itself a solid crime series. Far from being a routine refresh of characters and places, the new Hawaii Five-0 stands out on its own.

From classics like Star Trek and The Twilight Zone to more recent gems like Andor, these are the greatest American sci-fi TV shows ever made.

The new Hawaii Five-0 is slick and smart, and it knows what it has to offer, a charming cast and beautiful locations, and it makes sure to never stray too far from those critical aspects. It's not just a new paint job, it takes what made the original such a crowd pleaser, and cranks those elements as far as they can go.

Michael Crichton's Westworld was a movie before it was a TV show, and surprisingly, despite Michael Crichton directing the 1973 film, it was never a Crichton book. He wrote the story as a script. The film is fun, but it hasn't aged well, and it's almost entirely concerned with the horror of being chased by androids.

HBO's Westworld investigates the philosophical implications of the original's conceit, and there are a lot of them. The series dives deep into questions about what it means to be human, and what we're owed as humans. Each season of the show varies in quality and scope, but as a whole, it's a thrilling watch.

2017's animated series DuckTales is a remake of the original 1987 series which starred Scrooge McDuck and his three grandnephews, Huey, Dewey, and Louie, as they went on adventures, usually in pursuit of treasure and other valuables. The 2017 show adds some new characters, like Della Duck, Donald's twin sister.

Whereas the first series was a classic, low-stakes Saturday morning cartoon adventure, the 2017 version investigates its characters on a deeper level, giving them motivations and more agency. DuckTales' understanding of the legacy of Disney and animated cartoons keeps the series fresh and funny.

Taika Waititi's 2014 horror mockumentary, What We Do in the Shadows, is a delightful, flavorful, and somewhat unfulfilling story about what vampires do when they need to get along in boring, 21st-century society. The series, What We Do in the Shadows, brings the story from Wellington, New Zealand to Staten Island.

The TV show fills in on all the fascinating and hilarious gaps of the show. Everything the film hinted at is explored more in depth in the show, and the ensemble of actors in the series have the time and luxury to make their characters their own. It's one of the rare times where the remake completely removes the need to watch the original.

Prime Video's Mr. & Mrs. Smith was a bit of a surprise remake. The 2005 film starring Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie is a classic romantic thriller, and there didn't seem to be much more to explore with the idea. However, Prime Video's series, soon to be an anthology, found a way to succeed.

Instead of being about a couple who realize they are both spies working for opposite organizations, Mr. & Mrs. Smith the show sees two spies assigned to go undercover as a married couple. Donald Glover and Maya Erskine star as coworkers whose fake relationship blooms into something real.

James Clavell's 1975 novel Shōgun was first turned into a miniseries in 1980, and while it was a respectable attempt to adapt the story of political dynastic clashes in 1600s Japan, it wasn't until the Hulu series that the story was really done justice. The series follows John Blackthorne (Cosmo Jarvis), an English seaman who accidentally winds up in Japan.

The Japan John ends up in is one in the middle of a tumultuous succession crisis. The death of the last ruler has left five regents vying for power, and their clashes are violent and sudden. The Hulu series expertly portrays this tense balance of politics and warfare, and is a TV show remake with more to say than even the original book.
