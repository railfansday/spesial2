10 points on marathon cardiac arrest risk from a major U.S. study

The marathon death rate in a major U.S. study was about 0.39 per 100,000 participants, and these 10 points explain where that small risk comes from

A runner who lines up for a U.S. marathon or half marathon faces a very small chance of dying on the course. One large study puts the marathon death rate at about 0.39 per 100,000 participants. That figure comes from research by Kim and colleagues, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012. The study focused on cardiac arrests during long-distance road races in the U.S.

The researchers examined U.S. marathons and half marathons held from November 2000 through May 2010. Those events drew about 10.9 million participants. Across that period, the team counted 59 cardiac arrests among runners, and 42 of those runners died. The arrest figure translates to about 0.54 cardiac arrests per 100,000 participants.

A single death at a large race can draw national attention. The underlying numbers give a steadier picture of the risk. Comparing rates per 100,000 participants puts any one event in proportion.

For anyone weighing whether to sign up for a first 26.2-mile race, the useful questions are more specific than "is it safe." How often does the heart stop during a race? How many of those runners survive? Does distance change the odds? Which runners are most affected, and at what point in the race?

The Kim study answers several of those questions. It found a higher cardiac arrest rate in full marathons than in half marathons. It also found a higher rate among men than among women. Most marathon arrests occurred in the final miles. Among cases with an identified cause, the underlying conditions differed by age. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy appeared more often in younger runners. Coronary artery disease appeared more often in older ones.

Cardiac arrest is not the only danger on a race course. Exertional heat illness is a recognized risk when marathons are run in warm weather. Exercise-associated hyponatremia, a drop in blood sodium caused by drinking too much fluid, has also killed marathon runners.

The 10 slides that follow cover these aspects of marathon cardiac arrest risk and other race-day hazards. Each one sets out what the research found and where the data stops.

U.S. marathons and half marathons produced about 0.54 cardiac arrests per 100,000 participants between November 2000 and May 2010. The figure comes from a study by Kim and colleagues, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012.

The rate rests on 59 cardiac arrests across races with about 10.9 million participants. Dividing the arrests by the participant total gives the 0.54 rate. Scaled up, that is roughly five arrests for every million race entrants.

The rate measures cardiac arrest, not death. A cardiac arrest occurs when the heart suddenly stops pumping blood effectively. It is different from a heart attack, in which a blocked artery cuts blood flow to part of the heart muscle. A heart attack can trigger cardiac arrest, but the two are not the same event.

Without fast treatment, cardiac arrest is usually fatal within minutes. Some runners in the study were resuscitated and survived. For that reason, the arrest rate sits above the death rate drawn from the same races.

The 10.9 million total counts race participants rather than individual people. A runner who entered several events during the study window appears in it more than once. The 0.54 rate is therefore a measure of risk per race entry. It describes the odds attached to a single start, not to a running career.

The study window ran for about nine and a half years. Over that stretch, the 59 arrests average out to roughly six a year across all U.S. marathons and half marathons combined.

The 0.54 figure also blends two distances. Full marathons and half marathons were counted together to produce it, and the risk was not evenly shared between them. The longer race carried the higher rate. As a result, the pooled figure understates the risk of a full marathon and overstates it for a half marathon.

The pooled rate also says nothing about which runners were affected or why. It does not separate cases by the runner's sex, the underlying heart condition or the point in the race where the collapse happened. Each of those details requires its own breakdown of the 59 cases.

Forty-two runners died after suffering cardiac arrest during U.S. marathons and half marathons between November 2000 and May 2010. Those races drew about 10.9 million participants. The resulting fatal rate was about 0.39 deaths per 100,000 participants, according to Kim and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012.

Expressed another way, 0.39 per 100,000 equals about 3.9 deaths per million participants. A group of races with 100,000 combined entrants would, on average, record fewer than one death at that rate.

The study window lasted about nine and a half years. Across that span, 42 deaths average out to roughly four a year among all the U.S. marathons and half marathons the researchers counted. Participation over the same period averaged more than a million runners a year.

Not every cardiac arrest in the study proved fatal. The researchers recorded 59 arrests in total. Of those runners, 17 survived. About 71% of the arrests ended in death, and about 29% did not.

Those survivors explain why the death rate sits below the overall cardiac arrest rate. The two numbers answer different questions. One measures how often a runner's heart stops. The other measures how often that event ends a life.

The 0.39 figure combines both race distances into a single number. In the study, full marathons and half marathons did not carry the same level of risk. The combined death rate describes the pool of 10.9 million participants as a whole, not either distance on its own.

The death count also has a defined scope. Kim and colleagues built their analysis around cardiac arrest. The 42 deaths in their count are runners who died after an arrest. The study was not designed to tally every race-related death from every cause. Other race-day hazards, including heat illness and low blood sodium from overdrinking, fall outside what the cardiac arrest count was built to measure.

Running a full marathon carries a higher cardiac arrest risk than running a half marathon. In U.S. races analyzed by Kim and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012, the full marathon rate was about 1.01 per 100,000 participants. The half marathon rate was about 0.27 per 100,000.

In absolute terms, the two rates differ by about 0.74 cardiac arrests per 100,000 participants.

Expressed as plain odds, about one in every 99,000 full marathon participants went into cardiac arrest during a race. For half marathon participants, the odds were closer to one in 370,000. Both sets of odds come from the same dataset and the same method of counting.

Distance is the clearest difference between the two events. A full marathon covers 26.2 miles, or 42.195 kilometers. A half marathon covers 13.1 miles, or about 21.1 kilometers. Full marathon runners cover twice the ground before they reach the finish line.

The risk gap is larger than the distance gap. The full marathon is twice as long as the half. Its cardiac arrest rate is about 3.7 times higher. Per participant, the rate rose by more than the mileage did. The figures show that pattern but do not explain it.

The split also matters when reading the study's overall rate. That combined figure blends both race types into one number. It sits below the full marathon rate and above the half marathon rate. As a result, it describes neither race well on its own.

For a reader choosing between the two distances, the race-specific rate gives a more precise picture than the blended one. These are population-level rates, not a measure of any single runner's risk.

The distance comparison counts cardiac arrests, including runners who survived. It does not give a separate death rate for each race type. The share of arrests that proved fatal in full versus half marathons cannot be stated from these figures.

Both rates remain small in absolute terms. Even the full marathon rate amounts to about one cardiac arrest per 100,000 participants.

Male runners had a higher rate of cardiac arrest than female runners in U.S. marathons and half marathons. The finding comes from a 2012 New England Journal of Medicine study by Kim and colleagues.

The comparison is a rate, not a raw count. It sets the cardiac arrests in each group against the number of participants in that group. That keeps the comparison valid regardless of what share of the field each sex made up. A difference in totals alone would not show which group faced more risk per runner.

The study's overall cardiac arrest rate blends men and women into one figure. Because men had the higher rate, the male rate sits above that combined figure and the female rate sits below it. The all-runner average does not describe either group exactly.

A group rate describes runners in aggregate. It does not give any one man or woman a personal probability. An individual runner's odds also depend on factors the group rate does not capture, such as an underlying heart condition.

A comparison split by sex also does not, on its face, account for other ways the two groups may differ. The direction of the gap is a pattern in the data. It is not, by itself, evidence of a cause.

Marathon $MPC cardiac arrests tend to strike late in the race. Most of the arrests recorded during full marathons happened in the final miles, according to the 2012 New England Journal of Medicine study by Kim and colleagues.

That timing puts the bulk of the recorded risk in the closing stretch of the course. By that point, runners have already covered most of the distance.

The start line and the opening miles were not where most marathon arrests occurred. The cluster came toward the finish.

The finding describes when the arrests happened, not why. The study's timing result does not, on its own, explain the late pattern. It should not be read as proof of any single cause.

The result also has clear limits. It refers to full marathons. It does not name a specific mile marker where the cluster begins.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was the cause found more often in younger runners who went into cardiac arrest during U.S. marathons and half marathons. That pattern comes from the 2012 New England Journal of Medicine study by Kim and colleagues.

The finding applies only to cases where a cause could be identified. It does not describe every arrest in the study. It also does not give a risk rate for any age group.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, often shortened to HCM, is a condition in which the heart muscle becomes abnormally thick. The thickening frequently involves the septum — the wall that separates the heart's two lower chambers. In some patients, the thicker muscle stiffens the heart and partly blocks blood as it leaves the heart.

The condition is usually inherited. It is linked to variants in genes that make the proteins heart muscle uses to contract. A parent who carries one of these variants can pass it to a child.

The main danger during hard exercise is electrical. Thickened, disorganized heart muscle can set off a fast, chaotic rhythm in the lower chambers called ventricular fibrillation. When that happens, the heart stops pumping blood effectively and the person collapses.

Many people with HCM have no symptoms. Others notice chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting or a racing heartbeat, often during exertion. For some, a collapse is the first sign of the condition.

Doctors usually diagnose HCM with an echocardiogram, an ultrasound scan that shows how thick the heart wall is. An electrocardiogram, which records the heart's electrical activity, can also reveal abnormal patterns.

The condition runs in families. A diagnosis in one person often leads doctors to check parents, siblings and children with an echocardiogram or genetic testing.

Treatment depends on a patient's risk. Doctors may prescribe beta blockers to slow the heart and ease symptoms. People judged at high risk of a dangerous rhythm may receive an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. The device sits under the skin and shocks the heart back into a normal rhythm.

That sets HCM apart from diseases of the coronary arteries. In HCM, the problem lies in the heart muscle itself, not in the vessels that supply it with blood.

Coronary artery disease was the cause found more often among older runners who went into cardiac arrest during U.S. marathons and half marathons. Kim and colleagues reported that age pattern in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012, in a study of races held from 2000 to 2010.

The pattern covers only the cases in which a cause was identified. It shows which conditions turned up among those victims, grouped by age. It does not show what share of all arrests had a known cause. Among younger victims in the same study, a different condition — hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — appeared more often.

Coronary artery disease is a narrowing of the coronary arteries, the vessels that supply blood to the heart muscle. The usual cause is atherosclerosis. That is a buildup of plaque inside the artery walls. The plaque is made of cholesterol, fat and other material. It accumulates over decades. As a result, the disease is far more common in older adults than in younger ones.

It is also the most common type of heart disease in the U.S. Its main risk factors include high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, smoking, diabetes and a family history of early heart disease. Age itself raises the risk.

Hard running sharply increases the heart muscle's need for oxygen. A narrowed artery may not carry enough blood to meet that demand. The resulting shortage of blood flow is called ischemia. Ischemia can disrupt the heart's electrical signals and set off ventricular fibrillation. In that rhythm, the heart quivers instead of pumping blood. Ventricular fibrillation is a common rhythm behind sudden cardiac arrest.

A plaque can also rupture. A blood clot then forms at the site and can block the artery, causing a heart attack. A heart attack and a cardiac arrest are different events. The first is a blockage of blood flow to part of the heart muscle. The second is a sudden loss of the heart's ability to pump. A heart attack can lead to cardiac arrest.

Regular training does not rule the disease out. Coronary artery disease can cause no symptoms for years. In some people, the first sign is a heart attack or a sudden cardiac arrest. The older runners in this group were fit enough to enter and attempt a marathon or half marathon. Some of them still had coronary artery disease when their hearts stopped.

Exertional heat illness is a recognized danger in marathons run on hot or humid days. Its most serious form, exertional heat stroke, is a medical emergency that can kill a runner if the body is not cooled quickly.

The risk comes from the way running generates heat. Working muscles release large amounts of it. The body sheds most of that heat through sweat that evaporates off the skin. A faster pace produces more heat. Humid air slows evaporation. Sweat then drips off instead of cooling the runner. Direct sun adds a further load.

Heat illness runs along a spectrum. At the milder end is heat exhaustion, in which a runner becomes weak, dizzy or nauseated and cannot keep going. Body temperature rises, but brain function stays largely intact. Rest, shade and fluids usually resolve it.

Exertional heat stroke is different in kind. It is commonly defined as a core body temperature above 40°C (104°F) combined with signs that the central nervous system is failing. Those signs include confusion, slurred speech, irrational behavior, collapse and seizures. A runner in this state may not recognize that anything is wrong.

Measuring core temperature accurately in the field takes a rectal thermometer. Oral, ear and forehead readings can show normal values in a runner who is dangerously overheated. A misleading reading can delay treatment.

Treatment depends on speed. Whole-body immersion in cold or ice water cools a runner faster than other field methods. Some large races set up cold-water tubs in their medical tents for this purpose. The standard approach is to cool first and transport second. Organ damage worsens the longer core temperature stays dangerously high.

The risk is not limited to beginners. Fit, fast runners can push their pace hard enough to overheat. Runners who have not trained in warm conditions face a higher risk. They lack heat acclimatization, an adaptation that increases sweating and lowers heart rate during exercise in the heat.

The warning signs are visible to others on the course. Stumbling, disorientation or odd behavior late in a hot race are signs that a runner needs medical assessment.

Drinking too much fluid during a marathon can kill a runner. Exercise-associated hyponatremia is a drop in blood sodium that occurs when a runner takes in more water or sports drink than the body loses. It has caused deaths in marathon runners.

The condition is a matter of dilution. Sodium helps control how water moves between the blood and the body's cells. When extra fluid floods the bloodstream, sodium concentration falls. Water then shifts into cells, which swell.

That swelling is most dangerous in the brain. The skull leaves little room for brain tissue to expand. Severe cases can produce cerebral edema, and that pressure is what makes the condition life-threatening.

Early symptoms are easy to mistake for ordinary race fatigue. They include nausea, headache, bloating and confusion. More serious cases can bring vomiting, seizures and loss of consciousness. Dehydration and heat illness can look similar at a finish-line medical tent. A blood sodium test is how medical staff tell them apart. Giving a hyponatremic runner more fluids can make the problem worse.

The body's own hormones add to the risk. During long exertion, the hormone vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone, can stay elevated. That limits how much water the kidneys release as urine. Excess fluid then stays in circulation instead of being cleared.

A 2005 study in the New England Journal of Medicine tested Boston Marathon $MPC runners at the finish and found hyponatremia in a minority of them. The study tied the condition to weight gain during the race, longer finishing times and body mass index at either extreme. Weight gain matters because it shows a runner drank more than the body lost through sweat and urine.

The study found no link between hyponatremia and whether runners drank water or sports drinks. Sports drinks contain sodium, but far less than blood does. Drinking too much of either can still dilute blood sodium.

The odds of dying in a U.S. marathon or half marathon were about one in 260,000 participants between 2000 and 2010. The odds of a cardiac arrest, fatal or not, were about one in 185,000.

Both figures come from a 2012 New England Journal of Medicine study led by Kim and colleagues. The study reported its findings as rates per 100,000 participants. The odds here are those same rates turned upside down.

A fatal rate of about 0.39 per 100,000 means roughly one death for every 260,000 people who started a race. A cardiac arrest rate of about 0.54 per 100,000 means roughly one arrest for every 185,000 starters.

Split by distance, the same conversion gives very different odds. The study's full marathon arrest rate works out to roughly one cardiac arrest per 99,000 participants. Its half marathon rate works out to roughly one per 370,000. Those are arrest odds, not death odds. The study compared the two distances using cardiac arrest rates, not fatal rates.

The denominator shapes what the figure means. The study counted race participants, not unique people. A runner who entered several events over the decade appears once for each race. The one-in-260,000 figure therefore describes the risk attached to a single race start. It does not measure a person's lifetime risk from distance running.

The data also has limits on what it can compare. It covers U.S. races only, from November 2000 through May 2010. It does not show how race-day odds stack up against other sports. It does not cover races held in other countries. It does not measure the risk of an ordinary training run. Races held after May 2010 fall outside the data entirely.

Plain odds can make a rate easier to grasp than a figure per 100,000. They can also invite comparisons the study never made. The study did not set marathon risk against everyday hazards, and its numbers alone cannot support that kind of comparison.
