A No-Layoff Promise Isn’t About Compassion. It’s About Discipline.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Most leaders who promise “no layoffs” drop the idea when revenue falls.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

That’s how Paul Gund described his position on job cuts. His son Steve, now the third-generation CEO of The Gund Co., still runs the St. Louis composite materials manufacturer by it. It’s a great line. It’s also the least interesting part of the story.

Plenty of leaders say something similar during good years. Few still mean it when revenue falls. The difference isn’t conviction, but what happens long before the downturn arrives.

The results are hard to argue with. As The Wall Street Journal’s Allison Pohle reported, Gund has averaged 18% annual growth for a decade, with about 1,300 employees and roughly $400 million in annual sales, and has never laid anyone off. Hundreds of family-business leaders have visited to study the model and most struggle to copy it.

That’s not surprising. They’re studying the promise when they should be studying the plumbing.

When Pohle was asked how the company avoids layoffs, the first answer wasn’t about values. It was operational: the company staffs conservatively and relies on employees picking up overtime.

A no-layoff policy doesn’t survive on willpower. It survives because hiring decisions made in a strong quarter are made as if a weak quarter is coming. Every open role gets weighed against a promise that can’t be broken later. Headcount isn’t a reward for growth. It’s a long-term obligation the company has to be able to keep.

Almost all companies run the opposite way. They hire ahead of demand when times are good, then cut when the forecast turns. Then they issue a statement about “difficult decisions” and “the long-term health of the business.” The value they claimed – that people matter – was never connected to how they staffed, budgeted, or planned. It was a sentiment, not an operating rule.

This is what I call the Translation Gap. Organizations are good at stating what they believe. They’re good at executing tasks. What they rarely build is the bridge in between: the specific, repeatable behaviors that make a belief true in everyday decisions.

That’s what’s worth stealing. Every principle, value or cultural belief you want to hold under pressure needs a short list of supporting actions that anyone in the organization can point to and apply to their own role. If you can’t name them, each concept will fold the first time things get expensive.

The Gund Company is candid that no layoffs doesn’t mean no one leaves. People get coached out. Not everyone buys into the culture, and when the company finds out someone isn’t a fit, they have the hard conversations.

But that’s the most essential point. A culture that protects people fiercely has to be selective about who is in it. If you promise employees their jobs are safe from the business cycle, you’d better be sure the people you’re protecting are carrying the culture forward. Otherwise the promise becomes a shield for the wrong behaviors, and employees who do buy in start to resent it.

Most leaders tend to treat kindness and rigor as opposites. But the kindest thing you can do for a team committed to a shared way of working is refuse to let that way of working erode.

Gund also admits the no-layoff commitment creates strain. Because hiring is so intentional and the business is growing fast, it’s hard to develop leaders quickly enough. Overtime has its limits. Lean staffing has its limits.

But culture and values don’t die because things get hard. Rather, they die because of the bait-and-switch. Leaders who adopt a principle because it sounds good and never discuss the cost will turn around and abandon it when the bill arrives. The cost was always coming, they just hadn’t planned for it, and employees know it.

A principle worth keeping is one whose trade-offs you’ve already accepted. Gund accepted slower leadership development, heavier reliance on its existing people and harder conversations about fit. That’s the price of the promise. And because they named it, they can manage it instead of being ambushed by it.
