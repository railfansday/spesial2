Here's What the Average Social Security Benefit Actually Covers

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

The average check is designed as a foundation, but senior households spend far more each year than most retirees expect.

By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 20, 2026 at 2:00PM EST

Most retirees lean on Social Security for much of their income, yet the average check was built to replace only part of a paycheck. As of October 2026, the average monthly benefit for a retired worker is $2,092.17, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA).

That amounts to $25,106.04 per year. While this provides a predictable stream of income, it's often significantly less than what you'll need to maintain your standard of living after you stop working.

A household headed by someone age 65 or older spends an average of $61,432 per year, according to the 2024 Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Consumer Expenditure Survey. While housing and healthcare are major line items, costs such as transportation and food also consume a large share of the budget.

That $25,106.04 average annual benefit covers only 41% of typical senior household spending. This gap exists by design, as Social Security is built to replace roughly 40% of your pre-retirement income. The benefit formula is the same for everyone and is meant to be a foundation, not a full budget.

The SSA bases your benefit on an average of your 35 highest-earning years, where earlier wages are adjusted for inflation and run through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. This results in an average yearly shortfall of $36,325.96 that retirees must cover through savings, a pension, or other income.

For those without a pension or deep savings, part-time work may be the only realistic choice to bridge that gap. A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027, adding about $75 a month to the average benefit.

These adjustments are designed to help your buying power keep pace with inflation, though they aren't intended to close the structural gap between your benefits and your total expenses. The average check remains a foundation for your retirement income, but the challenge for most is finding the right way to cover the rest.

Personal savings are the best option to pay for what Social Security won't cover. It doesn't matter whether you keep these funds in a 401(k), an IRA, or even a health savings account (HSA), so long as they're accessible to you when you need them.

If you qualify for a pension through your employer, this may also go a long way toward covering your remaining expenses. Talk to your employer to get a rough idea of how much your pension might offer you in monthly or annual income in retirement.

When personal savings aren't enough, and a pension isn't an option, you might have to explore alternatives, like working in retirement. It might not be ideal, but there are plenty of flexible options these days that enable you to work from home or set your own hours, so you should still have plenty of time to enjoy some of the freedoms of retirement.
