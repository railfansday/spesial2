CVS Has Beaten Earnings Estimates 6 Quarters Straight. Why Is the Stock Under $90?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

CVS's breakup plan introduces new uncertainty for shareholders.

By Jeff Siegel – Nov 24, 2026 at 4:30AM EST

CVS Health (CVS -1.47%) has consistently beaten Wall Street's earnings expectations, raised its financial guidance, and improved profitability across its major businesses. Yet its stock continues trading below $90. In fact, CVS closed at $81.35 on Nov. 18, down 5.2% for the day, despite reporting another strong quarter earlier this month.

The company's latest results included $108.4 billion in revenue, up 5.4% year over year, and adjusted earnings of $2.09 per share, compared with $1.60 a year earlier. Analysts had expected roughly $1.65 per share. Management also raised its full-year adjusted earnings forecast to at least $8.20 per share, up from its previous range of $7.90 to $8.10.

So why isn't Wall Street rewarding CVS for its improving financial performance?

CVS has spent the past several years managing rising healthcare costs, particularly in its Aetna insurance business. When patients require more medical care than insurers anticipated, insurance companies must pay more in claims. Unless premiums rise enough to offset those expenses, profitability suffers. CVS experienced precisely that problem.

In 2024 and part of 2025, its insurance business struggled with elevated medical costs, particularly among Medicare Advantage members. Those pressures contributed to disappointing financial results and forced management to reconsider pricing, benefits, and membership growth. Fortunately, the latest numbers show improvement.

In Q3, Aetna's medical benefit ratio fell to 91.3%, compared with 92.8% a year earlier. That ratio measures how much of an insurer's premium revenue goes toward paying medical claims. A lower number generally means the company retains more money to cover administrative expenses and generate profits. Still, a 91.3% medical benefit ratio leaves relatively little room for unexpected increases in healthcare costs.

On Nov. 4, CVS announced plans to separate into two publicly traded companies. One will include Aetna, CVS Caremark, CVS Specialty, Oak Street Health, and Signify Health. The other will operate approximately 9,000 CVS pharmacies, as well as MinuteClinic and related businesses. Management expects to complete the separation during the first half of 2028.

The announcement surprised investors. As a result, CVS shares plunged 12.5% that day, despite the company's earnings beat and improved guidance.

The decision also marks a departure from the strategy CVS pursued when it acquired Aetna for approximately $70 billion in 2018. At the time, management argued that combining insurance, pharmacies, and healthcare services would create a more efficient healthcare company. Now, CVS believes separating its retail pharmacy operations from its insurance and healthcare services businesses will provide greater flexibility and allow each company to focus on its own priorities.

CVS also faces pressure in its pharmacy and pharmacy benefit management businesses. Pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) negotiate drug prices and manage prescription benefits for insurers and employers.

CVS Caremark is one of the country's largest PBMs, but the industry faces increasing regulatory scrutiny over drug pricing, rebates, and transparency. Changes to reimbursement arrangements could reduce profitability.

Meanwhile, CVS's retail pharmacy business continues to deal with reimbursement pressure and competition. Third-quarter retail pharmacy revenue declined 2% to approximately $35.5 billion, although adjusted operating income increased 10.6% to $1.64 billion. Prescription volume also increased. Those results suggest the business remains financially healthy, but revenue growth isn't guaranteed.

At $81.35 per share, CVS trades for approximately 9.9 times management's projected 2026 adjusted earnings of at least $8.20 per share. That's a relatively modest valuation for a company expected to generate more than $420 billion in annual revenue.

CVS also generated $15.3 billion in operating cash flow during the first nine months of 2026, and management recently increased its full-year operating cash flow guidance to at least $15.5 billion, up from its previous forecast of at least $11.5 billion. The company also expects adjusted earnings of at least $8.65 per share in 2027.

At $81.35, the forward price-to-earnings ratio is approximately 9.4. However, adjusted earnings exclude certain expenses, and strong operating cash flow doesn't automatically translate into free cash flow available to shareholders. CVS still needs to fund capital expenditures, service its debt, and finance the planned separation.

CVS's valuation suggests investors have already priced in considerable uncertainty. The insurance business is recovering, pharmacy profitability is improving, and management continues raising earnings expectations. But the proposed breakup complicates the outlook, while rising medical costs and regulatory changes remain legitimate concerns.

I'd be reluctant to dismiss CVS simply because its stock has struggled, though. If management delivers on its 2027 earnings guidance and successfully separates the businesses, today's valuation could prove attractive.
