Isaiah followed close behind me, tongue unraveling from his mouth. He rubbed himself against my skirt and smeared saliva over it.

Normally, Sam waited by an upturned barrel beneath the pub’s bay windows. He played cards there alone, practiced tricks one of the Colsons had taught him, bothered anyone who passed for a game, tasked to accompany me through the parish at all times. He now toted a small revolver at his hip and had a tendency of spinning it in his fingers whenever a young girl walked by, which was precisely what he was doing at that moment. I heard the revolver clatter on the cobbles and the girls laugh as I slunk out into the foot traffic. The trolley ambled down the street. I’d be aboard it before Sam would notice me.

I lost Isaiah between a horse cart and a woman toting two babies in slings. He barked as I spirited myself away, to the tracks, to the trolley as it passed, my hand on the frame as I moved to step onto it.

As my first foot left the ground, two large hands gripped my waist and pulled me away. I was unceremoniously placed back onto the cobbles. The trolley bell clanged, people alighted and climbed on as it passed, and I was left behind.

“Can’t imagine where you’re headed,” said a chilling, smoky voice. I turned abruptly and found Patrick looking down at me expectantly. “All alone in a no-good town like this.” Before I could respond, he had an arm around me. He escorted me out of the crowd and to a side street, where he could corral me between a brick wall and himself.

I turned, and a growl escaped me. “I’m not a child,” I said between my teeth, stepping closer to him, unintimidated. “I’ll go where I like, whenever it pleases me.”

Patrick rolled his eyes. And Idia be damned, my heart thundered. He wore his cap and coat, just like every other day. Large leather boots laced haphazardly, short brown hair curling at their ends behind his ears, shirt unbuttoned at the throat, revealing a neck spotted in pale freckles, blue eyes impossible to look away from. “Haven’t even been back a full minute, and already she’s testin’ my sanity,” he said to the sky, then placed one hand on the wall by my head and brought his face so close to mine, it was difficult to hold onto my anger. “You agreed to some terms, as I remember it,” he said. My breaths shortened. “You go where you like, so long as you’re guarded.”

“As I remember it, I agreed to those terms under duress,” I hissed back. It was true, in a sense.

“My head between your legs ain’t duress, darlin’.”

I blushed, looked either side of us, but there was too much noise to be overheard.

Patrick grinned. “Nevertheless, you agreed. It ain’t safe to wonder about alone.”

“You’ve been busy most of the time,” I said, meeting his gaze unwillingly. “With all those affairs too dangerous for me.”

He grimaced. “That’s why you have Sam. Or Otto, or Scottie if you prefer.”

At that, the shadows beneath his eyes darkened. “Did they insult you?”

“Patrick,” I said, my tone turning derisive. “Every person here detests me. They don’t need to say it for me to know.”

Patrick straightened. He looked down Main Street, then back at me, deliberating.

I crossed my arms over my chest and waited.

He tilted his head, then held out his hand, and I eyed it like a man had never proffered his hand to me before.

When Patrick did nothing but raise his eyebrows expectantly, I scoffed. “Is that supposed to be my invitation?”

I tightened my crossed arms against my ribcage and lifted my chin.

Patrick smiled down at his boots, then he placed both hands in his pockets and leaned in so his mouth was by my jaw, his breath curling over the shell of my ear, and the gooseflesh of my neck awakened. “Miss Harrow, would you do me the honor of promenadin’ downtown with me?”

His lips barely skimmed over my cheek, and I expelled a breath. “You won’t,” he said assuredly. “You wouldn’t have your gift then.”

He pressed a kiss to the underside of my jaw then stepped away, hiding a smile. “Come on, Scurry girl,” he said. “Give in. Walk with me.”

I shook my head, exasperated, but put my arm in the crook of his and felt suddenly at home. “Not very gentlemanly to bribe a woman.”

“There ain’t a single thing about you that inspires me to be gentlemanly,” he said, pulling me into his side.

We passed Sam, who was leaning against the brickwork of Colson & Sons, a toothpick hanging from his mouth. He pulled a card from behind a young lady’s ear, and her friends giggled and blushed.

“Sammy boy!” Patrick called to him, and the kid had the good sense to flinch, then stand taller. “Cold this mornin’, eh?” Patrick continued. “Not as cold as you’ll be in the boatyard tonight.”

“Best you go home. I hear your mother’s runnin’ you a hot bath for the two of you to share.”

The gaggle of girls went fully red-faced in the presence of Patrick. They scurried away, trapping laughter in their hands.

Sam’s arms rose and fell at his side. “Ah, come on, Patty!” he protested to our receding backs. I was only talkin’ to ’em!”

“Talk to the rats in the boatyard,” Patrick said without turning. “Impress ’em with your cards.”

We advanced down Main Street to the sounds of Sam’s faint cursing. The last remnants of winter blew through the alleys, smelling of charcoal and chimney smoke. Snow drifts had given way to puddles. Kenton Hill was beginning to look like itself again, though the tops of some buildings were still blackened and crumbled. Piles of bricks waited at door fronts, and the lanterns that hung overhead sagged. Some dangled precariously off the eaves. Briggs stood on a giant ladder held by two undersized teenagers; he pulled pliers from his back pocket and restrung them one at a time.

But the men too old to mine still sat at their card tables and shouted to each other. Bedsheets hung between the highest floors of townhouses. The lanes filled and emptied with horses, wagons, and all manner of Crafter. On a hill to the east, the dead had been buried and their graves marked. Kenton Hill churned cautiously on. The war raging across the brink hadn’t penetrated the parish again since the invasion of the Lords’ Army. They were each waiting on the day it would.

Each tunnel into Kenton Hill had been caved in at its closest end. It was the first thing Patrick had asked of me after our return. Every direct line to those surrounding parishes, and the diverging paths beyond them had been sealed. “Not a single comrade between us now,” he had said.

Loyalty had held those tunnels up. Now, there wasn’t a lick of it to be found in the brink.

The Kenton Hill miners came to Colson & Sons in the evening and spoke of hearing shovels on the other side of the walls. Some said it was paranoia. Patrick said it was more likely brigades of angry men, picking at the dirt in some last spasm of futility.

The newsprint continued to flame the ire. The House reported “progress” in their new partnership with the chair of the Miners Union. The members throughout the brink either threw down their weapons or threw up their hands, and as far as the press was concerned, Patrick and I had given the House a seam of terranium so large, it could power generations of Artisans for the foreseeable future, and we’d done so for handsome compensation.

John Colson, the first chairman of the Miners Union—the one each member had followed into the rebellion—was dead.

His son, Patrick, had sold the movement for coin. He was a swank lover. A turncoat.

The Miners Union was done. That was the news of the day.

Every evening, I closed my eyes and pictured Terrence Shop standing overhead, watching us run about our mazes, stumbling into traps, gnawing through the walls.

Patrick saw me shudder and pulled his coat off.

He ignored me. The coat was draped over my shoulders, and he was left in just his trousers, suspenders, his white buttoned shirt, the chill passing through his clothing with little effect. His breath fogged the air. “Gave my coat to a girl when I was fifteen,” he grinned. “Thought it’d win her over for sure.”

“She ran off with it,” Patrick said. “With the coins in the breast pocket.”

I tsked. “And who had you stolen the coins from?”

“Donny,” he said. “Who stole ’em from our dad.”

Patrick shrugged his shoulders. “Weren’t much point. He could always tell when we were lyin’.” His mouth closed in a hard line, his expression closing at the mention of his father.

It wasn’t a topic I’d yet been able to touch. John had betrayed him in the worst ways a father could.

So, Patrick had shot him between the eyes.

There had been no funeral. No remarks other than to explain to his brothers that the man’s name would never be mentioned again.

The subject, I knew, wasn’t to be broached. It was to be accepted: Patrick had killed his father, just as he’d said. There wasn’t anything more to know.

I looked up at him as we strolled, my fingers trailing down his forearm. “You must feel . . . conflicted,” I ventured carefully. “I know you respected him.”

Patrick’s extremities stiffened, something withheld in their channels. His face turned shadowed, his irises crystalized. “Show me a place where conflict ain’t,” was all he said.

I chewed on my tongue for a moment. “What . . . what exactly happened when—”

“Patty!” someone shouted. A man with a thick mustache and a waist apron was weaving his way through pedestrians on the street side. He heaved great breaths as he approached. “There’s fightin’ at the market!”

Patrick’s brow creased and he swore beneath his breath. “Who?”

“That Hal lad and . . .” The man hesitated. “The Charmer boy.”

Patrick closed his eyes. “What are my chances of gettin’ you back to the hotel?”

He muttered something I couldn’t hear. “Keep up then, darlin’.” He wrapped one hand around mine and pulled me along. “No earthquakes today. I mean it.”
