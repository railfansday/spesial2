Jensen Huang's Downfall Shows the Dangers of Investing in Companies like SpaceX and Anthropic

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Some companies give their founders full voting rights.

By Jennifer Saibil – Nov 18, 2026 at 1:05PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Space Exploration Technologies ›

Jensen Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia (NVDA -2.79%) last week, no longer leading the company he built over more than three decades, creating uncertainty for shareholders. Investors love Nvidia for its business strength, which includes unmatched graphics processing units (GPUs) and full compute systems that create high barriers to entry and create huge demand for its products.

As artificial intelligence (AI) expands, Nvidia seems like a sure bet to keep growing and maintain its spot as the engine behind AI growth. However, new reports suggest there were potentially illegal business dealings with China that Huang may have been party to. While Nvidia's board was reportedly weighing its response to disturbing news about Huang, Huang resigned as CEO.

If the allegations were true and if the board acted to remove him from the position, it would have been in their power to do so. But not every company's board has the authority to make such consequential decisions. Consider Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +1.66%) and Anthropic, which will soon go public.

Public companies have boards for exactly this kind of situation. Imagine if Nvidia didn't have a board, and Huang wouldn't be held accountable for his actions.

The purpose of the board is to act as an oversight organization that can govern the executives and act in the interest of shareholders. Nvidia's board enforces its code, which states (these are excerpts): "We ask ourselves what the impact would be if our conduct or action became public or came to the attention of respected colleagues," and "Managers at Nvidia play a special role promoting equitable and ethical decisions."

Jensen Huang owns 3.58% of the voting shares, and at Nvidia each share has the same voting rights. BlackRock (BR -2.26%) has the biggest stake at 7.43%. That no one individual or group owns a large percentage spreads power among more shareholders.

Without this kind of structure, the board may not have had an easy time dealing with the fallout of Huang's actions.

Nvidia's structure is the norm for public companies, but SpaceX and Anthropic are doing it differently, concentrating power at the top.

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk owns 12% of SpaceX's Class-A shares and 94% of its Class-B shares, which gives him 85% of the voting rights. That means it would be very hard to make any corporate or hiring decisions without his support.

Other executives at the company own the remaining Class-B shares, giving them full control over the board and who serves on it.

Anthropic's structure is even more complicated. It has five kinds of stock, including 50.1% voting rights going to an LLC owned equally by seven co-founders, who pick three of the seven directors, a class for its long-term benefit trust, which appoints the other four directors, general Class-A shares, and two classes for employees and partners.

It's not just these two companies who concentrate board power, although they're notable as the two blockbuster initial public offerings this year. Meta Platforms (META -0.52%) and Alphabet (GOOG +1.32%)(GOOGL +1.46%) both also give majority control of the board to their founders, and shareholders' only recourse if there's a problem is to sue the company or sell their shares.

While what happened to Huang is highly unusual, it highlights the risks inherent in owning stock in public companies. Investors looking at companies to invest in need to include the company's voting structure in their investment analysis if they want to make an informed decision about what they invest in. Not doing so could increase the risks around their capital.
