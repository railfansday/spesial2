Voice Robot for Outbound Calls: A Latency Budget from ASR to TTS

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

A voice robot that confirms appointments can be perfectly accurate and still feel broken. The customer says "yes, I'm coming", then hears nothing for two seconds, says "hello?", and the robot starts answering on top of them. The transcript looks fine. The call does not.

On the phone, latency is the product. People tolerate a short pause after they finish speaking, and they start filling the silence themselves somewhere around one second. So the useful question is not "which speech model is best" but "where do the milliseconds go between the end of the customer's phrase and the first sound of our reply". This post breaks that path into stages, gives each stage a budget, and lists the traps that quietly eat it.

Measure one number per turn: time from end of user speech to first audio byte played back. Call it turn latency. Not the ASR processing time, not the LLM time, not the TTS real-time factor. Those are components. The customer experiences only the sum, plus whatever the telephony network adds.

The two biggest and most variable items are endpointing and the dialog decision. That is where most of the engineering goes.

Before you can answer, you need to decide that the person has finished. The naive approach is a fixed silence timeout: if the VAD reports no speech for N milliseconds, the turn is over.

Set N to 1000 ms and every turn starts one full second in the hole before any model even runs. Set it to 200 ms and the robot will interrupt people who pause mid-sentence: "I'd like to... reschedule".

Batch recognition ("record the turn, then send the file") adds the full processing time after the endpoint. Streaming ASR processes audio as it arrives, so when the endpoint fires the hypothesis is already almost done and only finalization remains.

Things to check when choosing or self-hosting an engine:

For a booking confirmation the set of meaningful answers is small: confirm, decline, reschedule, "who is this?", "call me later", and everything else. A classifier or a rule set over the transcript decides in single-digit milliseconds.

An LLM is useful for the long tail: "I'm on the road, can my wife come instead?" But a full generation call easily costs 500-1500 ms to first token, which blows the whole budget. Patterns that keep it under control:

Do not hide a slow LLM behind fillers like "hmm, let me think" on every turn. One filler is human. Three in a minute sound like a stalling machine.

A TTS engine that synthesizes a 3-second phrase in 300 ms looks fast, but if it returns audio only after the whole phrase is ready, you wait the full 300 ms. Streaming TTS that emits the first 100-200 ms of audio early lets playback start while the rest is still being generated.

Even with a good budget, people interrupt. "Your appointment at the clinic tomorrow at..." "Yes, yes, I know." The robot must stop speaking within roughly 200-300 ms, or it sounds like a recorded message.

The SIP trunk and mobile network add their own delay, often 100-250 ms round trip, plus the jitter buffer on your media server. Two settings matter:

Log timestamps per turn, in one clock domain, on the media server:

user_speech_end comes from the VAD, so it is an estimate, but it is consistent, which is what you need for trends. From this you get the per-stage breakdown, p50 and p90 per stage, and a direct view of which stage regressed after a deploy or a model update.

Also record the business outcome per call: confirmed, declined, rescheduled, no answer, escalated. A latency improvement that does not move the confirmation rate or reduce talk-overs probably was not the bottleneck.

Short, predictable scenarios like booking confirmations, visit reminders and one-question surveys are where these budgets are easiest to hit, because most replies can be scripted and cached. The usual path is to start with one scenario, listen to the test call recordings, tune pauses and wording, and only then scale the volume.
