25 Years Ago, The Lord of the Rings Became a Horror Movie for One Perfect Scene

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Hannah covers horror, sci-fi, and games at Collider, where she writes news and features, reviews new releases, and interviews the people who make them. She’s helping build out Collider’s gaming coverage, with Pokémon as one of her specialties. If she isn’t writing about a monster, she’s probably watching one on a tape from her VHS collection.

The skeleton is already down the well when the chain starts to go, and the worst part is realizing how much of it is still up there. In The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring, Pippin (Billy Boyd) has plenty of time to stand there and think about what he’s done. So does everybody watching. A skeleton falling down a well is a pretty good gag on its own. Let the chain drag the noise out long enough, and suddenly you’re wondering how far sound travels through a mountain.

It helps that Peter Jackson has spent the movie making the Hobbits’ screwups funny. Pippin asking about second breakfast is part of what makes him good company, and a wizard telling him off for touching something he shouldn’t feels like a scene you already know how to watch. There’s even a little relief when Gandalf (Ian McKellen) starts yelling. If he has time to call Pippin an idiot, maybe getting yelled at is the worst thing that’s about to happen to him. Then the drums start in Balin’s tomb, and the Fellowship gets to hear a sound that, until a few seconds ago, belonged to the dead. That’s the moment The Fellowship of the Ring becomes a horror movie. Gandalf has just read what happened to the last people who tried to hold this room, and the drums are the first indication that the Fellowship could die the exact same way. Suddenly, knowing the history of Moria makes being there worse.

Gimli (John Rhys-Davies) is the reason the tomb feels like a loss before it feels like a trap. He’s been talking about the welcome his cousin will give them, which is the kind of promise that gets you through a miserable trip. The shaft of daylight in that chamber makes it look like they’ve finally found somewhere they can stop. It falls right on Balin’s grave. There’s something especially cruel about giving Gimli enough light to see exactly what he came all that way to find.

The book does something nastier with that same sense of recognition. The Dwarves who held the chamber were thinking about the same things the Fellowship has to think about now. Which halls are still safe? How long will the door hold? Where can everybody go if it gives? “We cannot get out” lands so harshly because the Watcher in the Water has already brought down the entrance behind them. Gandalf is reading somebody else’s last words in a room where they could just as easily belong to him.

That’s also why the scene can get so much out of a drumbeat. A growl would tell the Fellowship there’s something down there. Drums tell them there are enough of whatever’s down there to organize. By the time Boromir (Sean Bean) announces that they have a cave troll, the joke buys about a second of relief. He’s just looked through the door the Dwarves once had to defend, and his best idea is to shut it again.

Jackson spends the fight at the tomb teaching the audience how hard it is to keep the Orcs out of a room. When they surround the Fellowship in the pillared hall, they’ve finally got what they wanted. Then a roar carries through the stone, the hall starts to glow, and the Orcs give up a fight they’re about to win. After watching them bring a cave troll to a nine-person fight, there’s something deeply upsetting about finding out what they consider a bad idea.

Gandalf’s reaction makes it worse. In the dark earlier, he could sit down with Frodo (Elijah Wood) and make the whole problem of being lost in Moria feel manageable. When he tells Aragorn (Viggo Mortensen) that swords are useless here, it takes away the one thing the Fellowship could still count on after the tomb. They knew how to fight their way through a room, but now the person who knows the most about what’s coming is telling them to run. The bridge gives Gandalf a chance to do the thing a fantasy hero is supposed to do. He stands his ground, breaks the stone beneath the Balrog, and sends it down into the dark. It’s a win for just long enough that the audience can start thinking about getting out of Moria again. Then the whip comes back up. The same darkness that hid the Balrog on its way in hides the last thing it does to Gandalf, right up until it catches him by the ankle.

The series hails from ‘Star Trek: Picard’ and ‘VisionQuest’ showrunner Terry Matalas.

The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power gets to make the Balrog scary from the other side of that history. When King Durin III (Peter Mullan) breaks into the mithril cavern in the Season 2 finale, there’s a whole city above him that the audience has actually spent time in. The horror of the scene is how quickly Durin understands what he’s put at risk. His son gets his father back in the moment Durin takes off the ring, and there’s barely time to register that before he charges the creature.

With Season 3 on Prime Video and Simon Pegg voicing the Balrog, the creature gets to tell us something about itself. 25 years earlier, however, Jackson had a nastier idea: he let the last Dwarf in Balin’s tomb tell us what it sounded like to die down there, then made the Fellowship listen for the same sound.
