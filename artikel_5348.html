Black Friday will put AI shopping agents to the test. Consumers aren’t buying it yet

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Earlier this fall Visa instructed 22 different personal AI agents to purchase three everyday items—a TV, a laptop, and a hair appliance—to see how they’d do. It was not a resounding success. Only 57% of the 15 shopping trips each agent made led to a purchase that matched the shopper’s needs or even resulted in a suggestion of something suitable.

Many agents misunderstood consumers’ preferences or didn’t know what questions to ask, such as, “Is this TV game-compatible?” leading to many wrong purchases.

The biggest names in retail have spent the year wiring their stores and websites for AI assistants, betting shoppers will soon rely on bots for their buying. Walmart made a big show of supporting agentic commerce early this year when it announced a partnership with Google and its Gemini artificial intelligence assistant to make it easier for shoppers to discover and buy items from the world’s biggest retailer. Target consumers using ChatGPT can now tag the assistant and describe an occasion they are shopping for and get back product ideas they can buy in a single checkout. Home Depot in August expanded its AI-powered shopping assistant, Magic Apron, to all of its stores after an initial limited trial. And earlier this month, OpenAI unveiled its “Wallet” that connects with American Express, Visa and Mastercard to allow agents to pay and close out a transaction based on the limits a human customer sets.

But the Visa simulation illustrates the progress agentic commerce has made and how far it still has to go. This Black Friday-to-Cyber Monday weekend gives AI shopping agents their biggest stage yet, but the holiday season won’t belong to them. Instead, the 2026 holiday shopping season will be more dress rehearsal than debut. With the National Retail Federation forecasting more than $1 trillion in total holiday sales, retailers will be watching whether shoppers rely on AI just for gift suggestions—or whether they let bots do the buying.

Agentic commerce is already showing promise for retailers who want to get ready for what they see as the technology’s inevitable rise. A report by consulting firm Kearney noted that Amazon’s AI shopping assistant supported $12 billion in incremental sales last year, or 2% of total business, and Walmart earlier in 2026 reported that users of its Sparky assistant spent 35% more per order than the average customer. Emarketer research found that in the first quarter of 2026, AI-referred traffic to U.S. retail sites rose 393% on a year-over-year basis from an admittedly small base. A Bain & Co. analysis this month of Similarweb data found that AI assistants account for less than 1% of retailers’ web traffic.

Still, retail executives aren’t waiting for these teeny numbers to grow before they start placing bets.

Target CEO Michael Fiddelke told Wall Street analysts last week that agentic shopping was still behind only a small amount of sales, but that “it’s important for us to be at the forefront of any changes to the consumer purchase funnel.” Meanwhile, last month, Kohl’s CEO Michael Bender said that agentic commerce was still small but drew shoppers who spent more than average, and crucially he said he saw big opportunities for “purchase confidence” to increase over time.

“We have an opportunity to participate in a larger pie. And our only question is: Which are going to be the best tools to partner with and how do we make sure it doesn’t add more friction?” said Macy’s CEO Tony Spring at the Fortune Innovation Forum in Detroit earlier this month.

Consumers are more cautious than retail CEOs. They are still getting used to the technology and seeing what it is capable of, and, importantly, what they can count on it for.

A Morning Consult poll for Visa last month found that 54% of adults had already used AI for at least one shopping task, up from 47% last year. But only 23% of U.S. consumers trust generative AI to handle payment transactions on their behalf.

That finding harkens back to the hesitancy consumers first exhibited towards e-commerce and mobile commerce. That caution faded with experience and time and as mobile payment platforms proved they were reliable. Now most consumers don’t think twice about paying for things online.

For now, consumers are most comfortable using AI for search, and that too poses an opportunity.

“Generative AI, more than agentic commerce, will play a significant role in this year’s holiday shopping,” says Anna Marrs, group president for global Merchant & Network Services at American Express. “Consumers are using large language model tools to do a better job searching for what they want.”

The limitations of retailers’ sites themselves offer an opening for AI, says Forrester Research principal analyst Emily Pfeiffer. “Retailers’ websites are terrible at comparison shopping and surfacing things you haven’t thought of before,” she said. That is especially crucial for things like gift ideas or areas where a customer is less knowledgeable. “We’re more open to suggestions,” Pfeiffer said.

Crate & Barrel Holdings CEO Janet Hayes says her customers are most willing to engage with agentic commerce when they’re building wedding registries. “When it’s complex and hard and they’ve never done it, they want suggestions,” she said. “The more they ask in there, the more it informs how to write better copy and give better content.”

The option to search with natural language (“a hard-shell suitcase that meets Delta Air Lines’ carry-on size requirements and costs between $100 and $200”), versus clunky key terms (“hardshell+suitcase+small”), is also an advantage of AI. “[That] will make a huge difference,” says Pfeiffer. Her research found that 20% of shoppers will flat out refuse to shop on a website that can’t handle natural language.

Black Friday and the holiday season may also not be the right use case for agentic shopping. An American Express survey found that 52% of U.S. consumers are fine allowing an AI agent to purchase low-cost items like household essentials and groceries, but only 15% feel that way for big-ticket items, like electronics and appliances. And a poll this month by marketing platform provider Zeta found that only 9% of respondents would let AI purchase gifts without personally reviewing them first.

Moving consumers’ AI use from searching and browsing to full-fledged buying requires giving them peace of mind.

“Consumers need to know what’s going to happen if something goes wrong. They’re going to feel good if they know they’re going to be taken care of if an agent makes a mistake,” says Kearney partner Katherine Black, who leads the firm’s food, drug and mass market retail practice. (Walmart said in October that its returns policy applies even when an AI messes up. “Customers are in control,” Walmart wrote in a blog post.)

Retailers are looking for reassurance too. They seem to love the added traffic to their sites from AI agents but also fear loss of control of the customer experience and of customer data, which supports marketing and loyalty programs. In September, Amazon blocked Meta’s Muse agent from shopping on Amazon. (Walmart is taking a different tack: last month it rolled out infrastructure to enable third-party agentic commerce by creating a profile for agents that links to a shopper’s Walmart account.)

The major credit card issuers have all unveiled technology this year that allows verified AI shopping agents to make transactions with all the protections consumers are used to. Amex has announced that it plans to launch its Amex Agent Purchase Protection offer to protect eligible customers from charges when a registered AI agent makes a purchase inconsistent with the customer’s authenticated instructions.

If the 2026 holiday season experiment with agentic shopping is a success, then next year’s may well prove to be the tipping point for the tech. “It’s really going to grow at the speed of trust,” says Marrs.

Phil Wahba is a senior writer at Fortune primarily focused on leadership coverage, with a prior focus on retail.
