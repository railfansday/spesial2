Inside Stockholm’s Gasometer, Europe’s Most Ambitious New Music Venue: ‘This Will Create Waves Far Beyond Here’

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The venue will host its opening performances in March 2027.

On a frigid November morning, Juliene Planté, head of music at Miramis, and the team lead a preview tour of Gasometer, a new venue in Stockholm that’s slated to open in March 2027. Sections of the site are finished, while in others, we sidle between boxes and pallets of wood, and peer over red tape while the site undergoes its legal assessments.

Inside the venue, the scale of the venture becomes clear; our guide says that she’s starstruck every day when she comes into the space. We enter by the rear stage door, facing the pristine, pillarless seated auditorium. It’s easy to see why she feels that way. “Gasometer is not just for Stockholm, or Sweden or the Nordic countries, but for the whole of Europe,” says Planté.

The space features two venues: a main auditorium and a second, more intimate venue built into the Gasometer’s roof meant primarily for intimate gatherings,banquets and dinners. Miramis estimates that the investment into the project so far has totaled €100 million ($109 million).

The venue’s first main space, called Theatre, features three levels of stalls seating and a standing area which is customizable depending on the production. In a theatre format, the room accommodates 1,750 guests and up to 2,300 with floor standing.

Planté speaks proudly of the significance of this venture, which was undertaken by Miramis, the entertainment and hospitality group that already operates the city’s popular Cirkus venue in the Djurgårdsslätten district. Miramis is a sister company to Pophouse Entertainment, a private entertainment company, co-founded by ABBA’s Björn Ulvaeus, which launched the wildly successful ABBA Voyage show in London.

Gasometer will arrive at a time when some of the world’s leading acts are emerging from a variety of countries in Europe, such as RAYE (U.K.), Rosalía (Spain) and Zara Larsson (Sweden). The venue aims to be a standard bearer for creative innovation in the European venue scene, combining the programming diversity of London’s Southbank Centre with the technological advances of Manchester’s Co-op Live and Aviva Studios.

The venue is an ambitious and dazzling undertaking. It repurposes one of two former gasworks in the city’s northern district, and is part of a wider development of the area bringing living and work spaces to Stockholm in an area that was first built in 1899 to supply the city’s growing need for energy, and was eventually decommissioned a century later in 2011.

For the first five years after opening, Gasometer will be home to the Royal Swedish Opera as the city’s opera house is renovated. That has pushed the building’s developers. A specialist stage floor is required to allow the correct level of bounce for ballet performances, and its sound design has been customized to allow for surround sound, with operators enlisting French audio giant L-Acoustics to shape the venue’s sonic profile.

The building’s original features have been meticulously reproduced or preserved, including the brickwork that surrounds the internal venue. An exact replica of its original roof, which was first designed in 1899, features a latticework of wood and steel and reaches 17 meters above the ground.

The Dome is similarly ambitious. Miramis’ focus on hospitality led them to find a way to integrate gastronomy into the building’s plan. The top floor will house a kitchen  that will serve up to 250 people in a restaurant setting and up to 1,000 guests depending on the formation. Swedish chef Stefan Eriksson will shape Gasometer’s gastronomy profile and offerings using local produce. The customizable space can be booked for artist performances, and The Dome and Theatre can run performances concurrently. The group’s garbage room is just 15 square feet, a deliberate decision to ensure the venue operates as efficiently and with as little waste as possible.

It’s easy to see why Planté — formerly executive producer and head of cultural & special events at London’s KOKO — was attracted to the role. The long-term vision and license allows him to plan “5 to 10 years in the future which is a fantastic, unique opportunity.” He says that the independence, away from big groups such as Live Nation or AEGis  “a golden opportunity”  and “so exciting as a programmer, as a producer, to have that creative freedom.”

The venue’s opening season will kick off on March 17 with ALEPH, a production created by composer Jacob Mühlrad and artist and creative director Alexander Wessely, whose work includes staging for The Weeknd and Rihanna. Across the opening weekend, FKA twigs, Patti Smith and performance artist Marina Abramović, as well as a special score composed and arranged by Mühlrad, and the choreography of Sir Wayne McGregor of the U.K.’s Royal Ballet will perform.

“I want people to feel small when they step into the gasholder, as they would in a church,” Wessely says of the launch production “In ALEPH, music, choreography, light and sculpture surround the audience from every direction. Bringing these artists together in a single work makes it a ritual rather than a traditional concert.”

In Planté’s mind, Gasometer’s programming and building is incomparable to anywhere else in the world. “This is not simply a venue opening. It is a statement of what live performance can become,” he proudly says. For the venue’s first five years, the show will run annually, with new acts expected to perform each time. Alongside the Royal Swedish Opera’s regular performances, Planté will be in charge of 10 nights of programming a year, alongside an additional 10 nights of co-production with the Opera. “For each of those nights, I really want to make them count. I want to programme historic moments,” he says.

He intends to ensure all fans who walk through the venue’s doors remember the performance they witnessed for a long time to come. “I would love to develop a unique format to invite artists to perform with the backing of an orchestra, which is often their goal.” He points to performances by Portishead, Little Simz and Loyle Carner, where having a backing orchestra served to elevate their work.  He says he considers Nick Cave as a dream booking, and he hopes some day artists will release live performances of their albums recorded at Gasometer. While the venue will not be a standard stop for touring artists,  it will be a place for those who want to push their creativity and try something new.

It adds up to a bold new frontier for Europe’s music scene. Come March, Plante says he knows that the Gasometer’s impact will be impossible to ignore: “This will have some waves far beyond here, for sure.”
