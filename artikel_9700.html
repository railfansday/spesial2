When Everyone Looks Qualified, Who Actually Is?

We've spent decades building systems that recognize achievement. AI is forcing us to reconsider how we recognize capability.

Spend enough time around startups and you'll recognize the pattern.

An ambitious founder. A compelling pitch deck. A product roadmap promising to transform an industry.

Investors get interested. People start talking. Talented employees leave secure jobs to join something that feels like the next big thing.

And somewhere along the way, the ability to tell a convincing story starts getting confused with the ability to deliver one.

Strategy meetings fill with ambitious promises. Deadlines slip. The people doing the difficult work begin losing confidence.

Eventually, the distance between the pitch and the product becomes impossible to ignore.

By then, good people have left, teams have burned out, and trust has eroded.

There's a particular kind of bullshit that thrives in environments where confidence is mistaken for competence.

Persuasion, of course, is an essential part of entrepreneurship. Great founders attract talent, capital, and belief long before their vision becomes reality. Some of the world's most successful companies began with ideas that seemed almost impossible.

The trouble begins when the ability to sell the vision outpaces the ability to execute it, and nobody recognizes the difference until the consequences arrive.

AI is making that distinction even harder.

In April 2026, recruitment firm Robert Half reported something revealing about Australian hiring managers.

While 97% expected new employees to have some proficiency with AI and automation, 88% were struggling to find candidates with the right AI and automation skills. More than a third said AI-generated résumés were making applicants harder to evaluate.

There's an uncomfortable contradiction here.

Employers increasingly expect people to work with AI, while the same technology is making it harder to determine what those people can actually do.

A candidate can arrive with an impressive résumé, a sophisticated portfolio, a working prototype, and a beautifully articulated strategy.

Some will have exercised extraordinary judgment in creating that work. Others may understand very little of what they're presenting.

And from the finished product alone, distinguishing between them is becoming increasingly difficult.

We've encountered versions of this problem in education for decades.

Two students earn the same grade. One has developed deep understanding through experimentation, feedback, and revision. The other has learned how to satisfy the assessment.

The learning behind it may be very different.

Now imagine those students graduating, entering the workforce, and eventually founding or leading organizations.

A degree opens a door. A résumé earns an interview. A portfolio demonstrates experience.

But what happens when the signals we've traditionally trusted become less revealing about the capabilities people have actually developed?

Consider a startup founder evaluating a developer with an impressive portfolio.

Clean interfaces. Working demonstrations. A GitHub profile filled with activity.

The founder could ask which AI tools the candidate uses, how quickly they built the applications, or what frameworks they know.

But imagine asking them to show the part that almost failed.

The architectural decision they got wrong.

The solution AI confidently recommended that they eventually rejected.

What made them question the original approach? What evidence changed their mind? What would they do differently today?

That conversation might reveal more about their capabilities than the finished portfolio ever could.

The cost of mistaking appearance for ability remains extraordinarily high.

There's a phrase from Web3 that feels especially relevant here. Proof of Work.

In blockchain, it refers to a mechanism for validating computational work and helping secure a network. I'm borrowing the phrase as a metaphor for something more human.

What if we became better at recognizing the thinking behind what people produce?

Consider the founder who abandoned a promising pitch after discovering that customers didn't actually need the product.

Or the designer who rejected their most visually impressive prototype because user testing exposed a fundamental flaw.

Or the graduate who challenged an AI-generated analysis, discovered that its assumptions were wrong, and rebuilt the argument from the evidence.

There's something revealing about the work people decide against doing.

The ideas they abandon. The shortcuts they refuse. The assumptions they reconsider.

Those decisions can reveal judgment, adaptability, and intellectual honesty.

This is where portfolios, meaningful data, and visible learning become valuable.

Imagine students graduating with evidence of projects, demonstrated skills, feedback, and how their thinking developed over time.

Founders presenting validated experiments and customer evidence alongside projections.

Employees being recognized for the problems they've solved, the independence they've developed, and the contributions they've made.

These records could tell richer stories about human capability.

Of course, AI can also manufacture convincing portfolios, reflections, and accounts of someone's process. Credible evidence still requires verification, authentic demonstrations, and the ability to apply what we've learned.

There may be a useful role here for portable digital credentials, including Web3 approaches that help establish where evidence originated and who verified it.

But the meaning of that evidence will always require judgment.

Every system of recognition shapes behavior.

Schools reward grades. Companies reward performance. Startups reward growth, momentum, and the ability to attract belief.

The questions someone asked. The assumptions they challenged. The mistakes they recognized. The ideas they had the judgment to abandon.

And imagine what might change if we became better at recognizing those moments.

A school that helps students see how their thinking has developed.

A founder who values the candidate who can explain a failed project as thoughtfully as a successful one.

An organization that recognizes the employee who quietly prevents a costly mistake.

An investor who looks beyond the pitch to understand the evidence and judgment behind it.

These are choices about what we value, what we measure, and what we encourage people to become.

They also connect to the central idea behind Designing for AGENCY: as intelligent machines become more capable, human judgment, independence, and responsibility become increasingly important.

Perhaps those are the qualities our systems should become better at recognizing.

Because when everyone looks qualified, the most interesting question may be:

Can you show me the thinking behind what you've built?
