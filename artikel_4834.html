The Psychological Origins of Fear-Based Advertising

Updated November 2, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

When I was an undergraduate at Johns Hopkins University, I heard the story about how John B. Watson, a psychologist who is considered the father of the classical conditioning strand of American behaviorism (a term that he coined), was fired from a tenure-track position for having a sexual relationship with his 21-year-old graduate assistant, Rosalie Rayner. An earlier example of a very esteemed scholar who was fired by Hopkins for similar prurient reasons (in both cases they later married their girlfriends after becoming divorced from their first wives) involved the great philosopher, mathematician, and logician Charles Sanders Peirce. Neither man ever attained another academic post. While Watson went on to have great success outside of academia, Peirce continued as an independent scholar who lived in abject poverty, relying on financial support from many admirers, most notably his former JHU doctoral student William James, considered the father of American psychology and the co-developer, with Peirce, of the philosophical school of pragmatism.

The 1920 experiment for which Watson, assisted by Rayner, became famous (a year before they fired him, Hopkins doubled his salary in an attempt to keep him from leaving) involved an infant later known as “Little Albert.” The experiment exposed Albert, at age nine months, to various neutral stimuli–including a white rat, monkeys, and dogs–thus demonstrating his absence of fear. At age 11 months, every time that Albert reached for the white rat, a scary, loud noise (Watson striking a steel bar with a hammer behind the child’s head) occurred. The result was that the infant developed a fear response (distress and crying) to the rat even in the absence of the noise. This fear response generalized to other white objects, including cotton wool, a fur coat, and a Santa Claus mask with a white beard. Watson planned to extinguish the fear through exposure, but the experiment (today considered profoundly unethical) was prematurely ended by the child’s mother. Their scientific claim was that classical conditioning could be used to establish emotional responses, especially fear, in children.

In 1928, Watson and Rayner (who went on to have two children together) co-authored a child-rearing book, “Psychological Care of Infant and Child” in which they used the results of their experiment to argue (based on zero evidence) that a child’s personality is formed entirely by environmental forces with no role for innate nature or instincts. The book’s most controversial–and, to me, horrifying–advice for parents was that they should not hug, kiss, or show affection to their children, in order to toughen them up for the grim world they would face as adults. Interestingly, Watson and Rayner had two sons, both raised according to their no-love method. As adults, both attempted suicide, one of them successfully. (A third adult child, a daughter from Watson’s first marriage, also attempted suicide, and blamed it, whether fairly or not, on her father).

After being forced out of academia, Watson was hired by the New York-based advertising company J. Walter Thompson. He thrived there, eventually becoming a vice-president of the firm. The basis of Watson’s success as an ad man was developing the idea, based on his view of behaviorism, that the key to persuading consumers to buy a particular product was conditioning them to the idea that by not buying it they were exposing themselves or their loved ones to a feared physical or social danger. In the physical realm, Watson persuaded consumers to buy Scott’s toilet paper by showing doctors grimly surrounding a patient whose medical condition was attributed to wiping their bum with “harsh toilet tissue.” Another ad campaign, on behalf of the Johnson & Johnson company, emphasized to mothers that they were exposing their children to dread diseases by not purchasing the company’s hygiene products. But fear was also used as the basis for Watson’s ads in the social domain, with one example involving buying a brand of toothpaste not to promote good dental health but to avoid the social rejection that supposedly flows from bad breath and discolored teeth.

Watson thus pioneered in America the use of psychological concepts as the basis for advertising campaigns, grounding it on the conditioning of emotional responses, particularly fear. He was followed in the related field of public relations by Edward Bernays, a double nephew of Sigmund Freud (his mother was Freud’s sister and his father was the brother of Freud’s wife), and who based his ideas of propaganda–the title of his most famous book–not on behaviorism but, very loosely, on psychoanalytic theory. Bernays, whose parents moved from Austria to the US when he was an infant, often visited his uncle (who gifted him Cuban cigars, perhaps in thanks for getting one of his books published in America) and was a pioneer in the field of public relations. Although not himself trained as a psychoanalyst, Bernays claimed to have developed a public relations theory based on his famous uncle’s notion that people’s actions are controlled less by conscious ideas than by unconscious desires and fears.

Bernays, like Watson, was successful in promoting commercial products, with his biggest success, influenced by advice from Freud’s translator A.A. Brill, occurring on behalf of the American Tobacco Company. The purpose of that campaign was to persuade women to overcome the taboo against female smoking by portraying Lucky Strike cigarettes as phallic symbols empowering women to escape from male oppression. Bernays’ efforts, however, were mainly in the public sector, and involved such activities as drumming up support for America’s entry into WWI. Years later, when working for the United Fruit Company, Bernays promoted the CIA-led overthrow of the left-leaning President of Guatemala, Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán. In that campaign, Bernays took a page from the John B. Watson playbook by portraying Arbenz as a communist who posed a threat to the U.S. and its people. Bernays later followed the same Red-Scare playbook in the early days of the Vietnam War, but later backed off from the effort when that war became unwinnable, both in the field and in the realm of public opinion.

Bernays justified his efforts at propaganda (a term he stopped using when it began to take on negative connotations) by claiming that it was essential in a democracy for its leaders to have a way to keep the populace from spiraling out of control. Although Watson did not address the role of advertising in a democracy, he shared with Bernays the idea that people’s actions are governed mainly by emotion, and that psychology provided emotion-based mechanisms of social control. Neither Watson nor Bernays showed any concern about the need to base their arguments on actual truth. Ominous implications for the future of democracy in the U.S. and other countries flow from the ubiquity of political advertising based on fear-mongering that in many cases is based on completely made-up facts. It is in the political arena, more than in his dangerous advice to parents, that the lessons from John B. Watson’s Little Albert experiment have come to have profound influence on public life.
