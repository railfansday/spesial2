Three charged with using AI to impersonate CEOs and steal about $129 million

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Prosecutors say Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit mimicked executives and lawyers; extradition requests are pending

Federal prosecutors in New York unsealed an indictment charging Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit with deploying artificial intelligence to pose as company leaders and lawyers in a scheme that prosecutors say drained roughly $129 million from businesses in the U.S. and overseas.

The U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York refers to the technology as "super intelligence." Soall was arrested in Spain on June 17, Hazan in Spain on Aug. 5 and Sheetrit in the United Kingdom on Sept. 7, the office said. Extradition requests for all three are pending.

Each defendant is charged with seven counts, including two counts of aggravated identity theft and two of money laundering, along with one count each of wire fraud, wire fraud conspiracy and money laundering conspiracy. The maximum sentence is 20 years on each count except aggravated identity theft, which carries two years. The charges are accusations, and the defendants are presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

According to the indictment, the defendants and co-conspirators used the technology to mimic the voices or images of CEOs. They claimed to need assistance with a secret financial deal and sent employees to attorneys at big multinational firms, who were impostors as well. One of those impostors then instructed the employee to send a wire payment to an account the conspiracy controlled. The scheme ran from July 2024 to February 2026, according to NBC News.

The indictment lists victims including a Missouri hospital system, which lost $4,829,580, and a Rhode Island bank, which lost $2,684,580. A California hospital system lost $2,968,497, a U.K. financial technology company $11,578,474, a Massachusetts mutual fund company $4,639,918 and a Florida pet food company $5,242,747. Money went to accounts in the United States, Spain, Portugal, Greece, the United Arab Emirates, Singapore, Austria, Slovakia and China. The defendants converted it into cryptocurrency to hide its source and split it among themselves, according to the indictment.

Soall, 41, of Marbella, Spain, and the U.K., posed chiefly as British lawyers and carried a script he had written out by hand under the title "Lawyer Call." Hazan, 54, of Israel, handled the money laundering and bragged of having cleaned more than $400 million over a three-year span, the indictment says. Hazan was convicted in the Southern District of New York in 2008 of conspiracy to commit bank fraud. Sheetrit, 42, of Israel and the United Arab Emirates, is alleged to have laundered money as well.

"As alleged, Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan, and Guy Sheetrit abused groundbreaking super intelligence technology to impersonate real people and deceive corporate executives into sending them and their co-conspirators approximately $129 million," U.S. Attorney Jamie McDonald said in a statement.

FBI Assistant Director in Charge James C. Barnacle Jr. described the case as an example of sophisticated technology being turned to large-scale fraud. “The FBI remains committed to rapidly adapting our capabilities to identify, investigate, and disrupt those who seek to harm New Yorkers and businesses operating in our communities,” Barnacle said in a statement, according to The Associated Press.

Deepfake fraud has drained corporate accounts before. Impostors posing as senior executives on a live video call took roughly $25 million from the British engineering firm Arup in 2024. Cyber-enabled fraud has also overtaken ransomware as the top concern of corporate leaders, a shift that surfaced in January 2026.
