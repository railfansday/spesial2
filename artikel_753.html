In Choosing Robert Pattinson, Bond Producers Have Taken the Safe Bet — But Is It the Right One? (Opinion)

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

The star of 'The Drama' and 'The Odyssey' caps off a massive year by winning the highest-profile casting contest in ages, but it's hard not to wonder if he really needs this.

So much for the underdog. After the race to be the next James Bond was reported to be down to three on-the-rise Brits — Jack Lowden, Josh O’Connor and Callum Turner — Robert Pattinson crashed the conversation late in proceedings, hugely pulling focus in a matter of days.

Why wouldn’t he? Suddenly, a competition of next-big-things had a bona fide already-big-thing in the mix. 40 years old and known to multiplex audiences for almost half that lifetime, the Londoner is a household name who, even by his established standards, has been enjoying a banner year at the movies: sharing in the blockbuster glory of Christopher Nolan’s “The Odyssey” this summer, sandwiched by inspired star turns in two offbeat A24 projects, “The Drama” and “Primetime,” that both impressively overperformed at the box office. All that, and he’s still got “Dune: Part Three” coming up next month. Adding James Bond to his roster feels almost like gilding the lily, which is one reason I was skeptical that he’d ultimately be picked: Wasn’t the announcement of Pattinson (along with Nicholas Hoult) as a contender just a glittery distraction to juice up public interest in a lower-profile selection process?

Shows what I know. In picking Pattinson as the next 007, the 64-year-old movie franchise is heading into unprecedented territory: Bond has traditionally been a star-making role, not one for a readymade movie star, and that has worked out more often than not. (George Lazenby, it simply wasn’t meant to be.) Sean Connery had played just one leading role before he was cast in 1962’s “Dr. No”; prior to donning the tux, Roger Moore and Pierce Brosnan had both found their signature roles on TV. Timothy Dalton, a reasonably seasoned and well-regarded actor when cast as Bond, never quite made the leap to stardom via the role; Daniel Craig, on the other hand, very much did.

Lowden, Turner and O’Connor all made perfect sense given this history, while Pattinson, already known to billions for playing Edward Cullen and Batman, brings associative celebrity baggage that Bond has never had to contend with before. Is that a problem? Not necessarily. Director Denis Villeneuve, a high-end, blue-chip blockbuster artist with a distinct stylistic signature, isn’t the typical choice of Bond helmer either — so if he’s going in his own prestigious direction with the series, it makes sense for him to take an equivalently A-list leading man (and one with whom he has an existing relationship, through “Dune: Part Three”) with him.

And yet there’s something about the choice, on paper at least, that doesn’t sit right with me. It seems a little too neat, too convenient, even a bit too risk-averse — the proven safe bet taken over the chance to mint a brand-new megastar. I have little doubt that Pattinson, a reliably terrific actor, will make a fine Bond: tall and elegant and angularly handsome, he certainly looks the part and he can project the balance of charm and chill needed to play a character who, in any incarnation, remains a suave killing machine.

Is he a little too good for the part, though? Perhaps. Pattinson’s reputation as a movie star rests on playing two darkly brooding mainstream heroes; his reputation as an actor, however, has been considerably enriched by his predilection for playing, frankly, losers and weirdos: a hapless small-time criminal in the Safdie Brothers’ “Good Time”; a deranged, gull-mauling lighthouse keeper in “The Lighthouse”; a sniveling, pathetic read of Chris Hansen in “Primetime”; or whatever the hell he’s doing, multiple times over, in Bong Joon-ho’s loopy sci-fi “Mickey 17.” Even his blockbuster assignments are more interesting when he lets his inner freak out to play: His big, heightened, leeringly villainous turn in “The Odyssey” is off on its own beam entirely.

I don’t know that James Bond is really the role to mine Pattinson’s most compelling eccentricities as an actor: The very point of the character, across 25 films, has been that he’s a well-dressed, tightly controlled cipher who has a sharp way with a one-liner. Perhaps Villeneuve and his chosen star have other ideas, but it’s hard to imagine the character straying too far from a failsafe template. One hopes instead that Pattinson is negotiating himself enough time between 007 shoots (and at least one more “The Batman” entry still on the docket) to do the odd auteur projects he clearly loves — and that adventurously chosen collaborators like Claire Denis (“High Life”) and Lynne Ramsay (“Die My Love”) can only benefit from his cachet.

In the meantime, spare a thought for the less famous guys who, for a moment there, thought they had a shot at being cinema’s longest-running action man. They’ll all be fine — I confess to some relief in particular that O’Connor, a superb and unusual hybrid of character actor and leading man, may have more time for the intrepid arthouse projects in which he thrives — and will doubtless each get some manner of boost for having been so prominently on the shortlist. Sometimes, however, the winner really does take it all in this business: For Pattinson, snagging the most in-demand role going just feels like a victory lap.
