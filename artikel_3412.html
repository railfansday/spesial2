Reality Is Even Darker Than Jonathan Nolan & J.J. Abrams' 103-Episode Sci-Fi Masterpiece Predicted

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Even though Jonathan Nolan and JJ Abrams’ underrated sci-fi series from the 2010s explored a dystopian vision of a surveillance state, reality ended up outdoing this CBS hit. When Firefly was canceled after only 14 episodes in 2002, there is no way that the show’s creators could have known just how influential the short-lived series would be. Without Firefly, viewers might never have gotten SyFy’s Dark Matter, Killjoys, or Bass X Machina, not to mention the show’s influence on the Guardians of the Galaxy movies.

Similarly, author Phillip K. Dick's contributions to the world of sci-fi literature had an almost immeasurable influence on the genre in the years after their original publication. His short story “We Can Remember It For You Wholesale” was loosely adapted into director Paul Verhoeven’s satirical sci-fi action thriller Total Recall, while his sci-fi noir novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? spawned the entire Blade Runner franchise.

Arriving on CBS in 2011, when Fringe’s success and the arrival of 2008’s theatrical spinoff The X-Files: I Want to Believe had reignited interest in sci-fi police procedurals, Person of Interest also benefitted from a killer premise. Person of Interest followed Jim Caviezel’s CIA officer John Reese as he began working with an eccentric inventor and billionaire tech mogul, Harold Finch.

Finch’s invention, simply called “The Machine,” was designed to use an algorithm to predict when terrorist attacks would happen. What made the mogul’s innovation so noteworthy was his claim that “The Machine” could predict terrorist attacks before the offenders themselves had even planned them. Reese is the first CIA operative to use this technology to track down individuals, and Person of Interest doesn’t shy away from the moral and ethical implications of this plan.

In fact, throughout its five-season run, Person of Interest never makes it entirely clear whether “The Machine” is a good idea at all, and modern real-life policing has only made the show’s predictions seem tame in comparison. Where The Machine was designed to avoid prejudiced policing, real-life predictive algorithms used by the police rely on racially biased data from existing police reports.

While Person of Interest had a villainous AI that tracked civilians without their consent, real-life private companies have created this infrastructure and currently license these technologies out to law enforcement agencies, from police departments to ICE, to monitor civilians with no criminal record. Where The Machine almost never makes any mistakes, real-life facial recognition software is notorious for often falsely identifying innocent civilians, especially people of color.

Although Prime Video’s superhero satire The Boys became infamous for accidentally predicting real-life events in its comedic storytelling, Person of Interest was alarmingly prescient in the show’s depictions of policing developments that ran roughshod over the rights of civilians. However, where Finch was transparent about how The Machine works, the private companies that run real-life surveillance tech offer no transparency over oversight.

While The Machine was used to save ordinary civilians from terrorism, real-life policing technologies are used for immigration enforcement, avoiding low-level work, and tracking civilians without their consent. Thus, if Person of Interest’s story got anything wrong, it was assuming these technologies would come from caring, troubled geniuses, and not from for-profit private companies who aren’t answerable to anyone.
