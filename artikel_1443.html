Water demand is the volume of water required by a sector to satisfy its needs1,2. Across Africa, these demands are frequently unmet3, yet their spatial and sectoral distribution remains incompletely characterized, limiting informed water-management decisions4. Here, we provide an Africa-wide estimate of water demand for 2020 across six sectors and multiple subsectors at high spatial resolution. Because the analysis integrates heterogeneous datasets and modelling assumptions, the resulting values should be interpreted as harmonized estimates rather than direct observations of realized water use. We estimate total annual water demand at 347 km3, comprising 60 km3 (17%) for municipalities (57–64 km3, with ±20% key parameters variations), 225 km3 (65%) for irrigation (214–233 km3, 95% confidence interval, with ±20% variations in irrigation efficiency), 11 km3 (3%) for livestock, 15 km3 (4%) for manufacturing, 33 km3 (10%) for electricity including hydropower and 3 km3 (1%) for mining (3–4 km3, with annual production variations). Estimated demand increased by 26% between 2010 and 2020. The dominant water-demand sector varies across regions and river basins, broadly reflecting established geographical and socioeconomic patterns. In parts of northern and southern Africa, estimated demand exceeds water availability, although results depend on assumptions regarding environmental-flow protection5. Elsewhere, water availability exceeds aggregate demand despite limited access to piped drinking water, indicating that physical availability alone does not determine water security. Our analysis therefore provides a spatially and sectorally resolved baseline for identifying water-demand pressures and access constraints rather than establishing a new causal mechanism of water insecurity. Local estimates should be interpreted cautiously given uncertainties in the underlying datasets.

Africa is a vast continent with heterogeneously spread water availability6,7. Yet, how sectoral water use and water demand (WD) are spatially distributed over the continent is not well known. Sectoral WD is the volume of water used by a sector to satisfy its needs, if sufficient water is available1,2,8. In many Western countries, sectoral water use generally equals WD; however, in African countries, this is often not the case. As an example, in 2020 only 47% of 1.36 billion Africans had piped drinking water access3, mainly because of a lack of adequate infrastructure. The WD of the remaining population that has no piped access is not accounted for in water use statistics of Aquastat9.

WD can be expressed in terms of water withdrawal (WW) or water consumption (WC)10. WW refers to the volume of water extracted from its source (rivers, lakes, aquifers). WC is the portion of WW that is not returned to the original water source after being withdrawn. Water use is a generic term that can mean both.

Sectoral water uses are difficult to quantify, making related data hard to find. The only way to accurately quantify real-world physical water uses is by measuring them, yet such data are only scarcely available.

A persistent lack of reliable water data and information is a widespread constraint on water management in the Global South4. For detailed water management assessments and informed policy decisions, knowing detailed WDs of all sectors in high spatial resolution is a necessity10. It is also important for researchers and stakeholders working in Africa to obtain open-access data, as they often lack the funds to purchase high-quality data. There is thus an urgent need for open-access, reliable, spatially distributed information on WDs covering Africa, including all relevant sectors. This limits research efforts and resulting science-based decisions among stakeholders.

To fill this data gap, we conducted an Africa-wide year 2020 WD analysis, at unprecedented spatial resolution (5 arcmin for 2 sectors, 2.5 arcminutes (arcmin) for 2 sectors and point data for 2 sectors) as well as unprecedented sectoral and subsectoral detail. The spatial resolutions of 2.5 arcmin and 5 arcmin equal about 4.5 km and 9 km at the equator, respectively. We quantify WD in terms of WW. We account for six sectors. The first sector is municipal WD (piped and non-piped population) at 2.5 arcmin resolution. We use actual water supply and water loss data of more than 450 water supply utilities spread over Africa to assess piped WD. These utilities are primarily sourced from the International Benchmarking Network (IBNET; https://newibnet.org), a unique information source for utility-specific water data. This is a first-time novelty, to our knowledge, that no existing study covering Africa11,12 has done.

The second sector is irrigation WD for 46 Spatial Production Allocation Model (SPAM) crops and major crop groupings13. The third sector is livestock WD (buffaloes, cattle, chickens, goats, pigs and sheep). The fourth sector is the manufacturing industry WD, in 2.5 arcmin resolution, differentiating between several subsectors such as ‘food and beverages’, ‘clothing’ or ‘paper and paper products’. The fifth sector is electricity production at 2,534 individual power plants, for 10 different fuel types, that is, the fossil fuels coal, oil and gas, the renewables hydropower, solar, wind, biomass and geothermal as well as two other fuel types (nuclear and waste heat). The sixth sector is mining WD, for 682 individual mines, differentiating between 34 main commodities such as gold, diamonds or iron ore.

Africa is developing fast because of population increase, economic growth, urbanization and shifting consumption patterns. These developments have profound effects on sectoral WDs. To analyse the change in sectoral and subsectoral WDs for the whole of Africa, we conduct our analysis by using the same input data and methodologies for the year 2010 and compare the results with the year 2020. The current lack of temporal WD data has led to marked gaps in knowledge of water-use trends across different sectors throughout Africa.

None of the existing global water use and WD studies11,12 have reached the level of sector and subsectoral and spatial detail, or the explicit focus on Africa, that we provide here. We provide open access to all our results.

Furthermore, we assess how WD relates to water availability, accounting for environmental flows (EFs). The latter are minimum flows required to maintain ecosystem integrity in rivers and other water bodies5,14. We use two EF methods, one accounting for high EF protection, the presumptive standard in ref. 15. The other method is the variable monthly flow (VMF) method16, accounting for lower EF protection. We thereby analyse whether Africa has the water resources to provide current WD and identify river basins in which WDs are high relative to available water availability.

Our study sheds light on the temporal evolution of sectoral WDs in Africa from 2010 to 2020, and on the question of whether Africa suffers from water scarcity and/or a water management problem.

For the Richter EF method, the total WD related to water availability results in values exceeding 100% in more river basins throughout Africa (Fig. 6b). Many basins in North Africa, including the Nile, as well as several basins in southern Africa, including the Orange and the Limpopo basins, show amounts higher than 100%. Combined, these basins represent 197 km3 (Fig. 6e, Richter method), 57% of the total WD of 347 km3. It is therefore questionable whether this high level of EF protection can be maintained in these basins. However, this high level can be maintained in many other basins, such as the Congo (Fig. 6b), the Zambezi, Chad or Niger.

Trade-offs between human WD and the level of EF protection are important in many African river basins. These considerations should include the different ecosystem services EFs provide for humans, many with direct links to specific Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)10,20. As an example, EFs sustain crocodiles and hippos in basins with wildlife tourism, which brings revenue for local communities up to the national level21. These basins include the Limpopo, flowing through the Kruger National Park, or the Tana River basin in Kenya. Careful consideration of trade-offs between human WDs and the level of EF protection in these basins can be made by evaluating more site-specific EFs22.

Overall, the WD of all sectors in Africa has increased from 274,763 Mm3 to 347,033 Mm3 (by 72,270 Mm3 or 26%), for the period from 2010 to 2020 (Extended Data Table 1).

Irrigation WD increased 25%, driven by population increase, economic growth, changing diets and consumer preferences23,24. This has led to farmers aiming to increase production and close water-limited crop yield gaps by expansion of irrigated areas13,25, resulting in substantially higher WDs for crops such as rice, maize, fruit, vegetables, groundnuts, soybeans and sugar cane (Supplementary Table 3).

Although African diets have historically relied on staple crops such as maize, sorghum and cassava, there has been a shift towards preferences in rice consumption, especially in western Africa and Madagascar, where per capita consumption now exceeds 100 kg annually24,26. An increase in Africa-wide rice consumption from 32 to 44 million tonnes from 2010 to 2020 has been met through a parallel increase in rice domestic production (60% of consumption) and imports (40% of consumption) from primarily Asia24. As a result, rice WD has increased by 38% from 39,999 Mm3, mostly in countries such as Senegal, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Nigeria and Cameroon. Increases in irrigated rice areas are associated with exposure to greater malaria risk27, yet alternate wetting and drying in African rice production has proven to decrease this exposure and reduce the WD of rice fields28.

Large parts of the population in African countries do not eat recommended amounts of fruit and vegetables, depriving them of essential nutrients24,29,30,31. African farmers have increased domestic fruit and vegetable production24, resulting in a WD increase by 24% from 28,392 Mm3 for fruits and by 115% from 9,006 Mm3 for vegetables. This increased production has led to more overall fruit and vegetable consumption throughout Africa24. A substantial part has also been used for export, resulting in so-called virtual water export, both inside (between African nations) and outside the continent32. This export includes bananas from Côte d’Ivoire and Cameroon, citrus fruit and grapes from South Africa and northern Africa24, or tomatoes from Morocco, often contributing to local water stress (Fig. 6).

The double burden of malnutrition, that is, the coexistence of undernutrition (stunting and wasting) alongside overnutrition (overweight and obesity), is prevalent throughout Africa29. In many regions and socioeconomic classes, the per capita intake of animal-sourced foods is among the lowest globally24,29,33,34. Some nations have therefore developed policies to increase the production and consumption of animal-sourced foods to address nutrient gaps, especially for eggs and dairy35,36. Alongside population increase, rising income and urbanization, these factors have driven the expansion of livestock numbers throughout Africa37,38. Consequently, from 2010 to 2020, we quantify large Africa-wide WD increases for cattle (+30%), goats (+48%), sheep (+26%) and chickens (+29%) (Extended Data Table 2 and Supplementary Table 5), with large increases in Chad, Ethiopia, Tanzania and Nigeria.

Municipal WD increased 29% (Extended Data Table 1), driven by population increase (+30% or 305 million additional people) as well as the efforts of the countries to increase WASH for their populations3. In Egypt, for example, piped WD increased by 2,131 Mm3 (+27%), with a population increase of 23% and an increase in connection rate from 95% to 99%, in line with Vision 2030 of Egypt. The RSA increased access to piped water supply from 11.5 to 14.6 million households39 for a growing population, resulting in a piped WD increase of 753 Mm3 (+22%). Ethiopia has been and is actively working to increase household piped water connections through the multi-sector ‘One WASH National Program’ launched in 2013, increasing access rates from 28% to 43% for a growing population.

Africa is still experiencing a persistent, multi-faceted electricity gap, with more than 500 million Africans without access to electricity in 2020. Power plant expansion from 2010 to 2020 has increased electricity generation and access throughout Africa. We identify 846 additional power plants that became operational from 2010 until 2020, that is, from 1,511 to 2,357 operational power plants, almost doubling electricity production to 1,046,061 GWh (Extended Data Fig. 1). Electricity WD increased 24% (Extended Data Table 3 and Extended Data Fig. 1). Particularly, the WD related to gas-fired power plants increased (104%) because of expansion of such plants in primarily northern Africa and Nigeria. Hydropower, a water-intensive electricity source, expanded less (+7% in WD) compared with gas-fired plants. To decarbonize the continent, many African countries invested in solar and wind expansion, energy sources that have a low water intensity in terms of WD per electricity unit produced40, only adding a WD of 13 Mm3 while adding production of 19,801 GWh.

The WD of the manufacturing industry increased Africa-wide with 3,161 Mm3 (+27%), albeit with strong regional differences (Extended Data Tables 1 and 4 and Supplementary Data 6). Throughout Africa, the manufacturing industry contributes only a small part to GDP. However, in some countries such as Egypt or South Africa, WDs have increased and constitute a substantial part of the total national WD. In some countries, we computed a decrease in WD, such as Algeria, which passed its peak crude oil production around 2010, with output and related WD decreasing up to 2020. The clothing subsector shows a substantial increase in WD (from 1,301 Mm3 to 2,290 Mm3), with large increases in Egypt, which is emerging as a global player in textiles and apparel production. Moreover, the food and beverages industry shows a large WD increase because of increased demand for processed foods and beverages.

The WD of mining increases substantially by 65%, from 1,964 Mm3 to 3,247 Mm3, the result of new mines and higher production in existing mines for different commodities. Increased phosphate rock mining in Morocco is driven by increased demand from global agriculture41, resulting in a WD increase of 257 Mm3. Morocco holds more than 70% of global phosphate rock reserves42. In the RSA, the WD for coal increases by 230 Mm3, driven by growing electricity demands from coal-fired power plants. Also the WDs of iron ore, gold, diamond, cobalt, copper, rare earth and uranium increase substantially. The WD of cobalt mining increases almost 500%, especially in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, driven by soaring cobalt demands worldwide, such as for producing electric vehicle batteries43.

Our Africa-wide WD analysis in unprecedented spatial and sectoral and subsectoral detail, shows a heterogeneously spread spatial pattern over the African continent and a substantial increase in sectoral WDs from 2010 to 2020. Our results stress the importance of accounting for the WD of all different sectors and subsectors in great spatial detail, as any sector or subsector can represent the highest WD in a region or river basin (Fig. 5). All sectoral WDs are required to compute water stress (Fig. 6). Omitting certain sectors or using too coarse spatial resolutions can lead to biased recommendations and decision-making.

Combined with available water resources, which are also not evenly distributed, this results in a diverse spread of river basins, where at present WD exceeds available water resources or not, depending on trade-offs in EF protection. Water scarcity is now concentrated in the dry areas of North Africa and southern Africa. Nevertheless, WDs are often not met Africa-wide, even in wetter regions, because of a lack of adequate infrastructure. Non-piped water access is lacking for 717 people with a total WD of 23,989 Mm3, much of whom are located in basins that are not water stressed. Massive investments are needed to increase water access and to meet different SDG 6 targets by 2030. Africa, therefore, shows both water scarcity and water management problems.

Our results should be regarded as best estimates and not as absolute amounts. They are the result of a combination of intensive data mining, explicitly looking for as much African-specific data as possible, modelling and spatial analyses, which include different assumptions and input data gaps. Inherent to any modelling study, this leads to uncertainty in the WD amounts we present. We therefore validate our results through different means and conduct selected uncertainty and sensitivity analyses, as discussed in detail in Supplementary Information sections 3 and 4. In the former, we validate our results by (1) comparing them with national data from Aquastat9 (Supplementary Table 5); (2) comparing them with the few global studies that provide spatially distributed sectoral WD data covering Africa (Supplementary Tables 6–9 and Supplementary Figs. 1–4), and (3) comparing them with other modelling approaches (Supplementary Table 10 and Supplementary Fig. 5). We show the novelty in comprehensiveness as well as groundbreaking spatial and sectoral and subsectoral resolution of our study, compared with existing studies.

In Supplementary Information section 4, we show robustness in our results by ±20% variations in key parameters for analysing irrigation WDs (Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Fig. 6), municipal WD (Supplementary Table 12) and mining WD (Supplementary Table 13) for the year 2020. For irrigation WDs, for example, ±20% variations in irrigation efficiency put our total amount of 225,174 Mm3 in a 95% confidence interval of 213,736–232,818 Mm3. Variations of ±20% for the factors physical water loss, as well as the proportion of domestic water use within municipal water use, result in the total municipal WD varying between −-9 and +13%. Limitations in this study include the reliance on some non-regional industrial water intensity data, for which reliable African data could not be found, or the fact that night-time light intensity maps can overlook rural informal sectors when spatially distributing national industrial WDs.

Africa is rapidly growing and changing, with WDs projected to increase substantially in the future44,45, because of population increase, shifting lifestyles and growing economies. Our 2020 and 2010 WD analyses can be considered as a baseline, from which there is a need to analyse future growing sectoral and subsectoral WDs with high spatial resolution.

Future sustainable development should take current sectoral WDs and resulting local water stress into account. Recommendations and policy implications include (1) the increase of water efficiencies and reduction of water losses for all sectors and subsectors; (2) the application of integrated water resources management (IWRM) with a nexus approach, while accounting for available water resources and EFs; (3) the development of alternative water resources such as water recycling or desalination; (4) massive investment to provide WASH to 100% of the African population; and (5) evaluation of the costs and benefits of agricultural and related virtual water exports outside Africa.

Water losses, high in many African countries for sectors such as municipalities and irrigation, need to be reduced. By including actual water loss data from water supply companies, we confirm high average physical water losses in African water supply networks. These range from low values of 10% in Windhoek, a water-scarce city, in which much water infrastructure investment has been done46, more than 19% in Cape Town and 27% in Johannesburg to values exceeding 40% and even 50% in different Nigerian, Kenyan and Ethiopian utilities. Reducing physical water losses would decrease municipal WDs substantially.

IWRM at the river basin, national and subnational levels should be implemented, accounting for the WD of all sectors and water availability, addressing trade-offs and win-wins. This requires coordination and collaboration between different ministries and stakeholders, aligning policies on water, agriculture, energy, industry and the environment. Also transboundary cooperation is required, as many African river basins are international (Fig. 6). This also includes the evaluation of where and how additional WDs can be further developed, addressing water resilience in investment strategies. As an example, future power plant planning and development needs to account for water and carbon intensities of different fuel types45. Hydropower is vulnerable to drought, requiring a mix of energy source investments. Further expansion of the low-cost energy sources such as solar and wind is a win-win in many African settings. Another example is the expansion of rice production, which should focus on areas without water stress and WD reductions by alternate wetting and drying28.

To reduce water stress, alternative water resources such as water recycling or desalination need to be developed. These water management practices are already practised in water-scarce regions such as Northern Africa47. WD management is crucial for balancing water availability and demand, particularly in areas facing water scarcity or growing WD pressures.

The fourth recommendation is to massively invest in the expansion, rehabilitation and maintenance of water supply for all Africans as well as the collection and purification of wastewater. The fifth recommendation is to evaluate the benefits (revenue) and costs (national food security, depletion of water resources) of the (virtual water) export related to (cash) crops from different African countries outside the continent, especially from water-scarce areas.

We assess the sectoral and subsectoral WD for the years 2020 and 2010, for municipal WD, irrigation, livestock, the manufacturing industry, electricity production and mining.

WD is the volume of water used by a sector to satisfy its needs, if sufficient water is available1,2,8. When sufficient water is not available, the need is not met, often leading to underperformance of a specific activity. As an example, during periods of low water availability such as dry periods, farmers might not have sufficient water to irrigate the full WD of their crops, leading to lower crop yields. Another example is the lack of drinking water infrastructure, so that a person has to fetch water from a distant water source. This person can thereby be deprived of a minimum water need for activities such as washing or cleaning. In our assessment, we quantify WDs to satisfy all sectoral needs.

We quantify WD in the sense of WW, not WC10. Many different definitions of water use exist, sometimes leading to confusion when they are not properly defined and applied. WW, also called water abstraction, refers to the volume of water withdrawn or extracted from its source (rivers, lakes and aquifers). WC refers to the portion of WW that is not returned to the original water source after being withdrawn, such as through evaporation. WW minus WC amounts to the return flow. Water use is a generic term that can apply to either WW or WC.

We compute all sectors in a spatial resolution of 5 arcmin (which accounts for 371,665 data points), apart from municipal and industrial WD, which we compute at 2.5 arcmin spatial resolution.

The spatial resolution we use is 2.5 arcmin, which equals 0.041667° or about 4.5 km at the equator. For each grid cell of 0.5°, the resolution of most existing studies, we quantify 144 grid cells. We also use 5 arcmin, which equals 0.08333° or about 9 km from the equator. For each grid cell of 0.5°, the resolution of most existing studies, we quantify 36 grid cells. We use point data for mining and electricity, the spatially most detailed resolution possible. For the total WD (the sum of all six sectors), the restricting sectors are irrigation and livestock, as their input data (SPAM crops13 and Gridded Livestock of the World or GLW data48,49, respectively) are available in 5 arcmin. No crop- or livestock-specific spatially distributed data covering Africa are available at higher spatial resolution. The spatial resolution 5 arcmin for total WD is therefore the highest resolution possible.

The African population in 2020 was 1,36 billion people, whereas in 2010, it was 1,05 billion people (an increase of 30%).

Not all industrial producers meter their water use. Many abstractions are self-supplied from natural water bodies. Depending on the industrial sector of a country, the number of these connections can be very high56. For example, statistical surveys in the Zhejiang Province of China identified 3,282 self-supplied water industrial enterprises in their directory56. Large industrial water users, such as the manufacturing plants or mines, often view their water use metrics as proprietary information. Withholding these data protects them from public backlash and competitive disadvantages. When not publicly mandated, they generally do not share such data. China is an exception, where an industrial enterprise dataset includes the water use of more than 400,000 enterprises for the year 2008 (refs. 56,57). This database is, however, the exception and is not available for any African country nor most other countries in the world. Industrial water use amounts in national reporting are therefore generally best estimates, which do not provide information on subsectoral water use or individual companies.

For agriculture, the amount of WW connections for irrigating individual fields and providing water to livestock on individual farms throughout Africa is in millions, because of the high number of farms on the continent58. As farmers are generally not required to measure the water they abstract from surface or groundwater, countries have limited data to estimate the water withdrawn for agriculture, let alone in a spatially distributed way or crop- or livestock-type specific way.

National reporting on sectoral water use is therefore very limited in most countries, including Africa59,60. The only international database available is Aquastat of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)9, which provides annual national sectoral WW data for agriculture (irrigation and livestock), domestic use and industry (manufacturing industry, electricity and mining). Countries are expected to provide Aquastat with national best estimates, through their statistical offices or responsible institutions, yet for Africa sectoral data are often lacking or outdated. As an example, for the statistic ‘WW for cooling of thermoelectric plants’, of 49 mainland African countries, Aquastat provides only one statistic for Zimbabwe. For the other 48 countries, no statistics are provided. Aquastat also provides only national data, and not subnational data. Its use for water management decisions is therefore very limited.

Some countries have made efforts to provide sectoral water use data with subnational spatial resolution. The United States has been at the forefront of providing such data; the last version for WWs is up to the year 2020 (ref. 61). However, its spatial resolution is not very high, nor is its subsectoral disaggregation. As this assessment is also restricted in measured source data, it relies heavily on modelling, a standard practice for computing sectoral water use61. Modelling is also essential as remote sensing cannot measure WWs, although it can help to estimate irrigation WC. Studies on sectoral water use at the national and subnational levels are scarce in the literature because of these limitations. The few existing global sectoral water use studies that cover Africa are global ones, such as refs. 11,12. These do not focus on Africa, however. They rely mostly on modelling and limited national statistics.

WDmin is set at 50 l per person per capita per day (l cap−1 day−1). This is the value defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the minimum amount to meet basic health-related needs, based on water requirement for food preparation, hydration and basic hygiene74. This value is used in many studies6,76 and corresponds to ref. 2, which recommends a minimum value of 50 l cap−1 day−1, with 5 l cap−1 day−1 for drinking water, 20 l cap−1 day−1 for sanitation, 15 l cap−1 day−1 for bathing and 10 litres/cap/day for cooking and kitchen use. This study argues that these values may fluctuate according to climatological conditions. The WHO, for example, also indicates different values for drinking water, ranging from 3.2 l cap−1 day−1 for sedentary activity in a temperate climate to 4.6 l cap−1 day−1 for moderate physical activity at warm temperature. The latter is more appropriate as the average amount in the African context.

Household (HH) size is a notable determinant of household water use. An increase in HH size causes an increase in total household water use, whereas per capita water use decreases with an increase in HH size77. The decrease in per-capita use is due to many HH water uses, such as cleaning the house, washing machines, dishwashers, cooking and irrigation, being shared among the occupants of the HH. Studies in Europe, where per capita HH water use is high, have shown that these amounts can be substantial78. However, for low WDs such as 50 l cap−1 day−1, per capita reductions are much lower. A study in Uganda, for example, with low per capita water use, found a 0.65 l  cap−1 decrease for each additional HH member74,79. Given that average HH sizes in Africa are high compared with those of developed countries and that each member still needs water for basic services, we assume a reduction of 1 l per additional HH member, as expressed in equation (2). Subnational data for HH size (equation (2)) are provided by the Global Data Lab (globaldatalab.org), for 543 regions covering Africa, ranging from 3.08 in central Kenia to +10 in Senegal and Gambia. Calculated grid cell WDdomestic-noLoss amounts range from 42.5 to 49.0 l cap−1 day−1.

FACTORmunicipal (equation (1)) accounts for the fact that a certain proportion of municipal water is for non-domestic use, such as water for small businesses, services such as schools or hospitals, and others. Generally, this proportion is about 20–30% of municipal WD, as confirmed by most African water utilities in IBNET. Here, we used IBNET data, which were extrapolated to regions in which data are missing. FACTORmunicipal is thus about 70–80%.

LOSSmunicipal (equation (3)) is the physical water loss in municipal water use, defined as the percentage of water physically lost between what a water supply utility abstracts from the natural system and what it provides to end users. This physical loss is the result of leakage due to old pipes or poor maintenance, but is also caused by pipe breaks. IBNET does not provide many statistics on physical loss in African utilities, but it provides statistics on non-revenue water (NRW) for many utilities. Apart from physical loss, NRW, for example, includes unbilled water or water theft. For utilities in which LOSSmunicipal were not available, we use utility NRW statistics for our calculations. We assume that 75% of NRW is actual LOSSmunicipal, an amount that can be justified by different sources59. Representative utility data are extrapolated to regions for which data are missing.

People not connected to piped water access may have other kinds of access, such as yard taps, public taps, wells, boreholes or surface water. Often these sources are of poor quality and may require substantial round-trip travel time for water collection, especially in many African regions. Generally, people are not able to collect large quantities of water from these sources, not reaching the minimum amount of 50 litres per person per day74 we set in our analysis. They are thereby deprived of a minimum drinking WD and are defined as a population without piped access.

We also account for the WD of hydropower, because of its large current and growing water use in Africa, which ref. 40 quantified at 23,038 Mm3 in 2020. This evaporation does affect EFs, in the sense that the reservoir water is evaporated and therefore not available downstream, potentially contributing to the violation of downstream EFs and resulting in diminished ecosystem services. Our assessment does not account for the additional negative effect of altered flow regimes and dam barriers, which block fish migration when fish passage is not ensured, leading to fragmented habitats, reduced populations and interrupted spawning cycles120,121.

We acknowledge that night-time light intensity maps can overlook rural informal sectors, as they primarily capture artificial lighting associated with electrification, which is less prevalent in rural, low-income areas142. Rural informal sectors, such as home-based manufacturing, often operate without much electric lighting, rendering them invisible to satellite sensors. Outside urban centres, most night-time lighting tends to serve household needs—cooking, studying and socializing—rather than the manufacturing or commercial production. This limitation is inherent to all studies. Nevertheless, according to ref. 143, night-time light should not be underestimated as a method to overcome the lack of population census data and socioeconomic surveys at very fine levels and outside cities in middle- and low-income countries. Our assessment is to be regarded as best estimate.

Natural renewable water at a spatial resolution of 0.5° (which equals 55.5 km at the equator) was taken from two global models: WaterGAP2-2e144 and LPJmL5-7-10-fire145,146. We took two models to account for uncertainty in the output of global hydrological models. Their output is provided in ISIMIP (Inter-Sectoral Impact Model Intercomparison Project), simulation round ISIMIP3a, under the ‘nowatermgt’ run, which models naturalized flow (without human water abstraction or reservoirs). We used monthly outputs to be able to calculate EFs, for the period 2010 to 2020, a common practice to account for interannual variation.

We use two EF methods: one accounting for high EF protection, that is, the presumptive standard in ref. 15. It defines 80% of the natural flow as EF. The remaining 20% is considered as water available for human use. This presumptive standard is supported by empirical studies showing that flow alterations within 20% support native fish species and flow alteration beyond this level strongly affects biodiversity and ecosystem structure and function147. It is widely used in many water management studies5,76,148. The other EF method is the VMF method16, which accounts for lower EF protection. During low-flow months (when long-term mean monthly streamflow (MMF) is ≤40% of the long-term mean annual flow (MAF)), 60% of MMF is allocated to EFs; in high-flow months (MMF > 80% of MAF) the EF share is 30% of MMF; otherwise it is 45%. This method was tested by means of case studies of locally assessed EFs and is also widely used5. By using these two EF methods, we provide an upper and lower range in renewable water availability (equation (9)), because one represents high and the other lower EF protection.

We aggregated monthly amounts to the annual level for the African river basins. We then related total WD to annual renewable water availability to obtain a level of water stress. This approach is in line with SDG indicator 6.4.2 (ref. 149), and its statistics are provided by Aquastat. Values exceeding 100% theoretically mean that EFs are violated. More sophisticated water stress assessments also look at consumptive WD, on the monthly level and/or the sub-basin level, as discussed in ref. 10 and analysed by several studies5,150,151. However, using WD on the annual level provides a first indication of water stress and is in line with Aquastat and general SDG reporting.

We do not account for regional or local EF assessments, as they do not cover the entire continent of Africa and because the two methods we use are well established to provide an upper and lower range in renewable water availability. However, these regional EF methods exist, especially in southern Africa, where much effort has been done to identify these methodologies152,153,154.

The natural renewable water assessments we use, that is, WaterGAP2-2e144 and LPJmL5-7-10-fire145,146, include renewable groundwater. They do not account for fossil groundwater10, primarily found in vast, non-renewable aquifers beneath the Sahara. Groundwater is an important buffer for the availability of freshwater throughout the year.

Few studies provide spatially distributed sectoral WD data covering the whole of Africa. We validate our results with data from Aquastat9, through comparison with other studies and through additional modelling approaches.

Other studies include the study in ref. 12, which quantified municipal and industrial WDs for the year 2010 on a global level; ref. 11, which quantified sectoral WDs for the year 2010 on a global level in a spatial resolution of 0.5° (50–55 km); ISIMIP155,156, which quantified sectoral WDs for the years 2010 and 2020 on a global level in a spatial resolution of 0.5°; and ref. 157, which quantified industrial WDs for the years 2005, 2010 and 2015 on a global level in a spatial resolution of 0.5°. For specific sectors, we use selected high-quality studies118,158,159.

A previous study12 computed the WD for only three sectors (municipal, manufacturing industry and electricity) at a resolution of 5 arcmin until 2010, without accounting for subsectors, yet did not provide their data in open access. This study computed multi-sectoral water use up to 2010, in much coarser resolution (0.5°, that is, about 55 km from the equator) and by simple downscaling of the sectors mining11, manufacturing industry11,12 and electricity11 based on population density. The study did not account for subsectors, individual crops or livestock types. As first-time novelties, we explicitly identify and locate mines and individual electricity power plants; we account for actual water supply data of more than 450 African water supply utilities; we assess the manufacturing industry at the subsectoral level; and we provide an assessment for all sectors for the recent year 2020 as well as 2010 at the highest spatial resolution.

For additional validation of our manufacturing industry WD results, we conducted industrial WD quantifications for selected industrial subsectors where outputs are available in weight quantities.

WDind,subsectorX is the WD of a certain subsector X of the manufacturing industry;

QUANTITYind,subsectorX is the national weight quantity of a certain subsector X of the manufacturing industry for the year 2020; and

The food and beverage processing industry is a subsector in which these data are available at the national level, through the FAO Food Balance Sheets (FAO-FBSs)34. An FAO-FBS provides a comprehensive picture of the agrifood situation of a country in a specified reference period, showing the pattern of the food supply and utilizations of a country. Food and beverages processed in the national food and beverages processing industry are provided under the element ‘processed’. We used these quantities as QUANTITYind,subsectorX. FAO-FBSs does not provide values for all African countries. The specific subsector WI for food and beverage processing is set at an average of 8 m3 per tonne, a value supported by several literature sources, including African ones160,161,162. For the subsectors, ‘clothing’, industry output statistics are generally provided only in monetary output. We found only one reliable weight statistic for Morocco, a large textile and clothing producer on the African continent—that is, an annual production of 950,000 tonnes (ref. 163). The textile and clothing industry uses an average of 100–300 m3 of water per tonne of finished fabric (average 200 m3 of water per tonne), which is a wide range depending on technique and material used164,165,166,167.

For the subsector ‘paper and paper products’, we found a reliable production statistic for South Africa, that is, 1,891,283 tonnes for 2020 (ref. 24). South African and international data show a rather wide range in the water intensity of the paper processing industry, with an average of about 70 m3 per tonne, depending on the kind and state of processing mills and the product produced168,169. Modern mills have lower water intensities, and older mills have higher values.

Similar to any modelling study, different assumptions have been made, which lead to uncertainty in the WD amounts we present. We therefore conduct selected uncertainty and sensitivity analyses.

We account for uncertainty in water availability by using two different EF methods, as described in the previous section “WD related to water resources”. The resulting variation in WDs related to available renewable water resources in African river basins is presented in Fig. 6.

To compute crop-specific irrigation WDs, we used the factor IE in each grid cell (equation (5)). Although we provide IE best estimates based on several sources, including local studies, IE has a certain level of uncertainty. To account for this, we conducted a spatial Monte Carlo analysis by randomly varying IE (the random variable) amounts between ±20% and their effect on irrigation WDs for the year 2020, while accounting for the spatial distribution of individual irrigated areas and Tobler’s first law of geography170. To do the latter, we created clusters of points that are both adjacent (eight-way connectivity) and have similar nominal IE values. During the Monte Carlo simulation, a single random IE value is assigned to all points within each cluster (an irrigation area) in every iteration (1 million in total), preserving spatial autocorrelation in the generated uncertainty. For the range of IE uncertainty (±20% of the nominal value), we use a uniform distribution. A uniform distribution was used because all values within the ±20% range are considered equally likely. The resulting irrigation WD curve is presented in Supplementary Information section 4.

We also conduct a sensitivity analysis for municipal WDs in the year 2020, by varying the factors LOSSmunicipal (physical water loss) and FACTORmunicipal (the proportion of domestic water use within municipal water use in %), as used in equations (1)–(4). We vary LOSSmunicipal as well as 100 − FACTORmunicipal by −20% and +20% for all 1.4 million grid cells as a sensitivity analysis. We choose 100 – FACTORmunicipal, because other domestic water uses are in the range 20–30% on average. It is realistic to vary these amounts by −20% and +20%. Varying the domestic part—on average in the range 70–80%—by −20% and +20%, would be a much wider variation (for example, adding 20% to 80% results in 96%, leaving only 4% for other uses, which can be perceived as extreme and rare). Resulting municipal WD amounts (piped, non-piped and total) are presented in Supplementary Information section 4.

To account for uncertainty in individual mine production in the year 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, we conduct a sensitivity analysis based on individual mine production amounts for the years 2019, 2020 and 2021. The results are presented in Supplementary Information section 4.
