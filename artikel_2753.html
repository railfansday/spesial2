Rafael Benitez on Liverpool, the dangers of data and why he’s ‘better than ever’ as a coach

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Rafael Benitez has a bowl of strawberries and yoghurt in front of him.

It's breakfast time at VIPs restaurant in Valdemarin, a residential neighbourhood a short drive from the centre of Madrid. The former Liverpool manager is back in the Spanish capital after a trip to Barcelona to watch Hansi Flick's side beat Aston Villa 3-0 in the Champions League in his role as a UEFA technical observer.

"Interesting game," he says. "Barcelona playing very high and Villa trying to put the ball in behind to avoid the pressing, but they made some mistakes.

"Since the rule changed in 2019, allowing goal kicks to be taken short inside the box, the modern way is to play out from the back. They saw top teams being successful playing like that and thought, 'That's the way', but not-so-big teams lose the ball more times and concede more goals. Top teams are still winning because they press high and have faster, stronger players to regain the ball and score. The pattern is clear."

It is typical Benitez — forensic analysis of a sport he still adores after 40 years as a coach, even if his current work with UEFA involves providing advice and support to young managers across Europe.

"They ask a lot of questions, especially about the numbers (data)," he adds. "I say you have to strike the balance between numbers and experience. Numbers won't explain all the game. For example, little things like body shape when you are defending, which is crucial in one-v-ones. That's the difference between experience as a coach and theory."

Benitez paid a visit to Liverpool's training complex in September to meet Andoni Iraola, the club's new head coach. He was impressed.

"We had a long conversation about football in the Premier League," Benitez says. "He's working so hard to implement his ideas and he's had some good results already.

"When I was in my last year at Anfield, we were actually scouting Andoni when he was at Athletic Club and we hoped to sign him. Now he's the manager and the feeling I had from talking with a few players was very positive. He will need some time to build something."

Benitez's current life involves, as he puts it, analysing "tactics and trends" and then visiting different federations to share his views. He was recently in Norway; his next stop will be Belgium.

It's stress-free and, at 66, it keeps Benitez involved in elite football. But during 90 minutes in his company, it's also clear that he's not ready for a quiet life. Out of management since being sacked by Greek club Panathinaikos in May, he's ready to return.

"People say to me: 'You can retire now, relax.' No! I like to improve players. I miss it when I'm not out there on the training pitch.

"Yesterday I was in contact with (Barcelona's) Anthony Gordon. When he was at Everton, coming from the academy, we were working on him. It was clear he had the talent and desire to improve. Now he's playing for Barcelona.

"Go back to my time at Liverpool, we signed Javier Mascherano, who was playing for West Ham reserves (Mascherano played just seven games in his six months at West Ham, despite already being an established Argentina international). I have a meeting coming up with Mista, my striker at Valencia when we won La Liga twice. Nobody knew him but he scored 24 goals one season.

"For a lot of clubs, it's a case of improving young players and then selling them. We did that for years."

In recent months, Benitez has turned down offers from clubs in Brazil and Mexico, as well as several national team jobs. He's waiting for the right challenge and yearns for a fifth spell in England, having previously managed Liverpool, Chelsea (as an interim), Newcastle United and Everton.

"For me, the priority is the Premier League. After you go Italy, Spain, Germany and France. In general, the Premier League has the most money and is able to buy the best players. You have some fantastic teams in Spain and Italy, but I like the atmosphere in the Premier League.

"I'm not just watching games; I'm analysing with a tactical vision, trying to learn something new which I could implement. I don't just want to be in a job; I want to be sure I can compete for something — whether that's staying in the division, qualifying for Europe or winning something.

"I want to stay in Europe. Maybe I'll have to go to China but that's not the idea. There will come a point where if you cannot get what you want, you have to make a decision."

Benitez's own coaching journey started at Real Madrid's academy before spells at Valladolid, Osasuna, Extremadura and Tenerife in his homeland. But it was at Valencia where his profile rocketed, as he won two La Liga titles and the UEFA Cup.

Liverpool appointed him as Gerard Houllier's successor and his finest hour came in Istanbul with the 2005 Champions League final triumph over Milan when his team fought back from 3-0 down at the break to win on penalties. In his second season, they lifted the FA Cup.

However, further glory eluded him. The club was plunged into crisis by the damaging, debt-ridden reign of owners Tom Hicks and George Gillett, culminating in Benitez's departure in 2010. Does he ever think about what might have been but for the boardroom chaos?

"Yes, 100 per cent. When Hicks and Gillett came (in 2007), they changed the approach. All the things you did for the benefit of the club, they had different ideas.

"I realised, and the fans realised later, that something was wrong. They didn't have experience of football. I remember speaking to Gillett once in London after we played Tottenham. He said: 'We will give you money and then next season you'll get us into the play-offs.' I had to tell him there were no play-offs.

"They thought maybe: 'Rafa is a threat.' That period was difficult. But all I ever wanted was the best for the club."

Benitez moved to Italy and won the Club World Cup with Inter before taking temporary charge of Chelsea, which ended with Europa League success.

After two seasons in charge of Napoli, where he lifted the Coppa Italia, Benitez was appointed Real Madrid manager in June 2015. It was the realisation of a lifelong dream, but his tenure lasted just seven months. He was sacked with Madrid sitting third in La Liga, four points behind leaders Atletico.

"It was a complicated period," he says. "Iker Casillas was leaving and in pre-season Sergio Ramos wanted to go, so there was uncertainty. In the Copa del Rey, we were thrown out because of an administrative mistake (Denis Cheryshev played when suspended).

"There were a lot of issues around the club. They were a bit nervous and losing to Barcelona in the Bernabeu was a big one. It was still a great experience."

From Madrid, Benitez went to Newcastle United, locked in a relegation battle. They went down but he stayed and restored them to the Premier League.

"We achieved something very difficult. We made a £30m profit (on player transfers) and won the Championship. Then we finished 10th and 13th in the Premier League without spending big money. That was a great achievement."

In the summer of 2019, Benitez joined Chinese Super League outfit Dalian Professional, an "amazing" experience ultimately curtailed by Covid, but one which brought cultural challenges.

"There, it's the translator who gives the game plan, but if he knows too much then he thinks that he's the coach! I asked one of the translators: 'Do you know anything about this team we're facing?' He said: 'Yes, they will play 4-4-2. Their translator is a friend, he told me.' I said: 'What have you been telling him about us?'"

Given his history with Liverpool, his appointment at Everton in 2021 was controversial and triggered a backlash from some supporters. He was sacked the following January with Everton 15th, although he says he has no regrets about accepting the job.

"I considered it carefully. It was the chance to work in Liverpool again, my family were there and we love the city. It was also a project where I was told there would be big investment.

"Alisher Usmanov (the business partner of then majority shareholder Farhad Moshiri) was very keen to invest and develop something important. He wanted to win trophies. I trusted him. Everton spent more than £500m over five years on players, but with us they spent just £2m (on Demarai Gray).

"We were in the top four near the start of the season. But then we had injuries and didn't have the squad to cope with it. As soon as some things were wrong, you had some fans not happy because of my links with Liverpool.

"A lot of good Liverpool and Everton fans will understand, but the ones who are radical will not see it. I love Liverpool Football Club and I had a good time with a lot of people at Everton Football Club. Some people don't understand that, but that's fine; I can't change that."

He returned to Spain to manage Celta Vigo in 2023-24 but was gone within nine months with them sitting just above the relegation zone. He then spent last season at Panathinaikos in Greece, but left in May.

"That was an example of the goalposts being moved. The target was always to finish in the top four. We finished fourth, which meant we went into the play-offs (which determines the champions). Then they said: 'Now you must win the title.' No, we cannot. We weren't the best team in the top four; we were the worst. We maximised the talent we had but if you don't have the right players, you cannot win.

"After we left, they signed 14 new players. We were doing a good job with what we had… but football is very emotional."

Aside from working for UEFA, Benitez has been busy digitising a vast collection of notes dating back 40 years. They include training and tactical plans, team talks, half-time analysis and post-match assessments.

"I had folders everywhere with coaching notes going back to 1986, so I've been scanning," he says. "In fact, I started taking notes when I was 13 in Real Madrid's academy. Football has changed but the principles remain the same.

"Remember in my first year in the Premier League (with Liverpool), we were doing zonal marking when nobody else was. We conceded two goals from corners against Manchester United and people were so critical. For the next two years, we were the team conceding the fewest goals from set pieces.

"Now, everyone rotates but remember the criticism (in 2010) when I took off Fernando Torres at Birmingham and put on David Ngog?"

"I'm always trying to evolve. I was analysing one of my training sessions earlier and then putting the artificial intelligence into it to see if it gives me something I might have forgotten," he says.

"People talk about Spanish football and tiki-taka, but 60 per cent of goals are scored with less than six passes — that's more or less the same across all the leagues I have analysed. Then you have around 25 per cent of goals from set pieces. Not many are scored with lots of touches.

"I was talking with Spain manager Luis de la Fuente after I gave him an award at the Ballon d'Or. He said: 'I have Nico Williams and Lamine Yamal on the wings, so why would I play pass, pass, pass? It has to go in behind or to feet so they can beat players.' You have to work out what's best for your team and adapt."

Goalkeeping coach Joaquin Valerio joins us at the breakfast table. Like assistant coach Antonio Gomez and fitness coach Paco de Miguel, Valerio is waiting to discover where the journey takes them next.

"It has to be somewhere they respect your vision and experience," Benitez adds. "Are you creating something for the future? Is everyone on the same page? You need a clear vision and communication across the club, and time to improve a team.

"When you have had success in different countries, when you've had to handle difficult situations, when you know the mentality of players, you can manage in a different way and get the best out of them. Young people, especially, don't attach too much value to experience, but after all these years, I'm a better coach than I've ever been."
