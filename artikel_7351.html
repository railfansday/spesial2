Can Media Budgets Go Big on Brand Building and Still Drive Sales?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

This post was created in partnership with Marketing Architects

There are many avenues today for capturing demand and driving sales. But sacrificing slow and steady brand building to focus just on quick wins that satisfy the CFO could set brands up for future irrelevance.

During an ADWEEK House Commerce Summit group chat co-hosted with Marketing Architects, industry leaders discussed the importance of a two-fold marketing strategy that drives both immediate revenue goals and long-lasting consumer loyalty.

Capturing demand and demonstrating the results is clean and neat in the short term, but what happens next? That key question, and theme of the session, was introduced by Clark Hjelmstad, VP of strategic partnerships at Marketing Architects.

“When that immediate demand capture starts to run dry, what do brands do?” he asked. Successful marketers, he answered, plan for both today and tomorrow. “You need to be grabbing that demand in market right now at this time, while also building for the future.”

Katie Sheeran, VP of marketing at sweetgreen, shared her strategy for making that very argument to the C-suite.

“Let’s look at the next 18 months. You show that ROAS starts to tick down and down and down,” Sheeran said. “If you’re not priming the pump and welcoming people in, those numbers are going to get a lot worse.”

Evan Jones, former CMO of Fender, likened such conversations to a dance vs. a static conversation.

“The best conversations, the best strategy, the best decisions come out of the positive tension between who we’re serving today versus who we’re trying to reach; what’s working now, what might work later,” Jones said.

Back when Sarah Davis, founder, president, and chief creative officer of FASHIONPHILE, launched the designer luxury resale company, there were no Google Ads, Pinterest, TikTok, or Instagram. But today brands must set themselves apart amid a sea of competition across various channels.

Her team’s goal for reaching the consumer who might have 100 choices in front of them is to be the brand that gets them to reach this conclusion: “Oh my gosh, there’s FASHIONPHILE. I trust them. Their ship is fast and fair. It’s going to be authentic,’” Davis explained.

Star Sage, VP of business development at Hyperice, remarked on the added challenge of trying to market a brand that started in the training rooms of pro athletes to consumers.

“Our sales team is basically brand storytellers to the audiences that we’re trying to find,” Sage shared. “Most of our consumers don’t even know that they want our product.  It’s a lot of experimenting most of the time.”

Jones elaborated that Fender has also done a lot of learning about audiences through social media marketing trial and error.

“We launched an Instagram page at one point around just new players,” Jones shared. “What we found was there are places where beginners have universal problems, so a new segment has a universal issue, but they don’t want to be isolated and kept separate from the bigger community and the bigger brand experience.”

Ultimately, Fender decided to lean into stories and brand positioning that celebrated the journey, whether you’re a professional artist or somebody who’s just getting started. “We probably failed way more than we succeeded, but at the same time, we more than tripled the business,” he said.

You can’t build a brand reputation online by simply reposting the same content everywhere, Davis attested.

“We recognize that we have to also acknowledge the platform,” she said. As an example, when FASHIONPHILE’s usual elevated voice wasn’t breaking through on TikTok Shop, they brought on creators who could talk about the brand and its offerings in a more platform-aligned way.

“We’re not changing who we are, but we’re showing up in a way that is resonating with the audience that you’re finding there,” Davis said. “The sales are evidence that this is actually working.”

To explain the delicate balance of channel planning, Sheeran used a frog metaphor. She explained that frogs have fixed skeletal structures, but also amazing elasticity.

“You have to think about: What are those bones that are absolutely non-negotiable?  What needs to show up every single place you go, everywhere you hop?” she explained. “But then also, how do you challenge yourself to be as flexible and rubbery and frog-like as possible?”

Being malleable to the platform you’re on is crucial, agreed Kristin Patterson, VP of omnichannel retail at Pinterest. That said, she pointed out, Pinterest can help brands across the funnel.

“If you are on Pinterest, you should have your full product catalog—all your feed, product—on a white background,” she said, to reach the customers who are ready to convert. But having creator assets ready can help you catch the attention of consumers open to exploring, too. “You’re letting our algorithm and the AI surface what’s best for that person in the moment to be highly personalized,” she said.

Regardless of platform, Hjelmstad said building brand credibility is key. “We’ve had a lot of success bringing brands from digital into television to gain that credibility, to stand out.”
