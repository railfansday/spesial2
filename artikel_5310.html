Florida State fired Mike Norvell despite a $58.7 million buyout

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Ten college football coaches carry buyouts between $49.5 million and $105.1 million. See what each price says about the school.

At the top of college football, firing a head coach has become one of the most expensive decisions a university can make. Schools have shown they will pay sums in the tens of millions of dollars to change direction. The Associated Press reports that Texas A&M owed Jimbo Fisher more than $75 million when it decided to start over. LSU also accepted a large buyout to part ways with Brian Kelly, according to CBS Sports. Both schools chose to pay that price rather than keep a coach they no longer wanted. A buyout's size alone doesn't decide whether a coach keeps his job. It sets the price a school must accept to make a change, and the wealthiest programs in the sport have shown they will accept it.

The figure attached to a coach is less settled than it looks, because it marks the most a school might owe rather than what the school ends up paying. The Associated Press reports that the amount depends largely on how many years remain on a contract. A school that waits a season before acting owes less than one that acts today. A school can also bargain. Penn State owed James Franklin $49 million when it let him go, CBS Sports reports, and the two sides settled on $9 million. That changes how to read any coach's price tag. Each figure describes a contract on one date. It says as much about what a school was willing to promise as about how secure the coach really is.

Sports Illustrated set out to identify which college football coaches would cost the most to fire, at programs ranging from Indiana and Illinois to Georgia and Florida State. Each figure is the price a school would pay to change course, not a measure of a coach's salary or skill. Some of these contracts were written to keep a winning coach in place. For those schools, a large number is a statement of how much they want to keep the man they have. Other contracts are being tested by losing seasons, and for those schools the figure is the real cost of a change they may be weighing. At least one school on the list has already made that change anyway.

Georgia has made the largest commitment to its coach of any school in college football. As of Dec. 1, 2025, firing Kirby Smart would have cost the school $105.1 million, far more than any other known buyout in the sport, according to Sports Illustrated. Georgia faces a bill that large because of a promise it made, not because of a penalty clause. Bleacher Report reports that the school fully guaranteed Smart's deal if it fires him without cause through Dec. 31, 2028, and that the guarantee drops to 85% after that. Georgia's full promise therefore covers the next few seasons, and for those seasons the school has chosen to face the sport's highest price if it ever wants a change.

The contract binds Georgia far more than it binds Smart. Smart could walk away for a small part of what Georgia would owe to push him out, paying $5 million through the end of 2026, according to Bleacher Report. Smart, not the school, holds the easy way out. If another job ever tempts him, the decision is his.

Georgia has little reason to want a change, because Smart keeps winning. Under Smart, Georgia claimed back-to-back national titles in the 2021 and 2022 seasons and holds a 46-3 record over that stretch, according to Bleacher Report. That kind of winning makes the guarantee easy for the school to stand behind. Georgia wrote the largest buyout in college football to protect a coach who almost never loses. At a school with a struggling coach, a buyout is a price to weigh. At Georgia, it is mostly a statement of intent.

Georgia has promised more than any other school to keep its coach, and Smart is the one who decides whether the deal holds.

Ohio State has made one of the largest promises any school has made to keep its coach. The school would owe Ryan Day $70.9 million if it fired him, the second-largest buyout in college football, according to Sports Illustrated, and his salary ranks among the top 10 in the sport. Ohio State made that commitment to a coach who had already delivered. Day won the national title in 2024, according to CBS Sports. With a promise that large, Ohio State was declaring that Day was the coach it meant to keep, more than it was buying him insurance.

A disappointing season has turned that promise into a cost Ohio State has to weigh. The Buckeyes lost four games in 2026, according to CBS Sports, a heavy total for a team that had won it all two seasons earlier. The cost of acting has also fallen since the $70.9 million figure was dated Dec. 1, 2025. A firing once the 2026 season ends would leave Ohio State with a $57.5 million bill, a figure dated Nov. 21, 2026. The drop comes from how the contract is built. Day's buyout is set as a fixed sum for each remaining year rather than a share of his remaining salary, so the total shrinks as each season passes. The longer the school waits, the less it would pay to make a change, and the less protection Day gets from his contract.

A smaller bill alone may not settle Day's future. A school should fire a coach only when it has a clearly better replacement in mind, and the market for coaches is thin right now. Day has gone 89-16 at Ohio State, according to CBS Sports, and has won the large majority of his games. For a change to pay off, Ohio State would need to find a coach who could top that record.

For Ohio State, the money is only one reason to keep Day, alongside his winning record and a short list of better options.

Alabama would pay one of the steepest prices in college football to fire Kalen DeBoer, but most of that price comes from a promise the school made before its latest move. Firing him would cost Alabama $60.8 million, the third-largest buyout among football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated. FanSided reports that DeBoer's original contract already ran through 2031, and that Alabama restructured it and added a raise rather than writing a wholly new commitment. Alabama had largely accepted its side of the bill before the new terms. The school's recent changes were aimed at the other side of the contract.

The restructuring set what DeBoer would owe if he chose to leave. Under the original deal, he could have walked away owing Alabama nothing after Jan. 31, 2027, according to FanSided. Michigan and Penn State were rumored to be interested in him, which made a free exit a real risk for the school. The new deal has DeBoer owing $10 million to leave through Jan. 31, 2027, and that gives Alabama some protection. Even so, the contract binds the school far more than the coach. In exchange for a raise, Alabama made DeBoer harder to lure away.

Alabama made this commitment before DeBoer's results could show whether he is the coach for the long run. He has gone 20-8 in his two seasons at Alabama, according to FanSided, and that record has not settled the question. The cost of firing him comes from the terms Alabama wrote, not from how often his teams win. Alabama is paying to avoid starting over, with the expense and risk of a new coaching search at a program that has to keep winning now.

At Alabama, the big number is less armor for DeBoer than a premium the school accepted to keep the coach it knows.

Texas would face such a large bill to change coaches because the school made its biggest promise to Sarkisian only recently. Firing him would cost Texas $60.3 million, the fourth-largest buyout in college football, according to Sports Illustrated. Longhorns Wire reports that Texas signed the deal in 2025 and committed to Sarkisian through 2031. Nearly all of that long promise has yet to be paid. The size of the figure shows how strongly Texas bet on its coach a short time ago, more than how secure his job is today.

Texas is facing a test of that bet much sooner than the school planned when it signed the deal. The team held the No. 1 ranking only two weeks earlier, according to Longhorns Wire, and the fall since then has been fast enough that people now discuss a firing openly. "I believe, personally, that after Texas loses its third game (Texas athletic director) Chris Del Conte has an honest conversation about the firing of Steve Sarkisian," Cole Thompson, co-host of the SEC Unfiltered Podcast, told Longhorns Wire. Texas already has a possible interim coach on staff in defensive coordinator Will Muschamp. With a replacement in the building, the size of the $60.3 million check is the main reason for Texas to hold back.

The exact cost of firing Sarkisian is not a single fixed number. The $60.3 million figure dates to Dec. 1, 2025. Longhorns Wire reports a different amount for a firing after the 2026 season, putting the bill at $50.15 million. Even the lower figure would leave Texas paying tens of millions of dollars to a coach it no longer wanted.

Texas now has to decide whether one season's fast fall justifies a check that large.

Few schools have committed to a coach as firmly, or as far into the future, as Clemson has to Dabo Swinney. Firing him would have cost $60 million as of Dec. 1, 2025, the fifth-largest buyout in college football, according to Sports Illustrated, and his salary also ranks among the top 10 in the sport. Clemson pays Swinney like one of the game's elite coaches and has promised to keep doing so for years. Post and Courier reports that his contract runs until 2031, so most of that $60 million covers seasons Swinney has not yet coached. The buyout records how much faith Clemson placed in Swinney when it signed the deal. It does not guarantee his future.

Clemson is now weighing that faith against a season that has gone wrong. The team is 6-4 overall and 4-3 in the ACC heading into its game at Duke $DUK, according to Post and Courier, a middling record for a program that has grown used to contending. A school with that record has good reason to ask what a change would cost. At Clemson, the answer is one of the largest bills in college football. A sum that large can make a school hesitate, but it does not take the question off the table. Any decision about Swinney would cost Clemson tens of millions of dollars, and it would be a choice the school makes on purpose.

Clemson made its promise to Swinney before this season, and after a middling year the school now has to decide whether it still wants to keep it.

Mike Norvell's contract promised him far more protection than his paycheck would suggest. Florida State agreed to a $58.7 million buyout, the sixth-largest among football coaches, even though his salary ranked only 45th in the sport, according to Sports Illustrated. The school wrote the deal to make firing him very costly, not to pay him like one of the game's top earners. A price that large is meant to make a school think hard before it acts. Florida State fired Norvell on the Sunday after a loss to Clemson, The Post and Courier reports, and the size of the bill did nothing to slow the decision. The $58.7 million figure is dated Dec. 1, 2025, and is not a confirmed payout, so what Florida State will end up paying is still unsettled.

What outweighed the price was how far the team had fallen from a recent high point. Florida State went 13-0 in 2023, according to The Post and Courier, and that unbeaten season gave fans every reason for faith. The team has gone 10-23 since then and lost far more often than it won. A record that poor over so short a stretch made keeping Norvell harder to defend than paying him to leave. Once losses pile up at that rate, a buyout stops protecting a coach and becomes a cost the school decides to absorb.

The demand for a change was public before the Clemson game began. Fans at the stadium saw a plane circle overhead towing a banner that read "Fire Mike Novell + Peter Collins," with Norvell's name misspelled. The coach on the other sideline objected to the banner. "Listen, hey, I get it, man. Everybody's got contracts and all this type of stuff, but man, that bothered me," Clemson coach Dabo Swinney said, as reported by The Post and Courier. Florida State faced that pressure in full view of its own fans and its opponent.

For any school weighing whether to fire a losing coach, Florida State shows that even a huge buyout does not stop a school from acting.

Oregon put one of the steepest prices in college football on its coach while the team was at its best. Firing Dan Lanning would have cost $56.7 million as of December 2025, the seventh-largest buyout among football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated. Most of that commitment came after Lanning was hired, because his buyout was $20 million when he first signed, according to ESPN reporter Jake Trotter's figures reported by Bleacher Report. The school promised him far more as the team kept winning, and Oregon reached the College Football Playoff semifinals last January. The $56.7 million is a record of what Oregon chose to commit to Lanning while things went right. It does not show how secure he is when they go wrong.

Oregon now has to weigh that price during a slump. The team fell to 4-3 after losing to Illinois, according to Bleacher Report, which is a sharp drop for a program that played in a playoff semifinal months earlier. Oregon outgained Illinois 426 yards to 314 in that game, and the team moved the ball well but could not turn those yards into a win. It also played without its starting quarterback, Dante Moore, who missed a third straight game with a concussion. With Moore out that long, Oregon cannot yet tell whether the coach or the missing quarterback is more to blame for the losses.

Lanning has acknowledged that his team is falling short. "I think all things are on the table for us to make sure that we're the best version of ourselves, and we're currently not that. That's very clear," Lanning said, as reported by Bleacher Report. For now, Oregon would be weighing the $56.7 million against a coach who admits the team is not playing well and a starting quarterback who has missed three straight games.

At Indiana, the cost of firing Curt Cignetti moved well ahead of what the school was paying him. Indiana agreed to a buyout of $56.7 million, the eighth-largest among college football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated, even though his salary ranked only 18th in the sport. The school promised far more to keep him than it paid him to coach. Indiana used the buyout to show how badly it wanted to hold on to Cignetti, and it made that commitment before his paycheck matched it.

The gap opened because Cignetti won so quickly that other schools had good reason to want him. The $56.7 million figure dates to Dec. 1, 2025, before his biggest season was over. Indiana went on to finish 16-0 with a national title win over Miami, according to The Gazette. Each of those wins made him more costly to lose, and Indiana gave him a raise after the buyout figure was fixed.

The salary gap that put Cignetti in this company has since closed. As of March 2026, he was one of only three active FBS coaches paid $13 million or more a year, along with Georgia's Kirby Smart and Lane Kiffin, according to The Gazette. Indiana once paid him like a mid-tier hire, and it now pays him like one of the sport's elite. The mismatch was a delay rather than a lasting feature of the deal. Indiana wrote the buyout first, and the salary followed.

The $56.7 million measures what Indiana feared losing in late 2025, and the school has paid more to keep him ever since.

Nebraska does not pay Matt Rhule like one of college football's very top coaches, but firing him would cost the school more than almost any other program would pay to replace its coach. His salary ranks only 15th in the sport, yet his $49.6 million buyout is the ninth-largest among football coaches, according to Sports Illustrated. Rhule's contract with Nebraska covered eight years and $74 million in total as of 2023, according to The Associated Press. The length of that promise, not the size of his yearly paycheck, is what places him among the most expensive coaches to fire. The price shows how long Nebraska pledged to stand by its coach more than how highly it values him in any single season.

That protection is strongest at the start of the deal and weakens every year the contract runs, whether or not Nebraska does anything. In November 2023, Rhule's buyout stood at $62 million, according to The Associated Press. As of Dec. 1, 2025, Nebraska would have owed him $49.6 million, a much smaller bill only two years later. Any decision about Rhule becomes cheaper for Nebraska the longer the school waits, so the cost of firing him holds the school back less than its size suggests.

Nebraska wrote that protection on purpose. Trev Alberts, the school's athletic director in 2023, considered long terms and buyout clauses the tools that make it harder for a school to push a coach out, according to The Associated Press. He also saw the risk that those tools create for the school itself. "On the other hand, it certainly does put universities in precarious situations if it's apparent a leadership transition is required," Alberts said.

At Nebraska, the buyout is a bet that the school will never need to break its promise to Rhule, and each year he stays, Nebraska would pay less if it lost that bet.

Illinois has made Bret Bielema one of the most expensive coaches in college football to fire, and the cost runs well ahead of where his pay ranks. His $49.5 million buyout is the 10th-largest among football coaches, while his salary ranks 19th in the sport, according to Sports Illustrated. The cost is high because Illinois has committed to him for a long time, not because it pays him like a star. The News-Gazette reports that Illinois has extended Bielema's contract for a second time and that the deal now runs six years. The price of firing him reflects the school's own decision to renew its bet on Bielema more than any judgment of what other schools would pay him.

Illinois wrote this contract mainly to keep its coach from leaving. Starting July 1, 2026, the school will pay Bielema a retention incentive of $700,000 each year he stays, according to The News-Gazette. "We’ll pay a premium for that kind of certainty for our program and our student-athletes," Josh Whitman, the Illinois athletic director, said. The large firing price is the other side of that premium, because it is what Illinois would owe to give up the certainty it bought.

Bielema's terms are part of a policy that covers much of the Illinois athletic department. The News-Gazette reports that several Illinois coaches have non-compete clauses in their contracts. Whitman says the school wants steady leaders at a time when college sports treats turnover as normal. Buyouts carry weight in college football because schools replace coaches so often, and Illinois has chosen to spend money to avoid doing the same.

At Illinois, $49.5 million is less a fee for firing Bielema than a measure of how hard the school has worked to make sure it never needs to.
