GPT-6 Designed Proteins With Just Reasoning and Code — 35 of 322 Actually Bound Their Targets in the Lab

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

OpenAI just published a preprint with a bold claim: its GPT-6 family of models can design new proteins — their three-dimensional shapes and their amino acid sequences — using only reasoning and general-purpose coding tools, without calling specialized biology models to generate the designs.

The paper is titled "GPT-6 as a Universal Foundation Model for Protein Biology: Beyond Specialized Models." And unlike most capability announcements, this one comes with wet-lab receipts: proteins designed by GPT-6.2 were actually synthesized and tested in a lab, and some of them bound to their intended targets.

Here's what the models can do, what the lab proved, and where the honest limits still are.

Protein design has traditionally been a job for specialized models. Tools like RFdiffusion generate 3D protein backbones; ProteinMPNN proposes amino acid sequences that fold into those shapes. Each tool takes particular inputs, needs particular expertise to run, and can't easily accept new kinds of instructions without software changes.

OpenAI's claim is different: a general-purpose model that does the work of several specialized models, guided by plain language. In the preprint, the researchers could steer designs at scales ranging from a single bond between two atoms to the overall shape of a protein — just by changing the prompt, with no retraining.

For example, they asked GPT-6.2 to design small proteins called minibinders and marked exactly where each one should attach to its target, then specified how many coils or strands each minibinder should contain. An internal research model followed those instructions with near-perfect fidelity in its 3D designs.

The model's written reasoning shows it drawing on math, physics, chemistry, and biology at the same time. Designing a minibinder for IL-7Rα, it reasoned about how the two proteins might interact, then wrote and executed code to explore different shapes and amino acid choices that could support that interaction.

Computational benchmarks are one thing. OpenAI partnered with research lab Onava to test whether GPT-6.2's designs work in the real world.

The setup: GPT-6.2 was given the 3D structures of five target proteins, with the attachment site marked, and wrote code to create 3D minibinder designs — without using specialized protein AI models. A specialized model called SolubleMPNN then suggested amino acid sequences for those designs, and other specialized models helped select candidates for testing.

The results: 322 minibinders were tested in the lab, and 35 of them (10.9%) showed binding to their intended target — 5 hits for PD-L1 (a protein some cancers use to evade the immune system), 3 for IL-7Rα, 1 for TrkA, 8 for VEGF-A, and 18 for IL-17A.

That hit rate is lower than the original RFdiffusion study reported, and OpenAI says so plainly. Hit rates depend heavily on how strictly researchers filter designs before lab validation and on how binding is defined. But the point stands: designs produced by a general-purpose model's reasoning turned into proteins that bind their targets in a lab.

A second experiment tested whether GPT-6.2 could design the sequences itself. Researchers gave it 3D models of existing minibinders bound to their targets, withheld the original amino acid sequences, and asked it to propose new ones.

The proposed sequences often differed substantially from the originals — a median of 50% different for VEGF-A. And for three unpublished minibinder shapes, 14 of 107 proteins made from GPT-6.2's new sequences (13.1%) showed binding. Because those structures were unpublished and the sequences were substantially different, OpenAI argues the capability goes beyond memorization.

Antibodies got the same treatment. Starting with six small antibodies (VHHs) that bind IL-4Rα, GPT-6.2 designed new binding-loop sequences with the loops removed: 128 of 182 designs (70%) showed signs of binding. Then, redesigning about half the amino acids in the binding loops of five therapeutic antibodies, 179 of 272 fragments (66%) retained binding to their original targets, PD-1 or PD-L1.

This is where the paper is refreshingly honest — and where the story gets more interesting than the headline.

Full structure prediction is still a clear weakness. Given only an amino acid sequence, GPT-6.2's internal successor scored an average TM-score of 0.38 for predicting how the chain folds (1.0 is perfect; above 0.5 generally captures the overall fold). The specialized model ESMFold2 averaged 0.93. That's a substantial gap, not a rounding error.

The campaign still leaned on specialists. OpenAI notes its protein design campaign relied on specialized structure-prediction models such as Boltz-2, OpenDDE, and ESMFold2 to filter candidates. The results establish intrinsic capability at two stages — backbone generation (where researchers use RFdiffusion) and sequence redesign (where they use ProteinMPNN) — but they do not yet establish that the models can generate de novo minibinders end-to-end, zero-shot, without help from specialized models.

Some benchmark numbers need careful reading. In tests asking models to infer sequences from structures, unusually high accuracy may partly reflect memorization or artifacts of how those structures were generated. Those results alone don't establish reliable performance on unfamiliar proteins.

That said, on the component tasks the numbers are strong: on MotifBench (building a protein around a specified structural motif), the latest internal model succeeded on 43.7% of designs versus 27.8% for RFdiffusion. On small-molecule docking (PoseBusters), GPT-6.2 matched the observed position and orientation in 93% of scored predictions, versus 50% for the specialized DiffDock-L. And when asked to rank 50 mutated versions of a protein by stability, each model generation improved — from a rank correlation of 0.41 for GPT-5.6 to 0.66 for the latest internal model.

Strip away the biochemistry, and the pattern is familiar to anyone building with general-purpose models: a capability that used to require a pipeline of specialized tools, each with its own input format and expertise, is collapsing into a prompt.

OpenAI calls the direction programmable molecular design: scientists describing what they want a molecule to do in plain language, and models designing candidates to test in the lab. The stated next steps are harder targets, designing for what happens after binding (like killing a cancer cell, not just attaching to one), and extending beyond minibinders to multi-target molecules, RNA, small molecules, and PROTACs.

The lab results also sharpen the safety conversation. The same capabilities that help design proteins for new medicines could be misused, and OpenAI says its Preparedness Framework guides how it assesses those risks. Binding is only the first step toward a useful medicine — specificity, stability, biological activity, safety, and efficacy all still need to be established.

But the direction of travel is clear: the model that writes your code is learning to write biology too. The question for the next few years isn't whether general-purpose models can do protein design — it's how much of the specialized pipeline they absorb, and how fast.

Source: OpenAI, "GPT-6 models demonstrate intrinsic protein-design capabilities" (preprint: "GPT-6 as a Universal Foundation Model for Protein Biology: Beyond Specialized Models"), published November 24, 2026.
