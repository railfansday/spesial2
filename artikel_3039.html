​AI Is Growing Fast. The Chief AI Officer Role Is Not. Yet

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Rob Sloan is VP of Cybersecurity Advocacy at Zscaler, where he helps organizations navigate cybersecurity risk and business resilience.

​AI now touches every part of the business. Far from simply being a technology initiative, AI is now a governance issue, an operating issue, a board-level priority and, increasingly, a security issue.

Given its importance, you would be forgiven for expecting most companies to have clearly assigned executive accountability for AI. Many probably do. But that accountability is not yet visible publicly in many cases.​

To find evidence that a company had publicly named a C-level executive with “AI” in the title, I reviewed press releases, SEC filings, company websites and public appointment announcements for every S&P 500 constituent. Only 75 organizations, or 15% of the index, publicly have one.​

That may be about to change. Regulation is taking shape, the threat landscape is shifting quickly, and boards and executive teams have a clearer view of both the opportunities and the risks AI creates across the business.

The White House has added to that momentum by requiring federal agencies to designate chief AI officers, signaling that AI governance is moving toward more visible executive accountability.​

Of the 75 companies in the dataset, 36 use the stand-alone chief AI officer title. Another 18 use chief AI and data officer.​

My findings contrast sharply with those reported in IBM’s 2026 CEO Study earlier this year. That research drew on an international sample of responses from 2,000 chief executives, 76% of whom said their organization had a chief AI officer. The reason for the gap is not obvious from the published findings alone. However, the IBM research starts with a different dataset than mine and may include companies that haven’t publicly announced the role.​

From my own research, the split between stand-alone chief AI officer roles and blended AI titles points to a deeper governance question: Where does AI actually belong inside the company?​

It makes sense that 18 companies blend chief AI and data officers into one role. AI depends on data pipelines, governance, access controls and quality, and many companies already have a C-suite leader with responsibility for those areas. Four companies created chief technology and AI officer positions, three fused AI and digital, and one company alone had a chief information and AI officer.​

There is also a practical reason companies might hesitate to create a new title: cost. Adding a C-level role is expensive once compensation, recruitment, onboarding and organizational change are counted, which helps explain why many organizations add AI to an existing remit.​

However, when AI sits inside a broader data, technology or operations brief, accountability can blur just as the stakes are rising.​

In my view, the S&P 500 companies without visible accountability in the C-suite fall into three broad categories:​

• First are companies where AI may already be embedded deeply enough in product, engineering or platform leadership that a separate chief AI officer does not yet seem necessary. This appears to be the case for technology companies in particular.​

• Second, and probably the largest group, are companies where AI responsibility exists but sits inside another executive’s remit—often under the CIO, CISO, CDO, CTO or chief digital officer—without a title change or a dedicated budget.​

• Third are companies that still do not appear to treat AI as a distinct C-suite issue, which includes utilities, real estate companies, commodity producers and materials companies.

AI ownership often exists in each of these categories but is either not clearly signaled or not present in the C-suite. That position gets harder to defend as AI use spreads through employee workflows, vendor products, customer interactions and internal decision-making.​

Security is one reason this will not stay optional. My company’s recent ThreatLabz study based on hundreds of enterprise AI security readiness assessments found that 91% of organizations had publicly reachable AI infrastructure, while none had identity-based access controls in place.

As AI systems become connected to enterprise data and workflows, the potential impact of misuse or external compromise grows. To reduce the likelihood of chaos spreading at machine speed, companies will need clearer ownership of guardrails, approvals and risk.​

Regulation is also a factor. The EU AI Act is moving from policy into enforcement, with penalties of up to 35 million euros or 7% of global revenue, turning AI governance into a practical compliance issue. Not every company will need a stand-alone chief AI officer, but more will need to show who is accountable for how AI is deployed, governed and secured.​

I expect the chief AI officer role, whether stand-alone or combined with another C-level function, to grow quickly. The closest precedent is the rise of the chief information security officer.​

For years, security sat within broader IT leadership, but over time that became less tenable. The CISO role emerged as companies recognized that cybersecurity required clear ownership: The risk was too material, the accountability too important and the board-level implications too obvious. AI now appears to be moving along a similar path, albeit at a much faster pace.​

Companies still have time to decide what AI leadership should look like on their own terms, but they may not have much time left to treat clear executive accountability as optional.​​
