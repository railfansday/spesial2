I see your logic, but I am skeptical for the following reasons:
1. If you leverage when VIX is high, you (arguably) leverage when risk is high. (Let's assume for a moment that the market doesn't just fluctuate more out of thin air, but that there is a risk that the market see, as is usually the case, for example the Street of Hormuz is being closed, or some war starts. Let's also assume that you are not smarter than the rest of the market in assessing risk.) So yes, you may think you "win", as long as the backtesting timeframe (or the timeframe during which you run this strategy) does not have tail risk events (large drawdowns). But you incur more tail risk, whether or not it materializes. So one day VIX is high, you add leverage, and markets keep dropping... eventually you have to de-leverage (possibly near the market bottom). Ouch! So yes you (or may not) win, and possibly you even "win" asymptotically in the long run, but with more risk. We had many similar discussion in the mHFEA thread.
2. Some quant strategies do more or less the opposite: re-leverage when VIX is low, de-leverage when VIX is high. The logic is that they try to keep constant risk exposure. You would be doing the opposite. Ouch! I guess this is just another way of expressing (1.)

I would like to see a paper or rigorous study that shows that VIX based leverage strategies are superior to valuation based leverage strategies. I suspect that both have similar results, simply because VIX is correlated to sharp drawdowns, which usually come with valuation compression. I also think both might result in superior ex-ante returns vs. a passive index, if one avoids over-leveraging and applies rigorous risk management.
Re: your #1 counterpoint:
- I am not concerned about tail events because my risk management framework (10% max leverage in a PM account) eliminates any risk of forced selling at the bottom (at 15% required maintenance margin with 10% leverage, SPY would have to drop close to 90% to force a margin call. Even with anticipating a broker-related increase in house margin requirement to 30% during times of crisis/high IV, SPY would still have to drop 87% to force a margin call in my scenario).
- I am not saying my strategy (levering up on SPY 10% during VIX spikes) will produce better risk-adjusted returns, but I AM saying it will highly likely produce much better absolute returns compared to a 1X buy and hold SPY strategy. It makes sense that your Sharpe would decrease with this strategy (esp in the short-term) because you are going long volatility, inevitably decreasing Sharpe due to high standard deviation values in the denominator. I am capturing the volatility risk-premium.

The main power in this low-leverage VIX strategy is simply time arbitrage, since as the chart I posted above shows, the predictive power of the VIX for forecasting superior forward SPY returns is strongest at >6 month holding periods. This fact is also confirmed in this paper:

https://www.researchgate.net/publicatio ... ssertation

They found: "The VIX demonstrates statistically significant predictive power for S&P 500 returns across all examined horizons, with relationships strengthening substantially at longer forecasting periods. At the 6-month horizon, a one-point increase in VIX predicts 0.42 percentage points higher future returns (p < 0.001), with an explanatory power of 12.99%."

That paper also discusses a potential trading strategy where they increase exposure during high VIX and decrease exposure during low VIX (essentially opposite your #2 point) which they show did NOT have good returns. I would never employ this strategy since you are essentially excluding yourself from bull markets when VIX is traditionally very low.

The cherry on top with using short box spreads to finance the leverage is that they produce capital losses via 1256 contracts, so my after-tax borrowing rate is currently closer to 2.75% on a 4.1% nominal short box rate.
You are effectively doing a version of this, depending on the details how you define the leverage ratio trigger points, and your average exposure. Like I said, I think valuations and/or VIX (I still think both are roughly equivalent in practice) should ideally be an input to a leveraged strategy, and will lead to excess expected returns, if average exposure, min exposure, and max exposure are carefully selected. Regime changes as depicted in the paper that I linked are a risk to the strategy, which does however not invalidate using valuations as input altogether.
