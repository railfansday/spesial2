I am advised 70% stocks - but don't really like bonds and iffy about international for the other 30% based on my experience at EJ.
Not all investments are winners all the time, so if that's why you don't like bonds and int'l, that's not a good reason. Investing often means taking some risk for an expected/average reward that may be more or less once realized than the average expectation. Diversification is a way to mitigate that risk through an uncorrelated asset (bonds) or broader exposure (int'l stock is ~40% of the world market), but it is not a guaranteed risk-reduction (it simply is recommended based on the historical long-term record). Making decisions on short-term data (anything less than a 20-year sequence) is going suffer recency bias and likely lead you to bad conclusions that don't hold up for the multiple decades you'll likely have remaining before you both pass.

I'll also say that the traditional portfolio is stocks & bonds for the long-run, not stocks & cash as the latter just lowers your overall return through long-term Cash Drag. The desired Asset Allocation of 70/30 with 30% of stocks in int'l is something you should tailor to your risk-tolerance. Figure out the right AA for you & your spouse first as it's the blue-print for self-managing your portfolio. Consider one or more of the three exercises below to identify the right AA for you & your spouse (each of you should do this, then use an average for your unified portfolio).

Control Your Risk
1) Read the Wiki article for Assessing Risk Tolerance, take the Vanguard Investor Questionnaire, then tailor the asset allocation (AA) that was recommended by the quiz based on your knowledge of your personal risk tolerance having read the Wiki article.

2) Alternatively (or in addition to), ask "How much of a drop in portfolio value as a % of total value can I handle?" cut that % in half to get standard deviation, then lookup that std. dev. on the X-Axis of the chart below, and finally scan up to see what AA that corresponds to. As an example, if you can only stomach a -24% drop in portfolio value, that's a ±12% std. dev, which corresponds to an AA of 60/40. The return you get is an average and you'll get what you get with your unique sequence of returns (there's a lot of variance in outcomes due to the associated volatility of stocks so it probably will NOT be the average, but something more or less).

a. For a long time-frame (>10 years) AAs below 20% stock are dominated (red dots) by another AA with similar risk but higher reward (blue dots).
b. The dotted line represents a hypothetical linear risk-reward from 100% stocks down to 100% bonds; the historical risk-reward curve has an improvement for risk-adjusted return due to the lack of correlation between stocks & bonds.

3) Vanguard's Target Date Retirement Glide-Path Design is a also a good starting point for choosing an AA. The choice of a specific TDF year should not solely be based on age, but could be earlier or later than your expected retirement age based on your personal risk-tolerance. You're not necessarily picking a specific TDF fund, you're just getting an idea of how to change your AA over time to apply to your specific fund choices (e.g., the proportions of each fund in a 3-Fund Portfolio).


Generic guidance for asset classes by time-frame to the specific investment goal:
<5 years: Cash: (MMF, HYSA, CDs, T-Bills)
5-10 years: Cash/Bonds or Bonds/Stocks (exact % depends on risk tolerance)
>10 years: Stocks/Bonds (exact % depends on risk tolerance)

Your time-frame for a retirement portfolio is long (i.e., remaining life-expectancy of you or spouse, whichever is longer, so this is best invested in majority stocks & minority bonds), while the time-frame for your emergency fund is short (could be tapped at any time since you don't know when a lay-off might happen, so EF is all in cash).

On the 30% of stocks in int'l your advisor suggested, that's mid-way between typical suggestions I see here of 20% (minimum for int'l diversification, if you want less just don't use any at all as it's just clutter, not enough to diversify from a US-only position) up to 40% (about the world-cap weighting that many Target Date Funds from Vanguard, Fidelity, and Schwab all use).

Value of Int'l Diversity from US

In my view (not a consensus), there's essentially two camps among Bogleheads: a) Those that are on board with the global market cap weighting, which is about 60% US stock and 40% ex-US stock; and b) those that have a home bias (US will usually outperform), which is about 80% US stock and 20% ex-US stock (some even omit Int'l altogether and <20% is just clutter, not enough to meaningfully diversify). Stocks & Bonds vs Stocks & Cash

In the long-term, lack of correlation matters more than expected volatility... in the upper chart both fictious funds A and B have high volatility of ±19.7%, but since they have perfect negative correlation of -1.00 they have zero volatility when paired together in equal amounts. There's no such investment with negative correlation & two positive returns, but even uncorrelated assets (e.g., stocks & bonds) provide a higher return than bonds alone, for a similar risk as bonds alone (e.g., 50/50 @ 9% return > 0/100 @ 7% return, but volatility is still only around ±9.5% for both in the lower chart). Cash is also uncorrelated with stocks but the long-term return on 10y T-Notes (bonds) from 1928-2017 is 5.2±7.7% while for 3m T-Bills (cash) is 3.4±3.0%, so the higher return of bonds is preferred over cash for a long-term portfolio since the expected return matters more given both are uncorrelated with stocks (thus stocks & bonds, not stocks & cash).

Can you advise me on how to rebalance?
Yes, but that will depend on the AA you choose. Just as an example of 70/30 with 30% of stocks in int'l, this is how I'd restructure your portfolio to consolidate accounts and simplify holdings.

Your Current layout has a whopping 32 holdings (very complex to assess and rebalance annually!) and the AA is about 38% stocks with the rest in cash and gold (and a trivial amount of bonds). Holding nearly 40% of your portfolio in cash is like stuffing it under the mattress rather than investing for your future with stocks & bonds; it's not going to grow meaningfully and there's a serious risk of not even keeping pace with inflation. Fidelity's great customer service seems to be paid for by those holding cash there as it's the highest expense ratio for Money Market Funds (MMFs) among Van (lowest), Schwab (middle), and Fidelity (highest-cost, lowest yield). Nominal cash in Taxable is not good Tax-Efficient Fund Placement; ideally all your bonds & cash would be in Trad tax-deferred accounts, leaving Taxable at 100% stocks (max tax-efficiency) and Roth at 100% stocks (max tax-free earning attribute). So bonds & cash in Taxable and Roth have been highlighted in purple as not tax-efficient. There are a number of high-cost funds with an Expense Ratio (ER) greater than 0.30%, which I'd normally flag in yellow highlight, but these same high cost funds are in both Taxable and in other accounts which can result in Wash Sales if you ever sell one of these holdings in red at a loss in Taxable and inadvertently buy the same holding in Roth within ±30 days (can happen without auto-reinvest turned on or direct monthly purchases). There are also a smattering of individual stocks and gold, highlighted in blue, which are speculative gambles against the broad market index that (like in gambling) sometimes pay off but usually the house wins and you lose. Fortunately, the issues in the Roth accounts can be fixed with no tax-consequence, but fixing Taxable will incur a tax bill.

The Proposed layout is based on a simple, yet effective 3-Fund Portfolio of US stock, Int'l stock, and US bonds, with a small cash reserve in T-Bills that are at least exempt from state tax (still not ideal in Taxable but butter than any Fido MMF due to the comparatively low cost of SGOV). All the Taxable accounts were consolidated to a single joint account invested in US stock (FSKAX), int'l stock (FSPSX), and 5% of the overall portfolio in cash (T-Bills via SGOV). The Trad 401Ks were filled with US bonds as the preferred location to hold bonds (FXNAX). His Roth IRA was simplified to just US stock (FZROX to avoid wash sales with FSKAX in Taxable) and bonds (FXNAX). Her Roth IRA was simplified to just FZROX. That exactly meets an AA of 70/25/5 with 30% of stocks in int'l and greatly simplifies from 32 holdings down to 8. Again your desired AA may not be 70% stocks with 30% of stocks in int'l, but hopefully this illustrates how to restructure your portfolio to both simplify and meet your new AA.
