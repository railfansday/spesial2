At 01:50 on November 2, Beget posted a short message in its Telegram channel: a "major incident." A few hours later, it said the problem affected every layer of its architecture and that engineers were reconfiguring infrastructure by hand inside the data centers. Around midday it published an official force majeure notice. By Beget's own count, its cloud servers host more than 450,000 websites.

According to that notice, dedicated servers were back, DNS (the NS servers) was the top priority, email had no timeline, and the company could not yet give a date for everything else. Beget names a cyberattack as the cause, and the Coordination Center for .RU/.РФ, which runs the zone, says the same. No technical details have been published. Replying to customers in its official social media group, Beget says its legal position on services and consequences will be set out after the investigation ends.

Any host can have an outage like this. What made it worse for some customers was where their domain lived.

I saw it on two sites, an online store and a content site. Both had hosting and the domain at Beget. Everything stopped at once, and moving out wasn't an option either: the domain is managed from the same control panel that wouldn't load.

Moving a site has two halves. Bring it up on a new server, then tell the domain where the site lives now. The second half happens at the registrar, where the name servers are set.

Run a whois lookup on your domain and check two fields: the registrar and the name servers:

One login for site, backups, DNS, email and domain is convenient until the outage, when it cuts you off from all of them at once.

So in practice: check the expiry date in WHOIS, keep dated records of every renewal attempt, and write to both the registrar and the registry if the date passes. Better, renew early. For .RU you can file up to 60 days ahead (clause 4.4). Look up the equivalent rules for your own TLD before you need them.

You have a recent backup outside the host. The good case. Rent a VPS elsewhere, set up the stack, restore files and the database dump, test by IP or through your hosts file, then switch DNS. Before you switch, run through forms, cart and checkout. Plan around size: a 1C-Bitrix store (a CMS widely used for online stores in Russia) with a 100,000+ product catalog takes me about one working day to move. A small WordPress site goes faster.

You have the code but no recent database. Code in Git redeploys anywhere; it holds no state. A store doesn't work without its database, since products, orders and customers live there. What you can do is put up a temporary storefront on the same templates: contacts, main pages, an inquiry form that sends to email or a chat. That keeps paid traffic from landing on a connection error.

You have nothing. Pause your ads first, or the budget keeps paying for an error page. Put a contact page on any working host if you control the domain. The Wayback Machine can give back the text of pages it crawled, with no database, cart or accounts. Google's `cache:` operator was retired in September 2024, so that old trick is gone.

When the host is down, its backups are down with it. In the same notice Beget says file backups exist for the vast majority of shared hosting and cloud resources, but restoring from them "will take a long time" because of the volume of data. The copies exist; you get them when your turn in the queue comes.

Both are defined in NIST SP 800-34, the federal contingency planning guide.

You shrink RPO by copying more often. On PostgreSQL, continuous WAL archiving gives point-in-time recovery, and `archive_timeout` caps how stale unarchived data can get (the docs call about a minute reasonable). You shrink RTO only by restoring on a clean server and timing it.

AWS suggests testing in governance mode first. And a write-only key can still upload junk that also gets locked, so watch bucket size.

For ransomware, assume whoever gets into the server gets every key on it. So the key on the server can only `PutObject`, default retention locks every upload automatically, and a separate admin account that never touches production manages the bucket.

`alert` is whatever pings you: a chatbot, email, a pager. Once a month, pull the latest backup onto a clean server, decrypt, restore into a separate database, open the site, and time it start to finish. That's the "0" in 3-2-1-1-0, and the number you get is your actual RTO.

If the answer to the last one is "never," you don't know your RTO, and you'll find it out during the next outage.
