What is the 'Temple' device Shubman Gill wore during IND vs NZ 1st ODI in Auckland? ICC rules on advertising brand devices during matches explored

Shubman Gill sported the Temple device on the side of his head during India vs New Zealand 1st ODI in Auckland.

Gill wore the device for the opening few overs of the first match of the series.

Indian bowlers dominated New Zealand in the first innings of the 1st ODI at Eden Park.

The 'Temple' device that Shubman Gill wore at the start of the first ODI against New Zealand in Auckland is a small wearable stuck to the side of the forehead, and it is designed to track blood flow in the brain.

Gill has worn the gadget throughout the tour of New Zealand and was also seen with it during his training sessions. It drew far more notice on Wednesday, however, once India and New Zealand took the field at Eden Park.

Gill is not the only Indian athlete to have tried it. Badminton star PV Sindhu wore the same device during the World Championships in New Delhi in August, so it has already appeared on a major stage in another sport.

The sight has also raised a fair question about whether such a device is allowed in international cricket, and this report explains what Temple is, what it claims to measure and what the ICC's rules say about wearable technology and branding.

Temple is a wearable made by a team associated with Zomato co-founder Deepinder Goyal. It sits on the side of the forehead near the temple and is built to track cerebral blood flow along with other physiological signals linked to how the brain is working.

The device relies on optical sensors and a system called BrainFlow. In simple terms, the technology is meant to pick up changes in blood flow inside the brain through signals read from the side of the forehead.

Beyond blood flow, the device also aims to follow signals tied to oxygenation and cognitive load. That means it tries to gauge how the body and brain respond when mental effort goes up, which is a situation that matters a great deal for a player batting or captaining in a pressure game.

Temple also produces what it calls an Entropy score, which is meant to show physiological strain. Because that strain can move with exercise, sleep and stress, the score reflects several factors at once and should not be read as a sign of any single condition.

Wearable gadgets in international matches come under the ICC's Player and Match Officials Area (PMOA) rules and its Playing Conditions. Trackers that do not communicate, such as telemetry or performance monitors used to keep an eye on workload, are generally allowed for internal monitoring.

Real-time two-way data transmission, or any communication function, is strictly prohibited during live play. A device that sends information out or receives it while a match is in progress would therefore fall on the wrong side of the rules.

Branding is covered separately by the ICC Clothing and Equipment Regulations. These permit only pre-approved manufacturer and commercial logos, and the logos must meet strictly stated size limits on playing gear and apparel.

Showing an unapproved commercial logo, or using non-compliant equipment on the field, can breach the ICC's advertising and equipment guidelines.

Wearable technology is moving into sport faster than the rule books can keep up, and the attention on Gill's forehead device shows why governing bodies should address it.

A non-communicating tracker for internal monitoring is one thing, but a gadget that carries a visible brand raises a different question altogether, because the same object can be a health tool and an advertisement at once.

Fans will also want to know whether such devices give anyone an edge, and a short public explanation from the ICC would settle the matter far better than a week of speculation on social media. Players deserve the same clarity, since they should not have to guess whether a piece of equipment is permitted before they walk out to play.
