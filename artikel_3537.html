The science fiction genre has always prided itself on bringing stories that seem impossible to adapt to life. Although the genre has thrived for myriad reasons, one of the most important aspects is how wellsci-fi shows build their worlds. Several series have made this seem easy, when it is anything but. The Expanse, Battlestar Galactica, Foundation, and other sci-fi franchises have remained so influential in the space because they understand that a series with a world that doesn’t work won't grip audiences for long.

Prime Video could soon have another ambitious space opera on its hands that has the potential to surpass the likes of sci-fi giants like The Expanse and Battlestar Galactica. However, it has to get several elements of its source material right to achieve that admirable feat. In late 2024, Prime Video announced they would adapt James S.A. Corey’s expansive novel series, The Captive’s War, into a TV show.

For fans of The Expanse, this news was welcome, but it also brought the genre-typical fear that the source material would be poorly adapted. However, The Captive's War could be Prime Video’s next flagship sci-fi series because it could give the streaming platform a chance to explore a different side of the genre. The source material for The Captive’s War series indicates that it is simply not going to be another version of The Expanse, but a TV show with an entirely new universe.

Prime Video’s adaptation of The Captive’s War could completely change how sci-fi is brought to life on streaming platforms. Following humanity after the alien Carryx empire invades the planet Anjiin and abducts a group of humans, The Captive’s War presents a staggering world.

The first book in the series, The Mercy of Gods, is a slow burn and takes a while to get going, but once it does, the story takes on a life of its own. Corey’s novels feature a variety of alien planets, rich biology, and strange aliens, particularly the Carryx. The novels’ extensive world creates a strong foundation for worldbuilding because some of these aliens are so fascinating. The Carryx, in particular, represents an entire civilization, social structures, and history that make Corey’s story even more interesting.

With the amount of information in the source material, it's kind of difficult to imagine how it will all come together. Even The Captive's War ​​​​​​authors Daniel Abraham and Ty Franck have acknowledged that the books feature spectacular alien species and complicated internal character perspectives that will make the television adaptation challenging. However, there is some hope. Prime Video’s adaptation of The Captive’s War is stacked with an incredible creative team.

Naren Shankar, who served as showrunner on The Expanse, and director Breck Eisner, who also worked on the space opera, will helm the adaptation of The Captive’s War. Shankar is attached as writer and showrunner, Eisner as director, while Abraham and Franck are also involved in writing the adaptation. They've already proven their mettle with The Expanse, so this creative dream team should help to quell book fans' fears about the adaptation.

Given that The Captive’s War and The Expanse were written by the same author, comparisons between the novels are inevitable. Though both stories demonstrate a perfect understanding of science fiction worldbuilding, they approach the concept from very different perspectives. Corey's hard sci-fi novel focuses primarily on humanity's expansion across the solar system, whereas The Captive's War imagines an enormous galactic civilization.

Political worldbuilding is the main reason The Expanse became so compelling, particularly through its exploration of the competing interests of Earth, Mars, and the Belt. Arguably, The Captive's War’s political maneuvering is even more interesting, especially because the story explores an alien civilization built around conquest, domination, and the exploitation of other species.

All in all, The Captive's War’s setting is much larger than The Expanse and has more diverse characters. Despite the contrasts between these two stories, it is easy to agree that the worldbuilding in both of Corey’s literary works is what sets them apart.
