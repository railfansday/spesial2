Review: Wild Blue Skies (Switch) - An Unoriginal Alternative To Star Fox 64

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Wild Blue Skies is Star Fox 64-lite. Gameplay, identity, story: everything is pretty much a license-free version of Star Fox 64. That isn’t necessarily a bad thing, but in a year with Star Fox (2026), a superb remake of the N64 classic, the question immediately becomes: Who is Wild Blue Skies for?

Stop me if this sounds familiar. Chuhai Labs' game — which, it should be said, was in development and announced long before the Switch 2 Star Fox remake was publicly known — stars a rag-tag gang of four pilots who, after being recruited by a general, need to travel across the skies to stop an evil exiled engineer from world domination.

This is unequivocally a carbon copy of Star Fox’s plot: Bowie, Chuck, Thorne, and Roe are your team Star Fox equivalent; The Prowlers are your Star Wolf stand-ins; and Grimclaw and Andross are practically the same one-note villain. Even dialogue and story beats are presented in the same iconic square-window boxes.

But where Star Fox shines in its character interactions and iconic lines, Wild Blue Skies falters. There is no personality, no charming moments, nothing to set the Blue Bombers apart from their Nintendo counterparts. They are all simply there because Star Fox 64 said so, leading to some of the most unmemorable interactions in recent memory.

Gameplay-wise, I could easily have copy-pasted the section from Jim's review of Star Fox (2026), and it would be equally applicable. The gameplay is virtually identical. You play as Bowie in his not-Arwing on a seven-stage adventure as you make your way to the evil Grimclaw’s base across the world map. Levels are predominantly on-rails as you and your teammates shoot down enemy after enemy until you reach the stage’s boss.

Along the way, some stages have secret objectives, leading to branching paths that change your path to the game’s final boss. And of course, a few stages shift the gameplay into all-range mode, giving you full 360-degree control in a dogfighting arena. Really, the only elements missing are the Landmaster and Blue Marine.

From controls, power-ups, level navigation, and even stage objectives, it really feels like the developers are one barrel-roll away from the Nintendo ninjas bursting in through their door. It also means that every complaint someone has with the original Star Fox 64 is likely present here, too.

The game is incredibly short, likely only taking you around two hours to beat. Your teammates sometimes can get in the way, just like Slippy famously does. And the difficulty can be incredibly punishing, as losing all your lives means starting over from scratch. Nothing here is inherently bad, but the lack of originality here is truly staggering.

Really, the only change to Star Fox worth mentioning (besides the absence of the other vehicles) comes in the level scoring system. Like the original, you gain points for every enemy you shoot down, but that won’t be enough to earn you the stage’s medal. Instead, you also have four “commendations” to complete as well: no damage taken, fast boss defeat, full squad survival, and collecting the four WILD letters hidden in the stage.

Achieving each commendation gives you an extra 50 points on top of your score, and completing at least a few of those in each stage is likely required to achieve the stage’s medal. In theory, this adds more replay value on such a short game, but in reality, it complicates Star Fox 64’s system that already worked well enough.

However, if there is one aspect of Wild Blue Skies that stands out, it’s in the production value. Visually, it uses a cell-shaded look that arguably looks better at times than Velan Studios' Switch 2 remake. The colours are incredibly vibrant and a treat to look at, as are some of the stage environments. While it would have been great to see some more environmental objects, like buildings or trees, what is there is sufficient.

The game runs incredibly well on Switch 2 hardware (although this is a Switch 1 release), and it’s also worth commending that all dialogue is completely voice-acted and features a fully animated intro cutscene.

Wild Blue Skies is what happens when a spiritual successor takes too much inspiration from its predecessor. There is nothing inherently wrong with the game, but if you have played Star Fox 64, or the remake, you have played Wild Blue Skies. As this is releasing on the original Switch, there is an argument to be made that it might tide over Star Fox fans who don’t have a Switch 2 yet. But with the original widely accessible on Nintendo Switch Online, it’s hard to see that argument holding much weight.

Wild Blue Skies is a perfectly average game, but with no originality, it makes for a hard recommendation, even to the most dedicated fans of Lylat's finest.
