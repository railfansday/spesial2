The art of protection - inside an F1 helmet factory

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

As soft, instrumental opera music plays in the background of a precise temperature-controlled room in northern Italy, Thomaso is using a children's toy ball and hairdryer to perform his meticulous work.

Hugely focused, in a crisp white coat, he moulds a precious sticky black material over and over again for up to six hours.

One day, his fastidiousness may save the life of one of the best athletes in the world.

The room Thomaso works in is called "the magic shop". Its motto? 'One man, one shell.'

Much of what happens inside is top secret. What we can say is the black material Thomaso is working with is a blend of carbon fibre that has to be stored at exactly -16°C. He moulds it into a secret form, layer by layer, after which the entire unit is baked in a vacuum bag at a set pressure at 120C for three-and-a-half hours.

The handmade products he is working on are personally chosen by their clients - for their safety features, as well as comfort, practicality and look.

Thomaso's colleague Sven travels the world between February and December to keep those clients happy. A different country almost every week, with a reliable weather forecast almost as important as a large allowance of hand luggage.

Together with 32 colleagues, Thomaso and Sven work for one of the four leading manufacturers of F1 helmets.

They produce sophisticated engineering masterpieces, and works of art.

"Our philosophy is to make the best product for the best drivers," says chief executive Alberto Dall'Oglio.

In the early days of grand prix racing, protection was rudimentary. Drivers often wore little more than leather flying caps, goggles and - eventually - relatively basic open-face helmets.

Safety standards were inconsistent, materials primitive and the consequences of an accident frequently devastating.

According to the official F1 website, Giuseppe Farina - the first winner of an F1 race and first world champion - competed wearing nothing more than "his trusty cloth cap, two pairs of goggles (he wore the spare pair round his neck), cotton dungarees, leather boots and leather gloves".

The arrival of the full-face helmet in the late 1960s marked a defining moment. Bell introduced its pioneering Star model in 1966, and Dan Gurney became the first driver to race in a full-face helmet in 1968. The face - previously exposed to flying debris, fire and impact - finally had meaningful protection.

Progress was painfully slow, and motorsport's darkest moments repeatedly exposed the limitations of the technology.

In 1976, Niki Lauda's Ferrari erupted into flames at the Nurburgring. His helmet came loose, leaving his head exposed to the fire. He survived, but suffered severe burns and lifelong injuries. The accident became a defining lesson in the importance of helmet retention, fire resistance and the protection of the driver's entire head.

The tragic deaths of Roland Ratzenberger and Ayrton Senna at Imola in 1994, followed by Jules Bianchi's fatal accident at Suzuka in 2014, demonstrated even increasingly sophisticated helmets could not eliminate every danger.

Ratzenberger suffered a fatal basilar skull fracture; Senna succumbed to multiple fractures of the skull base, after a strut from the suspension pierced his helmet.

Bianchi's accident exposed the vulnerability of drivers' heads to heavy external impacts. Helmets are only one part of a much wider safety system, which now includes stronger survival cells, the Hans device and the halo.

In 2004, the FIA introduced its new helmet safety standard, requiring advanced carbon-fibre construction to deliver greater strength, improved impact protection and reduced weight for F1 drivers.

At Bahrain's Sakhir circuit in 2020, Romain Grosjean's Haas split apart against the barriers and burst into flames. After 28 seconds trapped in the wreckage, he emerged with burns to his hands and ankles. His Bell helmet, fire-resistant equipment and the halo were instrumental in saving his life - a powerful testament to decades of safety innovation and engineering.

"Today's helmets are withstanding up to 30 seconds at 700, even 800, degrees," says Dall'Oglio. "Then they have to self-extinguish within 15 seconds."

Dall'Oglio is the CEO of Schuberth Performance, Thomaso an engineer and Sven a racing service technician, working trackside at more than 400 grands prix, preparing helmets and visors for rain, sun or floodlights.

Needing to avoid any impact on the helmets, he must carry them - sometimes five or six at a time - to races.

Schuberth, a German firm, has a long history in head protection. Founded in 1922, the company began making industrial products before moving into helmet linings in the late 1920s, then mining and motorcycle helmets in the 1950s.

With its HQ in Magdeburg and F1 helmet production in Schio, northern Italy, it produces special helmets for military forces - made of Kevlar, and some of a similar carbon - in the same factory as the F1 helmets.

Its motorsport credentials grew considerably around the turn of the millennium.

Following Michael Schumacher's British Grand Prix accident in 1999, Schuberth developed a new generation of racing helmets, subsequently worn by Schumacher and other leading drivers.

Of the current drivers, four-time world champion Max Verstappen is the most prominent to wear a Schuberth helmet. He is joined by Red Bull team-mate Isack Hadjar, Cadillac's Sergio Perez, Audi's Nico Hulkenberg and Arvid Lindblad at Racing Bulls.

Dall'Oglio's team hand-makes about 250 helmets a year.

An F1 helmet is a remarkable marriage of science and aesthetics.

Constructed using advanced composite materials, including carbon fibre, it must be exceptionally strong while remaining light enough to minimise strain on a driver's neck.

Visors, ventilation systems, fire-resistant linings, communication equipment, a straw-like drinks system and carefully designed retention mechanisms all contribute to its performance.

Today, F1 helmets must withstand a 225g metal projectile fired at 155mph, a 790C flame and substantial impact and crushing tests. It is engineering with virtually no margin for compromise.

Schuberth works closely with motorsport governing body the FIA.

Dall'Oglio says: "There's a constant exchange of information. We are repeating tests on a regular basis. We are also working on the future regulation of the helmets.

"With the tiniest structural or superficial defect, the carbon shell cannot be used. This could be a lamination, a cooking or a tool problem. So it could be over 1,000 euros (£850) in material, and all those hours of work will have become obsolete."

Arai, Bell and Stilo are the three other leading manufacturers of F1 helmets.

Dall'Oglio says: "We all have the same issues, and in the end, we are protecting drivers. So, the responsibility is heavy for all of us.

"It's the fun part to work with the drivers, but it's also a high responsibility."

When I visit, production is paused and the team cluster to solve a problem. Thankfully this happens very rarely - and the majority of helmet shells are taken to the next station: a water-jet cutting system with a "needle" of water slicing through the rock-hard carbon.

It's 4,000 bars of pressure, like a knife through warm butter, to create the narrow visor opening, six centimetres at its highest point.

"It is genius, because you don't use mechanical force on to the helmet," explains Dall'Oglio.

The cut shell is then taken to what they call "the jigsaw" - the assembly point.

Dall'Oglio says: "The outer shell is created of nearly 100 pieces. Now we add another 100 pieces, depending on the type of aerodynamics parts we need to add on. In total, we can say a couple of hundred pieces make a helmet."

Once assembled, the helmet is taken to the driver's designer.

A helmet can be a driver's signature. From Senna's iconic Brazilian colours to Schumacher's Ferrari red, the designs are miniature works of art.

Combining intricate graphics, specialist paintwork and personal touches, each livery tells a story, celebrating a driver's heritage, family or defining moments.

But while fancy designs look fabulous, they can create headaches for the team in Schio.

"A helmet overall weighs about 1.4 kg, depending on the decoration, and aerodynamic specification," says Dall'Oglio.

"We try to work hard to remove like 20, 30 grams from the top-of-the-line helmets, and a driver then adds decoration of 80 or 70 grams. It's always a little bit of a negotiation with the driver and the painter, but in the end, it's their decision."

Dall'Oglio is proud of the company's relationship with Verstappen, who is very good at giving feedback to initiate improvements.

"We continue on cultivating it, of course, but it's like a happy marriage," he says.

The helmets produced by Dall'Oglio, his colleagues, and other firms are extraordinary pieces of design and engineering, but the greatest achievement will never be the paintwork, carbon-fibre finish or name emblazoned across the side.

Thomaso smiles, as he knows. It is the life they help save, and the driver who walks away.
