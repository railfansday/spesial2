The pig in the python in the marital bed: How boomers deformed the Silent Generation’s marriages, Gen X’s childhood and millennials’ housing

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The baby boom has been called “the pig in the python” since at least 1974, when Russell Baker gave the 76 million-strong bulge its name by using the disturbing image of a python swallowing a pig and struggling to digest it, just the way the economy is struggling to digest the bulge of the largest generation in history. It’s becoming increasingly clear, as the baby boomers push the median age of the U.S. population toward 40, that the generation is so big that it has created its own markets as it moves through life.

In the current economy, this market-making dynamic is most visible in housing, where empty-nest boomers own 28% of U.S. homes with three or more bedrooms, compared with 16% for millennial parents, according to a Redfin analysis of Census data. The first-time homebuyer age has jumped to match the record-high median age of 40, the National Association of Realtors found last November.

A new peer-reviewed paper suggests the python bulge was being felt decades ago in the marriage market of the 1970s and the bonanza in divorces that resulted. In an article titled “The baby boom and the US divorce boom,” published this month in the European Economic Review, Tereza Ranošová, an economist at the Deutsche Bundesbank Research Center, argues that the two postwar booms, in births after 1945 and in divorces about 25 years later, are linked. The U.S. divorce rate more than doubled between 1960 and 1980.

Ranošová’s explanation is mostly arithmetic. “As men typically match with younger women, a large young cohort presents a supply shock to the remarriage options of husbands in existing partnerships,” she writes in the paper. So when the boom’s huge cohorts reached marrying age, the men just ahead of them, mostly the Silent Generation, born just before and during World War II, faced an unusually large supply of younger women. Those young women, meanwhile, were short of single men their own age, “making it more worthwhile for young women to match with already married men,” she writes. While standard models assume singles only marry singles, Ranošová treats married men as still on the market: “When younger women are abundant, some divorce to rematch with them.”

“These men had the most options among women somewhat younger than them (who in turn were desperate for options, so they settled for already married men),” Ranošová told Fortune in an email. The paper finds evidence that some of those marriages ended in divorce and remarriage.

She’s careful about how much this explains. “I would not want to overstate here,” she said. “I would say likely about a third at most.” She sees the boom as a push that “brought people closer to the decision,” while shifting social norms made divorce possible. “Once the norm was broken, it would be hard to attribute things to specific causes.”

The paper’s evidence is a comparison across states. Using 1960 and 1980 Census data on more than 2.2 million people ages 35 to 44, Ranošová found that people born in states with a bigger early baby boom were more likely to have ever divorced. The link held when Ranošová used World War II mobilization rates, which earlier research ties to the size of each state’s boom, to make it less likely that those states simply had different family values.

The squeeze cut both ways. Early boomer women found men a few years older scarce, Ranošová said. Some married men their own age; others married older men, more often ones who had been married before. The paper also raises, without settling, the possibility that no-fault divorce laws loosened in the 1970s partly because demand for divorce was already building.

Betsy Cox isn’t just a boomer — and a divorcee — herself, she founded Blackbook, which led the way in creating the “divorce concierge” service for the ultrawealthy. She hadn’t read Ranošová’s research, or even thought of the angle, but said “I totally agree with that” when asked by Fortune. “It’s very true, very true,” she said, adding that the boomers were “probably one of the reasons I started Blackbook Divorce. She recalled being one of the first people in her cohort to get divorced and then she saw firsthand the boomer divorce effect take flight. “I was the first, but then it was, boom! It was like, oh my God, crazy. It was everybody.”

Cox’s business has evolved away from a pure divorce concierge; she’s just launched Blackbook Circle, which she described as a “private service that gives successful men a straight answer from a woman who’s seen it all, and then a team that handles it.” A lot of her new business consists of what she calls “AI guys,” who she sees as “refreshing.”

“They’re not full of themselves and I think they would make good husbands and boyfriends because they’re nice guys,” Cox said. At the same time, she “100%” sees a divorce wave coming in as the AI wealth transforms lives overnight. Many of them want the traditional trappings of wealth. “They move maybe from San Fran to New York, a few of them. They want to be in the Hamptons, and they want to be into the clubs,” she said.

The boomers are making the reverse commute, Cox said. “Those baby boomers that are in New York are now leaving because there’s a lot of men and women that, first of all, they’re getting taxed heavily — if you say New York or LA, but there’s nothing left.” She added that coastal boomers “feel like they’ve done it all. They have enough money. They don’t have to worry. They just can’t find anybody, and that’s a whole other story of how it’s not pretty out there.”

The boomer wave had a long tail, when you look at the marital outcomes of Gen X and millennials.

Gen X icon Kurt Cobain rejected the label of being a spokesman for his generation with trademark irony, writing the lyric “that legendary divorce is such a bore.” Nevertheless, nearly every alternative rocker came from what was called a “broken home” in the 1970s, from Billy Corgan to Eddie Vedder to Dave Grohl. The great music podcaster Yasi Salek, of Bandsplain, has argued that Cobain’s grunge genre was the product of a whole generation of American men reeling from their parents’ divorces.

The numbers behind them are broad. In 1960, 73% of American children lived with two parents in their first marriage. By 1980 it was 61%, according to Pew Research Center. Before 1950, fewer than 2% of children lived with a single parent who was divorced, separated or never married. By 2000, nearly 25% did, the Census Bureau reported. Still, the famous claim that half of all marriages end in divorce comes closest to true for the Gen X childhood decade. Economists Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers found that 48% of marriages formed in the 1970s had dissolved within 25 years. That’s the decade the baby boom hit the marriage market and Gen X was growing up.

The divorce rate peaked around 1980, and each marriage cohort since has done better. By 2024, the refined divorce rate was down to 14.2 per 1,000 married women, according to Bowling Green State University’s National Center for Family & Marriage Research. Ranošová ties much of the decline to people marrying less and later, which she said means “better matches in the first place.” What stuck from the 1970s was the norm. Divorce went from rare to ordinary and never went back.

Andrew Cherlin, a Johns Hopkins sociologist who has studied divorce for decades, wrote in 2000 that the American divorce rate doubled in the 1960s and ’70s, with a million children a year going through a parental divorce. Ranošová was reluctant to tie her mechanism to Gen X’s own attitudes toward marriage. “I think that would be way too much extrapolation. I have no evidence on that,” she said.

The generations at the center of the boom kept divorcing. Among Americans 50 and older, the divorce rate doubled from 5 per 1,000 married people in 1990 to 10 in 2015, Pew found. For those 65 and older, it roughly tripled. Remarriages are a big part of it. Over-50s in a second or later marriage divorced at twice the rate of those married once, and 48% of the over-50s who divorced in 2015 were in a remarriage.

Ranošová called it “not implausible” that her cohorts show up in those numbers. Those born between 1935 and 1945 being more often divorced in later life than the generation before “matches the mechanism,” she said. That could reflect both the original mismatch and the fact that more of them are in higher-order marriages, which “are statistically more prone to divorce.” Still, she guessed much of it “has to do also with less social stigma and a different economic role marriage plays.” The money stakes are real: retirement accounts get split and the lower earner can lose Social Security income, Fortune has reported.

The divorce boom’s longest legacy may be fewer marriages. That matters for more than the wedding industry. University of Chicago economist Sam Peltzman has spent years combing the General Social Survey, which has asked Americans since 1972 whether they’re happy. His measure, the share saying “very happy” minus the share saying “not very happy,” ran at roughly +20 points on average from 1972 through 2018, steady through wars and recessions. Then 2020 knocked it down 22.2 points, by far the largest single move in the survey’s history, and for the first time more Americans said they were not very happy than very happy. By 2024 it had come back only to around +6, he told Fortune in May. Peltzman called it a “regime change” and something of a happiness recession.

The crash split the country along marriage lines. Married Americans have scored roughly 31 points higher on his scale than unmarried ones for 50 years, a “marital premium” that held across gender, race, age, income and education. After 2020, the married fell, too, but stayed comfortably positive. The unmarried, about 45% of adults 25 and older, went from near break-even to -15. “We have a segregated happiness society,” Peltzman told Fortune. “I can’t see that persisting without consequences.”

The zeitgeist found its way recently onto Saturday Night Live. On the Nov. 14 episode, host John Mulaney played a hippie at Woodstock in 1969, laying out his generation’s plans for later life. “Hey, when we get older, I think we should buy all the houses,” he says, and then floats skipping the dying part altogether. His fellow boomers didn’t seem to understand what lay ahead in their future.

Later in the show came “Sister,” a fake ad for a service in which grown brothers hand over caregiving duties for their aging fathers to their sisters, who quit their jobs and move across the country to do it.

Neither sketch is about demographics, but both run on it. A generation of 76 million set the terms of every market it entered: marriage in the 1970s, then housing, and now care, as the bulge reaches the years when it needs looking after — the pig is slowly exiting the python.

Nick Lichtenberg is business editor and was formerly Fortune's executive editor of global news.
