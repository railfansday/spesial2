Yes, I don't believe their methodology—selling a bond after one month and buying back the original term—reflects how a real-world bond fund or rolling ladder actually works. As such, I question their conclusion that there is little benefit to extending maturity beyond two years.

To test this, I compared their 2-year CMT baseline against real-world ETFs and simulated HTM rolling ladders containing longer maturities using FRED data from Jan 2010 to Oct 2026. This period captures a full interest-rate cycle—both bull and bear markets—where long yields started around 4%, dropped to historic lows, and then rose sharply to finish roughly 100 bps higher. If anything, a net 100 bps rise should favor the 2-year CMT, making it a fair stress test for extending maturity.

While this isn't intended to decide the superiority of a fund vs. a rolling ladder—since that is holding-period dependent—we do see that volatility is reduced with the rolling ladder and that both the rolling ladder and funds beat the 2-year CMT returns that the authors concluded extending maturity wouldn't improve. Again, my previous post showed a rolling 10-year had a higher CAGR (8.67% vs 8.41%) on similar volatility (10.83% vs 10.75%), yielding a slightly higher Sharpe Ratio (0.46 vs 0.44) when compared to STT in the period since '45 [albeit using yearly rather than monthly data].

Methodology

Since the McQuarrie and Bernstein methodology uses constant maturity (CMT), I modeled the CMT portfolios with the same average maturity as the corresponding ETF to make a direct apples-to-apples comparison to the real-world ETF. For the hold-to-maturity portfolios, a decision had to be made. It would be possible to lump sum into the same maturity as the ETF and CMT portfolios, but this would make results too influenced by the rate of the starting period—especially for longer maturities, since bond returns are correlated with their starting rate.

As shown in the previous post for 10-year maturities, there is little difference in returns whether using a lump sum or a ladder over the long run (post-1945 period). This makes sense since after constructing the initial ladder—where some rungs are less than 10 years—all subsequent purchases are 10-year maturities. Over time, the average gets closer and closer to 10 years.

I chose to use a rolling ladder as it more naturally fits real-world usage: an initially constructed ladder followed by reinvestment of maturing rungs and new contributions into the target maturity. During decumulation, some of the maturing rung is spent and the remainder reinvested at the target maturity. Over one's investment horizon, the average purchased maturity converges toward the target maturity.

For the sake of apples-to-apples comparison, I used hindsight to calculate the exact hold-to-maturity (HTM) top rung needed so that the dollar-weighted average purchased maturity for the ETF, CMT, and HTM portfolios are identical over this 16+ year period. Because 60/40 rebalancing inflows from equities continually added to the top rung, those matched top rungs landed just a few months above the target maturities.

VGIT
VGIT targets the 3–10 year curve and has an average maturity of 5.6 years, which is what we will target. As you can see in the 60/40 Portfolio Growth image, the red 2-year constant maturity and orange 5.6-year constant maturity have similar terminal values. You only get 7 basis points in CAGR (+$469) for 0.10% more volatility and a 2.43% larger worst drawdown. With a lower Sharpe ratio for the longer maturity too (1.02 vs. 1.03), it doesn't seem worthwhile to extend maturity from 2 years in an academic constant-maturity view.

The picture differs for real-world portfolios. For the hold-to-maturity ladder, you get a 19 basis point CAGR improvement (+$1,264) for taking only 0.02% more volatility along with a 0.94% larger worst drawdown when compared to a short-term ETF of about two years (VGSH). Given the higher Sharpe ratio as well (1.05 vs. 1.03), the trade-off looks much more attractive.

The VGIT ETF offers even higher returns at 33 bps over the short-term ETF (+$2,210), with higher volatility (+0.09% vs. VGSH) and drawdown (+2.55% vs. VGSH) than the HTM ladder, but an even higher Sharpe ratio (1.06). In real-world use, a term premium appears to be available beyond what the academic methodology would suggest.




IEF
IEF focuses on the 7–10 year Treasury bracket with an average maturity of 8.5 years. On the constant-maturity side, extending from 2 years to 8.5 years finally starts to pay off modestly: the 8.5-year CMT adds 32 bps in CAGR (+$2,140) for 0.17% more volatility and a 3.81% deeper max drawdown, edging Sharpe up slightly from 1.03 to 1.04.

Real-world portfolios capture a noticeably better term premium here as well. Compared to the short-term ETF, the 8.5-year HTM ladder delivers 33 bps of extra CAGR (+$2,213) with only 0.09% additional volatility and a 2.41% larger drawdown, lifting Sharpe to 1.06.

The IEF ETF pushes returns further still—adding 48 bps over VGSH (+$3,274)—at the cost of slightly more volatility (+0.22% vs. VGSH) and drawdown (+4.15% vs. VGSH) than the ladder, while matching its 1.06 Sharpe ratio.




TLH
Moving out to the 10–20 year space (15.5-year target maturity), the academic CMT comparison shows a wider return gap—51 bps in CAGR (+$3,517) over the 2-year CMT—but the 0.46% bump in volatility and 6.64% deeper drawdown leave the Sharpe ratio completely flat at 1.03.

In the real world, the two vehicles diverge here. The 15.5-year HTM ladder stands out as the clear winner in this bracket: it adds 50 bps over VGSH (+$3,464) while keeping extra volatility (+0.26%) and drawdown (+4.76%) well below the 15.5-year CMT, pushing Sharpe up to 1.06.

By contrast, the TLH ETF is the one laggard among the real-world funds—gaining 36 bps over VGSH (+$2,441) but absorbing 0.74% more volatility and an 8.03% steeper drawdown, dropping its Sharpe to 0.99 (likely reflecting its 0.15% expense ratio and 10–20 year index quirks).




VGLT
At the long end of the curve (10+ years, 22.0-year target maturity), the 22-year CMT picks up 62 bps in CAGR (+$4,328) over the 2-year CMT, but the 0.81% rise in volatility and 8.62% deeper drawdown drag its Sharpe ratio down from 1.03 to 1.00.

Real-world vehicles still capture a meaningful term premium, though the trade-off between funds and ladders becomes sharpest here. The VGLT ETF posts the highest return of any portfolio in the study at 65 bps over VGSH (+$4,539), but it comes with 0.95% more volatility, a 9.26% deeper drawdown (-26.18%), and a 1.00 Sharpe.

The 22-year HTM ladder captures nearly all of that term premium (58 bps over VGSH, +$4,041) while noticeably softening the ride—cutting volatility (+0.50% vs. VGSH) and drawdown (+6.81%) enough to remain the only long-end option with a higher Sharpe ratio (1.04) than the 2-year baselines.




Bond Maturity as an Equity Diversifier
Extending maturity didn't just add a term premium on the bond side—it also acted as a better diversifier to equities. As target maturity increases from 5.6 years to 22 years, monthly correlation to VTI moves progressively more negative (from -0.01 to -0.07), and total 60/40 outperformance over VGSH climbs steadily from +$1,264 to +$4,041 per $10,000 invested. Because the portfolio stays balanced at 60/40, that extra growth leaves you with both a larger stock sleeve and a larger bond sleeve than staying in a 2-year baseline.

You can see this diversification benefit most clearly at the long end. On a standalone basis, the 22-year ladder actually finished behind the 15.5-year ladder (+$1,985 vs. +$2,267 over VGSH). Inside a 60/40 portfolio, however, the 22-year ladder flipped the script and pulled ahead by nearly $600 (+$4,041 vs. +$3,464). Longer-maturity Treasuries provided better ballast when equities sold off, and that paid off at the total portfolio level.




Conclusion
Contrary to the findings from academic constant-maturity methodology, results from real-world ETFs and hold-to-maturity ladders show that extending maturity earns a meaningful term premium and provides valuable equity diversification—improving both terminal value and, particularly for HTM ladders and intermediate funds, risk-adjusted returns.

Discussion

Why use an HTM ladder instead of an ETF
The main reason is that an ETF carries a higher risk of permanent capital loss. As you can see in the charts, both HTM ladders and ETFs can drop below the green amortization line. After a one-time rate shock, a bond fund normally recovers in roughly its duration (with dividends reinvested). Under steadily rising rates, however, that break-even horizon stretches to roughly 2 × duration − 1 years, and ongoing rate hikes can keep pushing recovery further out.

With an HTM ladder, each individual rung converges to par value at maturity without NAV loss. While a rolling ladder's mark-to-market value can remain below the green line during a prolonged rising-rate bear market, any maturing rung pays out at full par—so if you need to spend from the ladder, you aren't forced to sell shares at depressed prices.

Why not just use a TIPS ETF
We can argue that getting nominal value at par from an HTM ladder may still represent a real loss. That is true, which is why I would prefer a TIPS HTM ladder to a TIPS ETF. While the TIPS inflation adjustment keeps up with rising rates driven by inflation, TIPS ETFs can still lose value if real rates rise. Without making any predictions about the future, one plausible result of persistent large Federal deficits is upward pressure on real rates as the market demands higher real yields to absorb heavy Treasury supply. Rising real rates over an extended period could cause a TIPS ETF to underperform relative to a TIPS HTM ladder maturing at inflation-adjusted par. I may look at TIPS this way next.

Use whole numbers for the ladder
For the sake of comparison, we matched each bond ladder's average purchased maturity to the corresponding ETF. A 5.6-year bond ladder is impractical in the real world, though, and simply rounding up to clean integer rungs (e.g., 1–6y, 1–9y, 1–16y, or 1–22y) produces virtually identical results. Given that both the 8.5y and 15.5y ladders hit the peak 1.06 Sharpe ratio, a simple 10-year ladder is an easy real-world sweet spot.

No real difference from the initial ramp
Even though the HTM ladder starts with shorter initial rungs than the target and then reinvests slightly longer to reach the same average purchased maturity in this test, there is little effect on the overall conclusions. It does make results over shorter time horizons potentially a bit more sensitive to starting yields. Here, over 16+ years where locking in higher yields early was advantageous, that startup ramp if anything slightly understates the ladder's results.

Smaller portfolios & rebalancing
All charts and tables are normalized to a $10,000 starting value for readability; actually building a full individual Treasury ladder at a brokerage requires a larger portfolio (e.g., $100,000+) to meet $1,000 minimum bond denominations across rungs. Investors with smaller portfolios can still build a ladder organically over time by directing new contributions into the target-maturity rung as they accumulate. Similarly, while this backtest rebalances 60/40 monthly for consistency, in practice an HTM ladder investor would use maturing rungs, coupons, and new cash flows to rebalance without selling unmatured bonds early.
