The Zombie Engine: How Brussels Bureaucrats Are Breathing False Life Into Combustion

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

If you poured rapeseed oil into a combustion engine and declared it a “zero-emission vehicle,” you would be laughed at. But in the EU Parliament, this fiction is being drafted into law.

If you poured rapeseed oil into a combustion engine, watched toxic exhaust billow from the tailpipe, and declared it a “zero-emission vehicle,” you would be laughed at. Yet inside the corridors of the European Parliament, this exact fiction is being drafted into law.

Under heavy pressure from the fossil fuel lobby and legacy carmakers, EU lawmakers are preparing to endorse a bizarre new regulatory beast: the VEEF, or “Vehicle Exclusively running on Eligible Fuels.” In plain English, these are standard combustion-engine cars promised to run exclusively on liquid biofuels or e-fuels. In exchange for that promise, Brussels plans to grant them a legal tailpipe emission score of zero.

It is regulatory alchemy at its worst. Cloaked in the seductive language of “technological neutrality,” this proposal creates an escape hatch for an obsolete technology, all while setting up ordinary European drivers for an expensive logistical nightmare.

It is just another desperate tactic to extend the life of the combustion engine by any means possible.

To understand the absurdity of the biofuel car, look at what actually happens in the engine. It’s still the exact same combustion. A piston firing on biofuels produces the same toxic cocktail of nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, and fine particulates that has choked European cities for decades. Labeling these cars “zero-emission” launders the pollution through a regulatory loophole and continues to foster emission-induced pathology.

Nor does it save the climate. The average biofuel blend available today delivers a modest 20% to 40% emissions saving compared to fossil fuels. When you factor in the indirect land-use change of first-generation crop biofuels ploughing up forests to grow food for engines, crop-based biofuel cars actually emit 23% more greenhouse gases than a standard petrol car.

Biofuel cars are being sold to the public under the guise of affordability, yet running them is punishingly expensive. Hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO) biodiesel costs around €13 per 100 kilometers to run, compared to roughly €7 for an equivalent electric vehicle. Pushing drivers toward these cars locks households into volatile, high fuel costs at the exact moment renewable electricity prices and battery costs are tumbling.

Then comes the phantom fuel problem. Europe possesses a strictly limited supply of biofuels. The continent produces only enough advanced petrol biofuel to power roughly one million cars.

The Parliament’s compromise envisions allowing food-crop fuels until 2032, before shifting new biofuels models to run strictly on advanced wastes and residues from 2033 onward. But because sales skew heavily toward petrol while advanced biofuels skew toward diesel, the arithmetic collapses immediately. If carmakers sell one million petrol VEEFs cumulatively, European biofuel supplies would run dry immediately. Millions of drivers would be left with nowhere to fill up.

How does Brussels propose to stop a driver from pulling up to a regular pump and filling their “zero-emission” car with cheaper fossil petrol?

Through a bureaucratic and technical monster. The proposals require that manufacturers install complex onboard systems to ensure fuel exclusivity: fuel sensors, tamper-proof hardware, and engine-throttling software to strand your car if the wrong liquid is detected. Pumps would require cryptographic digital handshakes with the car’s chassis number to authorize a fill-up. On top of that, it could require two separate, parallel, continent-wide pump networks; one for pre-2033 crop fuels, another for post-2033 advanced fuels.

For an EU Commission committed to deregulation and “simplification,” this is pure loophole-hungry nonsense.

Naturally, the industry knows this. The fuel-combustion lobby is already quietly pivoting to their real goal: “mass balancing.” Under this proposal, carmakers wouldn’t need to put pure biofuels into your tank at all. Instead, by using the standard blended biofuel mix of today, they could manufacture “virtual” clean cars, allowing them to keep selling dirty combustion engines while selling fewer electric vehicles.

This is not just a technical Eurocrat debate, it is an industrial fork in the road. More than €200 billion has already been committed to building Europe’s electric vehicle and battery supply chain. That capital was deployed on the promise of a clear, stable regulatory path to zero emissions by 2035.

While China dominates the global battery supply chain and electric vehicle sales surge worldwide, European policymakers are entertaining a bizarre scheme to keep 19th-century technology on life support.

The VEEF loophole solves no climate target or air pollution, helps no driver, and builds no clean industry. It is simply a regulatory Trojan horse designed to buy legacy polluters a few more years of business-as-usual. When the vote arrives, lawmakers must slam the door shut on this costly diversion.

If you are looking for a terrifying Halloween costume, a zombie combustion engine masquerading as zero-emission is about as scary as it gets.
