I was listening to Tony Robbins on the Modern Wisdom podcast that came out yesterday, and he was waxing poetic about sophisticated investors, private equity and opportunities outside the public markets.

Not because I think nobody can outperform the stock market. Of course they can. But whenever I hear about an investment strategy that supposedly gives sophisticated investors an edge, I come back to Warren Buffett's famous $1 million bet.

In 2007, Buffett bet that a simple, low-cost S&P 500 index fund would outperform a collection of hedge funds selected by professional investors over the next ten years, net of taxes and fees.

It would be easy to extrapolate from this that nobody can beat the market, so don't even try. But I don't think that's the real lesson.

There's a huge gap between knowing there are investors who will outperform and believing I can identify them in advance, gain access to them, and retain the advantage after their fees.

The first one is obviously true. It's the second one that's so difficult.

At the end of the next decade, there will be stocks that returned 20% or 30% per year. There will be fund managers with incredible track records. There will be cities where real estate prices exploded.

We'll know exactly what we wish we'd invested in. But that doesn't help us today, because we're making the decision without the benefit of hindsight.

That's one of the reasons I've always found index investing so compelling. It doesn't require me to figure out who the winners will be. I can admit that I don't know and still participate in their success.

There will absolutely be winners.  I just don't need to know their names in advance.

I noticed recently that I was starting to pick up my phone more often than I wanted to, and when I paid attention to what I was actually doing, it was almost always one of two things: Reddit or the Google feed on my Android phone.

It started out innocently enough, as I was just looking for info on my favorite soccer team, Arsenal. But I started noticing how much time was disappearing into apps with infinite scroll.

I deleted Reddit from my phone, which was an easy decision because it is a cesspool. But the bigger win was something Erin figured out: you can turn off the Google Discover feed entirely.

On Android, open the Google app → profile picture → Settings, search for Discover, and turn it off.

Now when I open that part of my phone or go to Google something, instead of an endless list of stories, there's nothing there.

I don't make many guest podcast appearances these days, but I recently joined Robert Brokamp on The Motley Fool's Hidden Gems Investing podcast to talk about the current state of the Financial Independence movement.

We talked about why you don't see nearly as many FIRE articles in the news anymore. A decade ago, FI was novel enough to be a story. Now it's everywhere. To a large degree, the core ideas of FI have simply become personal finance.

Or, as I put it on the podcast: “We used to be (considered) these frugal weirdos. And we’re not so weird anymore.”

It’s almost exactly 10 years since Jonathan and I first met in 2016, and lately I’ve been thinking about what ChooseFI has actually been about over that time.

I came across a line while reading Drownproof this week that put words to something I’ve been trying to articulate for years:

“Until you view yourself as the author of your life, you will be the victim of it.”

We’ve spent a decade talking about index funds, taxes, travel rewards, career moves, college, healthcare and hundreds of other topics. On the surface, that can look like a collection of financial tactics. But the people who seem to get the most out of ChooseFI don’t simply accumulate more tactics. They start approaching their lives differently.

They start questioning things they once assumed were fixed. If something looks complicated, they learn it. An expense that seemed unavoidable gets another look. If a career isn't what they hoped it would be, they look for another one. They stop assuming that the way things are is necessarily the way things have to be.

I think that’s why financial independence is so powerful. Money creates options, but the process of pursuing FI also teaches you to notice that you have options in the first place.

Ten years later, I think that may be the larger idea behind ChooseFI.

I had a mindset shift. I was raised by my mom, who grew up in an extremely rural environment and who herself was raised by survivors of the Great Depression and WWII rationing. There was always a philosophy of "buy it just in case" in my family. This made sense when the nearest store was an hour away and you could be snowed in for weeks. But I live in an urban environment where the nearest store is 5 minutes away and the internet at my fingertips. So I've made myself some spending rules. I only buy if it's: (1) a necessity (utilities, clothes to replace outgrown ones for the kids, groceries); (2) Building freedom (shower organizers to make cleaning faster, precut garlic, scissors in every drawer); or (3) creates joy (travel, dining experiences, movies, books, artwork, exercise equipment, etc). There are exceptions to this rule, of course, but it's helped me see where my mindless spending was, like new clothes I didn't need. Not purchasing creates freedom, but so does understanding where I do and don't want to spend money.
