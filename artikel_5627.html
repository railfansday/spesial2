How a 2032 Social Security shortfall could land in 14 states and D.C.

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Social Security cuts by state could reach $556 a month under CRFB projections, and these 14 states and D.C. show how unevenly a 2032 shortfall would land

Connecticut and West Virginia sit at opposite ends of the same problem. Under projections from the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB), Connecticut beneficiaries would lose the most per check of any state, an average of $556 a month. West Virginia beneficiaries would lose a smaller $480 a month. Yet West Virginia's economy would absorb the largest hit of any state, equal to 1.9% of GDP.

Those figures come from "No State Spared: Mapping the Impact of Social Security's Insolvency," a CRFB report published June 3, 2026. It models what would happen if the program's trust fund ran dry and benefits fell automatically. The estimates are projections, not outcomes.

CRFB built its model on the 2025 Trustees Report, which assumed a 24% across-the-board cut. The 2026 OASDI Trustees Report, released June 9, 2026, places trust fund depletion in the fourth quarter of 2032. That is one quarter sooner than the earlier estimate. The newer report points to a 22% cut instead. Kiplinger considers the two-point gap small enough that CRFB's state figures remain a reasonable simulation. State benefit levels in the model come from the 2024 OASDI Beneficiaries by State and Country Report.

CRFB projects a national average cut of $500 a month. The reduction would reach 60.1 million people receiving benefits, or 17.7% of the population on average. Total benefits would fall by $345 billion, an average hit of 1.1% of GDP. Across states, monthly cuts range from $459 to $556, or $5,508 to $6,672 a year.

The pattern follows wages. High-wage states have larger scheduled benefits, so the same percentage cut removes more dollars from each check. Lower-income states lose fewer dollars per check. Beneficiaries there lean more heavily on Social Security as their main income. They spend it close to home, on groceries, utilities, fuel and healthcare. When those checks shrink, the loss shows up as a bigger share of the state economy.

Social Security cuts by state look different depending on which measure a reader picks. CRFB mapped all 50 states plus D.C., or 51 jurisdictions in total. The 15 slides that follow cover a selection of 14 states and D.C. They include places with large and small per-check cuts, high and low shares of residents affected and the biggest dollar totals in this group.

Connecticut beneficiaries would lose an average of $556 a month under the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget's projection. No other state shows a larger per-check reduction in the CRFB analysis.

Kiplinger places the Connecticut figure at the top of CRFB's state-by-state table of monthly cuts. The state's average cut runs $56 above the national average.

That $56 gap is not a one-time difference. It would recur with every monthly check for as long as the reduced benefit stays in place.

Kiplinger ties the size of each state's cut to wage levels. CRFB's model applies the same percentage reduction everywhere. A larger scheduled benefit therefore loses more dollars. Connecticut's top position reflects bigger checks rather than a steeper percentage.

About 700,000 people in Connecticut would be affected, according to the CRFB table. That equals 17.9% of the state's population, a share slightly above the national average.

The reach of the cut is close to the national norm. Connecticut stands apart for the size of each reduction rather than for how many residents it touches.

Across the state, the projected cut would remove $4.2 billion in benefits. CRFB puts that loss at 1.1% of Connecticut's gross domestic product, in line with the national average hit to GDP.

The two measures point in different directions. The largest per-check loss of any state does not translate into an outsized hit to the state economy.

The Connecticut figures describe a projected automatic benefit cut, not a final outcome.

New Jersey beneficiaries would lose an average of $554 a month under the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget's insolvency projection. That is the second-largest per-check cut of any state. Statewide, it adds up to $9.9 billion in lost benefits.

The monthly figure is $54 more than the national average in CRFB's state table. Over a full year, the reduction would come to $6,648 for the average person receiving benefits.

About 1.6 million New Jersey residents receive Social Security, according to CRFB's figures. That is 16.3% of the state's population. The share sits below the national average, so the cut would reach a smaller slice of residents in New Jersey than in the country overall.

The statewide total combines two elements. One is a per-check cut near the top of the national range. The other is a beneficiary base of more than 1 million people. Together they produce a projected loss equal to about 2.9% of the national benefit cut CRFB estimates.

Measured against the size of the state economy, the $9.9 billion equals 1.1% of New Jersey's GDP. That matches the national average economic hit in CRFB's projection.

The two measures point in different directions for New Jersey. The dollar loss per check is well above average. The proportional drag on the state economy is not. A reader looking only at the size of each check would see one of the largest losses of any state. A reader looking at the economy-wide share would see a state exactly at the national average.

New Hampshire beneficiaries would lose an average of $553 a month under CRFB's projection, the third-largest cut of any state. That reduction would reach 295,195 people, or 21.0% of the state's population.

Only Connecticut and New Jersey would see larger average monthly cuts in CRFB's state table. New Hampshire's figure sits $3 below Connecticut's and $1 below New Jersey's. The three states would lose nearly the same amount from each check.

What sets New Hampshire apart is how many residents would feel that cut. Its share ranks among the five highest in CRFB's table. It is one of the states where a large per-check cut overlaps with a high share of residents receiving benefits.

The two states ahead of it in the monthly ranking reach a smaller slice of their populations. Connecticut's share is 17.9%, and New Jersey's is 16.3%.

Over a full year, the average cut works out to $6,636 per beneficiary. On a monthly basis, that is $53 more than the national average CRFB projects.

Statewide, the reductions would total $1.9 billion in lost benefits. That sum equals 1.5% of New Hampshire's GDP, 0.4 percentage points above the national average.

The GDP figure also separates New Hampshire from the two states with larger monthly cuts. Connecticut and New Jersey would each lose benefits equal to 1.1% of GDP. New Hampshire's slightly smaller per-check cut would claim a larger share of its state economy.

Maryland beneficiaries would lose an average of $541 a month under CRFB's projection. That is the fifth-largest per-check cut of any state, and it would reach about 1.0 million people receiving benefits in Maryland.

Mississippi beneficiaries would lose an average of $459 a month under CRFB's projection, the smallest per-check cut in the country. The state's total loss of $3.0 billion would still equal 1.8% of its GDP, among the largest economic hits in CRFB's table.

The two figures measure different things. The monthly number describes what a typical beneficiary would lose from each check. The GDP share describes how much of the state economy that lost money represents.

On the per-check measure, Mississippi fares best. Its monthly cut sits $41 below the national average.

The cut would reach about 600,000 people receiving benefits. They make up 19.6% of the state's population, or nearly one in five residents. That share is higher than the national average in CRFB's data.

On the economic measure, the order flips. Mississippi's GDP loss runs 0.7 percentage points above the national average. A smaller reduction to each check does not translate into a smaller reduction for the state economy.

Mississippi retirees are more likely to depend on Social Security as their only income, Kiplinger reports. For a household with no pension, wages or investment income, the benefit check is the entire budget. A cut to that check is a cut to total income, with nothing else to absorb it. Kiplinger notes that even the country's smallest dollar cut would therefore remove a large share of purchasing power there.

Read together, the figures place Mississippi at the bottom on check size and among the states with the largest economic exposure.

The two state economies feel that identical sum differently. CRFB puts Connecticut's loss at 1.1% of GDP and Louisiana's at 1.2%. The same dollars take a larger bite out of Louisiana's smaller economy.

Mississippi offers a sharper contrast. Its average check cut is nearly the same as Louisiana's, yet CRFB projects a hit equal to 1.8% of Mississippi's GDP. The two states show that matching check cuts can carry different economic weight.
