Home Depot’s Comparable Sales Rose 2.2% Even as Customer Transactions Fell 0.5%. Here’s What’s Behind the Gap.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Those two metrics usually move in the same direction.

Home Depot (HD -2.45%) was not a winner in the stock market on Tuesday. Its shares fell by 2.5% largely because it published a quarterly earnings report that only narrowly beat the consensus earnings estimate and slightly reduced certain guidance items. There was also something of a disconnect between the rise in its comparable sales and a dip in customer transactions.

Actually, there's a perfectly sensible explanation for that dichotomy, and this might actually provide a shot of optimism for Home Depot bulls.

Home Depot booked total third-quarter sales of $43.4 billion, up nearly 5% year over year. The aforementioned comparable sales growth figure was 2.2%; meanwhile, the comparable number of customer transactions fell by 0.5%. I'll cover this apparent mismatch in the next section.

Net income under generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) rose by slightly more than 3% to $3.71 billion for the retailer; on a non-GAAP (adjusted), per-share basis, it increased at roughly the same rate to $3.86.

That meant a pair of beats on the consensus analyst projections for Home Depot. It edged past the $3.84-per-share average estimate for adjusted profitability and topped the collective pundit sales forecast of $42.8 billion.

The company quoted incoming CEO Richard McPhail as saying that "Our associates' strong engagement and exceptional execution, combined with our continued investments across the business, enabled us to grow market share in a dynamic environment."

Speaking of incoming and outgoing executives, Home Depot also announced a planned shake-up of its top management team on Tuesday. McPhail, currently the company's CFO, will replace Ted Decker -- who also serves as president -- as chief executive (although Decker will remain as chair of the company's board of directors). Senior executive vice president Ann-Marie Campbell is to become president and COO, while McPhail's CFO seat will be filled by executive vice president of customer experience Jordan Broggi.

All appointments will be effective next Monday, Nov. 23.

Departures of high-level executives usually raise some concern with investors, who understandably worry that a leadership change is really due to difficulties among the management team or other negative factors. They also fret about potentially detrimental changes in strategy. With Home Depot, this was compounded by the fact that not one, not two, but three such changes were announced by the company.

This affected post-earnings sentiment on Home Depot's stock, as did several of the company's adjustments to full-year guidance.

For all of 2026, it now expects sales growth of around 4.5% over the previous year. That's heartening, as management had previously provided quite a wide range from 2.5% to 4.5%. Its comparable-sales estimate is now 1.4%; the preceding outlook was flat to 2% growth. However, it narrowed its GAAP operating margin guidance to roughly 12.4%, the bottom end of its prior range of 12.4% to 12.6%. And it now expects GAAP earnings per share (EPS) to rise by 1.5%, compared with the previously provided range of flat to 4%.

Let's return to that apparent schism between comparable sales and customer transactions -- remember, the former increased 2.2% and the latter slumped by 0.5%. The explanation lies in a third metric, comparable average ticket -- i.e., the total spend per customer. This rose by 3%, which means that while there was a slight, not very concerning decline in the number of transactions, clients were spending more.

In the conference call discussing the quarter's results, executive vice president of merchandising Billy Bastek attributed the increase to strength in the company's business-to-business (B2B) division, Pro, and in categories such as appliances and power tools/equipment.

I take that as a positive development, as Home Depot is clearly a sticky retailer for the ever-critical professional segment of the market -- and in a period of decline for U.S. housing starts, no less (housing starts are a key indicator for the construction industry). The power category is an important one for the retailer, as despite its relatively lower margins, it sells almost 2,000 exclusive cordless battery-powered tools. This effectively locks a customer into the chosen system, which bodes well for future commerce.

Granted, both the power and Pro businesses are lower-margin, but the boost in sales and the sticky nature of many of these goods more than offset this. Adjusted operating margin fell to 13% in the quarter from the year-ago rate of 13.3%, which didn't put much of a dent in the bottom-line increase.

I feel the market's negative reaction to earnings didn't factor in what's ultimately a net positive in the company's sales dynamics during the quarter. Investors were also likely perturbed about the implied fourth-quarter slowdown in "comps" given the revised guidance. But I think Home Depot is positioning itself to do well if and when the home construction market improves, and I'd be bullish on its future -- particularly if I were an investor with a mid- to long-term horizon.
