6 of the most expensive video games, from $212 million to more than $700 million

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Call of Duty, Red Dead Redemption 2, and Cyberpunk 2077 are among 6 games whose budgets ran into hundreds of millions of dollars.

Video games began as small software projects. Some now carry bigger price tags than major Hollywood movies. Anyone who buys a console, plays online with friends, or waits years for a favorite series to return has a stake in that shift. Budgets decide which ideas reach players and which ones never leave the planning stage. A studio that pours a nine-figure sum into one title has little room for a misfire. The money shapes what players see and touch, from the detail on a city street to the servers that keep a match running. Knowing where the spending goes helps explain why every flagship launch carries such high stakes.

Time drives much of the bill. Work on a major game can stretch across five, six, or as many as eight years. Each of those years adds salaries for a large team. Detailed open worlds and advanced graphics demand more hands and more hours. Online play brings its own expenses, including servers, security systems, matchmaking, and technical support after release. Post-launch development keeps the meter running once a game ships. Marketing can add tens or hundreds of millions of dollars on top of the build itself. Earlier projects that came in around $50 million are giving way to AAA productions that need several hundred million dollars. The public's grasp of these sums has lagged behind the reality. Recent legal filings put numbers on some well-known games that dwarfed what outsiders had believed.

Metro Unfold ranked 10 video games by reported cost, drawing on publisher disclosures, court documents, leaked internal information, Guinness World Records, and industry estimates. The publication cautions that its figures are not like-for-like, because publishers follow different accounting practices. Some numbers span a game's full lifecycle. Others fold in promotion, and a few rest on third-party projections. One figure reflects a conversion from Polish złoty at exchange rates near launch and leaves out later outlays. The source describes no scoring formula beyond those caveats. This article examines six of the 10 games, in the source's order, and presents each figure on the basis the publication reports. Readers should weigh the gaps between entries with those differences in mind.

Call of Duty: Black Ops Cold War swallowed more than $700 million in development money, the largest sum on the full list of 10 games. Treyarch and Raven Software served as the main developers of the 2020 first-person shooter. Activision $ATVI published the game and unveiled it on Aug. 26, 2020, with a Nov. 13 release date set, according to Charlie INTEL. Metro Unfold returns to the game in its closing section and sets it apart from every other entry on disclosed spending.

Cold War carried the Black Ops story forward with a sizable multiplayer component and a Zombies mode that grew beyond earlier versions. Raven Software ran the campaign, and Treyarch steered multiplayer. Raven built in multiple endings, a first for a Call of Duty campaign, with each player's choices and playstyle deciding the outcome. Giving each lead studio its own share of the work turned one release into parallel productions running side by side. The story mode, the head-to-head matches, and the enlarged Zombies offering gave players three distinct ways into Cold War, and the budget had to fund all of them.

Four support studios extended the effort beyond the two leads. High Moon Studios, Beenox, Activision Shanghai, and Sledgehammer Games filled those roles. Beenox, a small Canadian studio, tuned the PC version to run on Battle.net. Activision Shanghai has played a major role in supporting Call of Duty's online features. Six studios in all put their hands on a single shooter. A payroll stretched across that many teams helps explain how the tab climbed past every rival in the ranking.

Activision disclosed sales of about 30 million copies, a scale Metro Unfold describes as the commercial weight required to justify a cost approaching three-quarters of a billion dollars. The build alone comes to more than $23 for every copy sold. At this price, tens of millions of copies became the baseline Activision needed to make the investment pay off.

Activision $ATVI spent more than $640 million on Call of Duty: Modern Warfare, the 2019 reboot that Infinity Ward built to give the long-running franchise a more realistic, cinematic look. The studio pursued that aim by turning physical things, places, and performers into game assets.

Photogrammetry supplied the raw material. Infinity Ward used it, along with other scanning methods, to recreate objects and environments from the real world. The process started with high-quality photographs, from which the team rebuilt each object and scene. Still images could not show how light reflects off different surfaces. Laser scanning recorded what the camera missed.

Infinity Ward paired those assets with a new engine that it said would deliver an immersive, lifelike experience. The engine's toolkit included world volumetric lighting, HDR, and DirectX Raytracing on PC. Infinity Ward Poland, a team based in Krakow, helped develop the engine. The game depicts its thermal and night vision modes through spectral rendering. Motion capture gave the cast's performances matching conviction. Plans for the game put Beenox in charge of the PC version, a Battle.net exclusive, bringing another developer into the project.

Investment kept flowing after release day as the game became bound up with the success of Call of Duty: Warzone. The game's announcement promised a full campaign, a co-op component, and multiplayer modes, with no sign of a battle royale. The tie to Warzone stretched outlays past the package first pitched to players.

Activision disclosed sales of about 41 million copies, a vast audience across which to spread the bill. Spread over those buyers, the cost works out to more than $15 per copy. Call of Duty: Black Ops III, a 2015 release, sold about 43 million copies on a budget above $450 million. Modern Warfare moved about 2 million fewer. Within the franchise, a heavier budget offered no guarantee of a bigger audience.

Industry estimates put Rockstar Games' outlay on developing and marketing Red Dead Redemption 2 at $370 million to $540 million. The western arrived in 2018 after Rockstar spent years assembling its world. In the publication's view, even the bottom of that range places the game among the costliest entertainment software projects ever undertaken. The judgment survives the uncertainty. Wherever the true sum falls inside those bounds, the western belongs in the same tier as the biggest spenders on the list.

Reports cited by the publication put the workforce at around 2,000 people spread across Rockstar's studios worldwide. Divided among that group, the $370 million low end works out to about $185,000 per contributor, and the $540 million top reaches $270,000. Each per-head amount captures total investment, promotion included, and says nothing about salaries. A team of that size helps explain why the long build carried such weight. Extended production cycles turn a headcount in the thousands into enormous payroll bills.

The 2018 release makes the western the oldest of the six games in this article. Even so, its upper bound trails only the two Call of Duty entries that followed it in 2019 and 2020, and its floor still outstrips the sums behind Cyberpunk 2077, The Last of Us Part II, and Horizon Forbidden West, all released afterward. Budgets on this scale, in other words, were already in place before most of the newer flagships arrived.

Red Dead Redemption 2's cheapest projection exceeds Grand Theft Auto V's roughly $276 million, a sum Metro Unfold credits to Guinness World Records, by about $94 million. At its upper end, the western nearly doubles that benchmark and outspends it by $264 million. The $170 million distance between the two ends of the western's band tops half of Grand Theft Auto V's entire reported tally. The doubt over this one game's price now dwarfs the entire cost of the older, smaller productions the industry is leaving behind.

CD Projekt RED set aside about 1.2 billion Polish złoty for Cyberpunk 2077, worth around $316 million at exchange rates near the game's 2020 launch. The studio channeled that money into a futuristic role-playing game set in Night City. Hollywood actor Keanu Reeves took on the role of Johnny Silverhand, a casting choice that tied the project to the film business whose budgets big games now rival.

Technical problems marred the debut, and players on older consoles hit the worst of them. CD Projekt RED answered by spending more to repair and improve the game. The troubled rollout exposed work the original plan never covered, and the bill arrived after copies had reached stores. Cash committed up front marked a starting line, and the finish lay well past it.

Update 2.0 and the Phantom Liberty expansion drew hundreds of millions of additional złoty. Even the low end of that range amounts to a substantial slice of the 1.2 billion złoty the studio first allotted. Each patch and add-on meant paying a team to keep building a game that had shipped. The true price of Cyberpunk 2077 runs past its published total by an amount no single disclosed number pins down.

The initial allocation alone earns Cyberpunk 2077 sixth place among the 10 games and edges past the roughly $315 million reported for Marvel's Spider-Man 2. The publication's closing assessment groups Cyberpunk 2077 with Red Dead Redemption 2, Battlefield 6, and Spider-Man 2 as evidence that enormous outlays are spreading across the top of the industry. The game makes an unusual member of that group. Its place rests on funds earmarked before any player saw Night City. The recovery effort sits outside the ranking. A rescue on that scale shows how a troubled release can turn a headline budget into a down payment.

Naughty Dog poured about $220 million into The Last of Us Part II, a figure that surfaced when Sony $SONY court documents exposed it by accident. The slip gave players a rare look at what a top-tier story game takes to build. The number came from a legal record, which gives it more weight than a rumor or an outside analyst's guess.

Production stretched across about six years before the game launched in 2020. Naughty Dog's staff peaked at around 200 full-time employees, and the studio leaned on significant outside support beyond that core. The outside help lifted the true head count above that full-time peak. Six years of wages for a group that size show how a calendar can swell a bill. A long schedule turns every month of polish into another payroll cycle.

Human actors performed every cutscene in The Last of Us and in its sequel, and motion capture recorded each performance. Naughty Dog extended the same approach to animals. The studio brought live horses onto a purpose-built recording area and covered the floor with large amounts of rubber matting for them to cross. Jeremy Yates, the game's lead animator, posted footage from the horse session in which one animal jumps an obstacle and another gallops across the stage. Building a dedicated stage for horses is the kind of detail that lengthens the work and adds salaried hours to the total.

Metro Unfold reads the budget as evidence that polished single-player narrative games had grown expensive before the newest consoles drove spending higher. The game sits ninth among the 10 entries on the publication's list, a placement that reveals how far ahead the priciest shooters and sprawling open worlds have run. A comparable project built for current hardware faces a steeper outlay than the one Naughty Dog paid on this game.

Horizon Forbidden West cost around $212 million to make, proof that the 10th and final entry on the source's list cleared $200 million. The game reached players in 2022 after roughly five years of work.

More than 300 full-time employees staffed the project at its peak. Outside contractors and support studios supplied labor beyond that in-house roster. Counting that extra help, the total number of hands on the game ran higher than the full-time tally alone suggests. A crew that large, held together across half a decade, points to salaries as a heavy force behind the bill. The peak team outnumbered the one Naughty Dog assembled for The Last of Us Part II, yet Horizon Forbidden West wrapped in a shorter span.

Guerrilla built the title on its own Decima Engine and passed over third-party tools such as Unreal. The studio gave mounts a larger role than they held in the first installment, adding travel over water and through the sky to widen what players could do. Ahead of the game's release, Guerrilla assigned a dedicated group of developers, which the studio called the Living World team, to one task: making the open world feel alive, realistic, and responsive.

Call of Duty: Black Ops Cold War, the priciest entry Metro Unfold lists, required more than three times the money Horizon Forbidden West did. The gap between those extremes shows how far spending stretches at the industry's summit, where the floor itself sits in nine figures.

Metro Unfold reads the outlay as a gauge of the financial scale a flagship PlayStation title now requires. Horizon Forbidden West and The Last of Us Part II landed within $8 million of each other, though the first leaned on a larger team and the second on a longer schedule. If that pattern holds, trading headcount for time does little to pull a marquee game's price tag below $200 million.
