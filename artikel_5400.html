The New 7 Wonders of the World were chosen by public vote, not by scholars

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

More than 100 million people voted for 7 wonders. See what the title means for each site, from Petra to the Taj Mahal.

The "wonders of the world" is one of the oldest lists people keep, and almost every structure on the original version is gone. Of the seven ancient wonders, only the Pyramids of Giza had survived by 2007, according to ABC News. That year, a campaign to name their successors left the choice to the public rather than to scholars, and more than 100 million people took part, according to RFE/RL. A wonder picked by that many voters wins on reach and recognition, and sites with famous images and proud national audiences start with an advantage. The seven winners are best read as the places the world most wanted to celebrate, which is a different claim from the places experts would rank highest.

Public appeal, not established prestige, decided the outcome of the vote. ABC News reports that the shortlist held 20 candidates, and the Acropolis in Athens and the Eiffel Tower were among the sites that missed the final seven, RFE/RL reports, even with their places in every textbook. No official cultural body stands behind the winners, and the organizers ran the poll themselves online. UNESCO, the UN's cultural body, said the new list would come out of a "private initiative," as reported by RFE/RL. What the seven places reliably gained from that private campaign was attention, along with the visitors, pressure, and scrutiny that go with it.

Britannica gathered the modern replacements for the ancient Seven Wonders that the public chose in a worldwide vote, from Petra in Jordan to Christ the Redeemer in Brazil and Chichén Itzá in Mexico. Taken together, these are working places, and the people who run, study, and visit them live with what the title brought. At some of the sites, that means crowds, construction, and real physical risk to the monuments and the people around them. At others, the story the public voted for rests on questions nobody has settled, from what a city was built for to how its people lived.

The Great Wall of China belongs among the wonders because people kept building it for an extraordinary length of time. The Wall is roughly 5,500 miles long and was built over two millennia, according to Britannica. Building on that scale lasted far longer than any person's lifetime, so the Wall is the work of many generations of builders rather than any one of them. For all that labor, invaders were barely stopped by the Wall. What the world celebrates here is the ambition and persistence of the people who built it. That is a different kind of greatness from the strength of a fortress that held.

Even the Wall's size, the quality people admire most, has no agreed figure. A Chinese study arrived at a far larger length of 13,170 miles (21,200 km), but that figure is disputed, and neither it nor the roughly 5,500 miles can be treated as final. Visitors to the world's most famous wall cannot say for certain how far it extends. The basic fact that defines it is still being argued over, so its scale remains part of its mystery and not a settled part of its record.

Whichever length turns out to be right, the Wall stands among the largest structures people have ever built.

The Maya built the great pyramid at Chichén Itzá, on the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico, so that people could read it as a year. El Castillo has 365 steps, one for each day of the solar year, according to Britannica, and that skill in astronomy is why the city belongs among the wonders. The builders also designed the pyramid to put on a show at set moments, and at the equinox a serpent-like shadow appears on its north stairway. People now come in great numbers to see that kind of precision, with about two million visitors arriving at Chichén Itzá every year, according to Smithsonian magazine. The Maya earned the site its title with their skill, and that same skill is the reason so many people crowd it.

Mexico is responding to those crowds by making it easier for even more visitors to reach the region. Smithsonian magazine reports that the Maya Train is an $8 billion tourist railway built through the Yucatán region where the pyramid stands. The government argues that the railway will boost the economy. Critics say the train will damage the jungle and the caves beneath it, and some people also fear for archaeological sites. If those fears prove right, Mexico could put less famous ruins along the route at risk in order to bring more people to one wonder. Mexico has not settled whether more visitors are worth that risk.

The money going into the wonder itself is far smaller. INAH has set aside about $14 million for upgrades at Chichén Itzá, according to Smithsonian magazine. Even that work has to proceed carefully because buildings that nobody has found may lie under the ground. "All the groundwork will be done manually in order to minimize the risk to any undetected structures," said Carlos Esperón, director of the Maya Museum in Cancún. Nobody yet knows everything that lies under the site, so anyone who builds there could destroy part of the wonder before anyone has seen it.

The world voted for what looks like a finished monument, but Chichén Itzá is a place where structures still lie undiscovered.

Petra, Jordan, belongs among the wonders as a whole city, not as a single carved monument. The Nabataeans made it their trade capital, according to Britannica. Nabataean builders shaped the rock faces into temples and tombs, and they devised a way to bring water to everyone in the city. The city reportedly had 30,000 residents at its height, which made it a sizable settlement and not a remote shrine in the desert. It was a trading hub whose merchants specialized in spices. What the world voted to celebrate, then, are the remains of a place where people lived, traded and managed their water, and that is a larger achievement than an attractive ruin.

Much of Petra's past is still uncertain, even though its carved stone is famous around the world. Earthquakes struck the city in 363 CE and again in 551. Those were two disasters, centuries apart, in a city that people had built around trade and a reliable water supply. Beyond those dates, many questions about Petra remain open. The people who voted for the city knew its cliffs and facades, but they were honoring a place whose record still has gaps.

A visitor can stand in front of the tombs and temples, but the history of the people who carved them is still unfinished.

The story most people know about Machu Picchu, a lost Inca city found by an explorer and explained, is newer and less certain than the stones themselves. The Inca built the city in the Andes mountains of Peru in the 15th century, according to Smithsonian magazine. For centuries few outsiders knew of it. Its "discovery," Britannica reports, is dated to 1911, and the explorer Hiram Bingham received the credit. Bingham also misread what he had found. He identified the ruins as Vilcabamba, and that identification was later disproved. The deeper question has no answer yet. Scholars hold competing views on what the Inca built Machu Picchu for, as Bingham did before them, and none of those views has been established. A visitor who climbs to the citadel today is walking into an argument that is still going on.

So many people now want to see Machu Picchu that no more than 4,500 to 5,600 may enter on a given day, depending on the season, according to Smithsonian magazine. Roughly 1.5 million travelers make the trip over the course of a year. Anyone who expects the quiet of a forgotten mountain city will share the terraces with crowds.

For centuries outsiders overlooked the city, and now the number of them allowed in each day is limited.

Christ the Redeemer is a figure that people look up at, placed on a peak above a city, and it earned its title by being seen rather than by being old. Brazilians began building the statue in 1926, according to Britannica, and in 2007 it was named among the seven winners, according to ABC News. The statue of Jesus is 98 feet (30 meters) tall and stands on top of Mount Corcovado, high above Rio de Janeiro. People see it from the streets below instead of walking through it. A single figure on a mountaintop is an image that people around the world can recognize at a glance, and that kind of recognition counts most in a public vote.

The builders meant the statue as a declaration of faith. It went up in the years after World War I, when some Brazilians feared a "tide of godlessness," and they answered that fear by placing a figure of Jesus where everyone in the city could see it. They set their message high over Rio de Janeiro for the people who lived there, and that same height made the statue easy to recognize far beyond Brazil.

The height that makes the statue visible also leaves it open to the weather, and lightning often strikes it. In 2014, the tip of its right thumb was damaged in a storm.

No one can move Christ the Redeemer to safer ground without giving up the summit that people around the world recognize.

The Romans built the Colosseum to put a city's worth of people in front of a spectacle, and the crowds have never stopped coming. It earned its place among the wonders as a feat of Roman engineering that seated 50,000 spectators for gladiator fights and mock sea battles, according to Britannica. Today up to 20,000 people visit on peak days, according to ABC News, so the amphitheater still serves as a mass venue rather than a quiet ruin. The new title added attention to a monument that was already in heavy daily use and that has to stay safe for everyone who walks through it.

Keeping an ancient amphitheater open means people have to work high inside its walls, and that work has now cost a life. Inside the Colosseum in September 2026, a worker was killed in a fall of as much as 10 meters, according to ABC News. Police are working to determine the circumstances, and no cause has been established. The death shows a cost of the Colosseum's celebrity that seldom comes up: the monument the world admires is a worksite as well as an attraction.

The death brought a rare pause to a site built around crowds. ABC News reports that officials first closed only part of the Colosseum and later shut the whole site to the public for the day on Wednesday, Sept. 23. For one labor leader, the loss came down to a question of priorities. "Humanity comes before tourists," said Natale di Cola, Rome general secretary of the CGIL labor union.

Voters honored what the Romans built, and keeping it on show has now cost a worker his life.

The Taj Mahal exists because an emperor lost his wife, and that grief is the core of its appeal. Mumtāz Maḥal, the wife of Emperor Shah Jahān, died in 1631, and the mausoleum he raised for her in Agra, India, honors her memory, according to Britannica. People remember the building for the love story as much as for its architecture, and the two cannot be separated. Shah Jahān turned his mourning into a state project that took 20,000 workers about 22 years to complete. That much labor over that many years makes the romance grand rather than sentimental, because one man's loss set a workforce the size of a city to work for a generation.

The tomb was made to be looked at, and its appearance is what people around the world know. Its builders covered it in white marble set with semiprecious stones, which gives the Taj Mahal an image people recognize at a glance. Residents of Agra marked the result of the vote with fireworks and sweets in front of the monument, according to RFE/RL. They celebrated the title as a win for their city and their country, not as an abstract honor for a building. For Agra, the Taj Mahal is part of the city's identity, and a title chosen by popular vote rewards exactly that kind of public attachment.

The love story may also have a sequel that has no building behind it. Some reports say Shah Jahān wanted a second mausoleum, in black marble, for himself, but work on it never began. The most dramatic part of the legend has nothing physical to show.

Visitors to Agra can stand before the white tomb Shah Jahān finished, while the black one he may have wanted left no stone behind.
