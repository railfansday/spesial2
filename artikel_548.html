Indonesian Cinema’s Next Chapter Is Reaching the World ‘Without Losing Our Cultural Identity,’ Says JAFF Market Director Linda Gozali

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Indonesia’s film industry has momentum at home. JAFF Market director Linda Gozali wants to help it travel.

Gozali runs the industry market attached to the Jogja-Netpac Asian Film Festival (JAFF), now in its third edition. The 2024 launch tested whether Indonesia needed a film market at all, and the industry’s answer was an emphatic yes. The 2025 edition opened the door to regional and international partners. This year, the market is taking stock of how well the business holds up against economic turmoil and political instability around the world, while widening the scope for credible partnerships at home and abroad. “Our next chapter is learning how to reach international audiences without losing our cultural identity,” Gozali tells Variety. “We aspire to become nationally indispensable, regionally relevant and internationally connected.”

Local hits have yet to translate into comparable business abroad, which Gozali puts down to producers and filmmakers treating the home market as their first priority. “Indonesia has learned how to win its own audiences,” she says, adding that plenty of untapped local viewers still need to be brought in.

Even so, Gozali says, a good share of Indonesian horror has sold across multiple territories, and independent films have been well received on the international festival circuit. She also notes that Indonesia is not alone in looking inward first, pointing to other populous countries such as China, India and Japan.

“Any growth should not be mistaken for maturity,” she says. “JAFF Market’s responsibility is to transform that growth into sustainability.”

That thinking draws on an industry report JAFF Market produced with Cinepoint. According to Gozali, it points to a basic mismatch between Indonesia’s volume of content production and appetite for investment on one side, and its limited exhibition infrastructure on the other.

“Sustainable growth requires wider audience access, diversified distribution access, responsible production investment and stronger business intelligence,” Gozali says. “Our collaboration with Cinepoint is intended to encourage evidence-based industry decisions.”

The market can already point to results. Projects that have met collaborators for their next steps include “Pangku” and “My Mother” from JAFF Future Project (JFP), and “Tikam Samurai,” “Bandits of Batavia,” “World Without Sleep,” “Timun Mas” and “Gloomy Sunday” from the JAFF IP Connection (JiPCon) strand. Joint ventures, memorandums of understanding and letters of intent have also emerged around “Khadam” and “Badut Gendong,” along with an agreement between JAFF Market and the Jakarta government.

Gozali singles out “Slaughterground” as the clearest example of the market linking things up over time. Presented as an IP at JiPCon during the 2024 inaugural edition, it returns this year as a film in JAFF Future Project. “The IP met its destiny in JAFF Market,” she says.

The deals, she argues, have created value beyond the event itself. But she sees the market’s main achievement as bringing the whole of Indonesia’s film industry under one roof for the first time.

JAFF Market shares the regional calendar with Busan’s Asian Contents & Film Market (ACFM), Hong Kong FilMart and the Taiwan Creative Content Fest. “We are the new kids on the block so we want to work alongside the other established markets and learn and partner with them in areas that will strengthen our programs and also theirs,” Gozali says.

Every market hopes to become a long-term international hub, she adds, but JAFF Market will take it one step at a time. Some ties are already in place. The market continues to collaborate with the Adelaide Film Festival, an institutional partnership Gozali says brings continuity, credibility and a route into established international networks. It also runs a reciprocal IP exchange with ACFM.

To bring in international sales agents, distributors, financiers and streamers, the market relies on curated programs, business dialogues and matchmaking designed to move attendees from general discovery toward focused meetings with decision-makers. “We recognize that international participation must be driven by business opportunities, not simply invitations,” Gozali says. “The quality and relevance of international participation matter as much as the number of delegates or countries represented.”

“As a producer myself, I recognize that one of our industry’s gaps (besides a shortage of stories or talent), is a shortage of sustained cross-border producer relationships,” she says of the market’s Producers Exchange strand, which aims to build trust, encourage knowledge sharing and help producers find compatible partners beyond one-off projects. “Connecting the right producers today can shape the regional production and distribution landscape of tomorrow.”

For newcomers, JAFF Future Project, Film Labs and Talent Day offer a way into the Indonesian industry. Gozali says the insights gathered across the market’s programs help first-time filmmakers find their way and have shaped how they handle business with international partners.

International co-productions open up additional financing, creative expertise, shared resources, distribution networks and new audiences. Indonesia, however, would benefit from clearer regulatory frameworks, production incentives and tax rebate mechanisms. “It is important to ensure that Indonesian filmmakers are ready to become equal creative and business partners,” Gozali says.

JAFF Market is backed by Amar Bank, Indonesia’s Ministry of Culture and Ministry of Creative Economy, Jakarta City and a roster of sponsors and partners. Gozali is grateful for that backing but says the platform needs long-term, sustained support in both funding and infrastructure to keep serving the industry.

“JAFF Market must remain commercially responsible without becoming commercially exclusive,” she says. Diversified revenue streams, strategic partnerships and curated programs are meant to keep the market open to independent filmmakers and emerging production companies. “Supporting emerging talent is not simply a philanthropic endeavor; it is an investment in the industry’s future creative and commercial potential,” she adds.

As for the next five years, “Growth is not necessarily about becoming bigger,” Gozali says. “It is about becoming more relevant, useful and consequential.”
