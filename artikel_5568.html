Norway tops the 2026 retirement index — the U.S. ranks 24th of 44

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index ranks 44 countries — see how Norway, Ireland, the Netherlands and Denmark score and why the U.S. slipped to 24th

No country spends more on health per person than the U.S. Yet the U.S. finished 24th of 44 countries in the 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index. That result sits well below its best showing of the past decade — 14th, in 2016.

The index comes from Natixis Investment Managers, which builds it with CoreData Research and publishes a new edition each September. Scott Staton reported the 2026 results for Boldin on October 13, 2026. Each country receives an overall percentage score drawn from four categories: health, finances in retirement, material wellbeing and quality of life. A country can lead one category and trail in another. Its overall rank reflects the balance across all four.

European countries fill most of the upper end of the list. Nine of the top 10 countries are European. Australia, at 77%, is the only top-10 entry from outside the continent. Norway holds first place at 83% for the second year running. Ireland is second at 81%, and the Netherlands is third at 79%. Switzerland is fourth, also at 79%, and Denmark is fifth at 78%.

Category leadership is spread across several countries. Luxembourg posts the highest health score, at 94%. Finland leads on quality of life. Ireland ranks first on finances in retirement, and Denmark tops material wellbeing.

The U.S. result points in the other direction. Its overall score slipped from 70% to 68%, and its rank fell from 21st. It now sits between Cyprus and Japan. In finances in retirement, the country fell eight places to 18th. Its material wellbeing ranking also dropped as unemployment and income equality got worse.

The slides that follow are a selection from the full ranking, not a complete list. Four cover European systems that finished near the top — Norway, Ireland, the Netherlands and Denmark. Each was chosen for a specific result, from first place overall to a leading category score. The fifth looks at the U.S. and breaks down where it lost ground, where it held steady and where it improved. For readers comparing national retirement systems, the category scores show where each country gains and loses points.

Norway is the top-ranked country in the 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index, with an overall score of 83%. It also finished first in the previous edition, which makes 2026 its second straight year at No. 1.

Boldin reported the 2026 results, which show a narrow margin at the top. Ireland, in second place at 81%, sits two points behind Norway.

Norway's overall score rests on strong results in three of the index's four categories. It scores 92 for health, 90 for quality of life and 81 for material wellbeing.

None of those marks is the highest in its category. Norway reaches first place overall without leading any single category. Its rank comes from consistent scores across the board rather than one standout result.

On health, Norway's 92 matches Ireland's score, sits just below Switzerland's 93 and is higher than the Netherlands' 88 and Denmark's 86. On quality of life, its 90 is higher than Denmark's 87 and the 82 that Ireland and Switzerland each record. On material wellbeing, its 81 edges the Netherlands' 80 and Ireland's 77 but trails Denmark's 83.

Finances in retirement is Norway's lowest category score, at 70. Ireland, at 76, and Switzerland, at 74, both rank above it on that measure. Norway still finishes ahead of the Netherlands, at 67, and Denmark, at 61.

Norway's retirement system includes a public reserve fund called the Government Pension Fund. Pensions there are linked to the Social Security Base Amount, known in Norwegian as Grunnbeløpet, or G. The country also pays a special supplement to people whose pension is low.

The U.S., 24th overall at 68%, trails Norway in all four categories. Its health score is 80 against Norway's 92. On material wellbeing the U.S. scores 55, and on quality of life it scores 72. Finances in retirement is where the U.S. comes closest, at 67 against Norway's 70.

Ireland ranks second in the 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index with an overall score of 81%. It also places first in the finances in retirement category with a score of 76, a position it has held for two years running.

Finances in retirement takes in measures such as inflation, government debt and tax pressure. No country in the 2026 index posts a higher score in this category than Ireland. Holding that top spot in consecutive editions means the lead is not a single-year result.

Other high-ranked countries trail Ireland on this measure. Switzerland, fourth overall, scores 74. Norway, the overall leader, scores 70. The Netherlands scores 67, and Denmark scores 61. Norway's lead in the overall table does not carry over to this category.

Ireland's other category results run higher than its finance score. It scores 92 on health, level with Norway. Its quality of life score is 82, the same as Switzerland's. Material wellbeing comes in at 77.

That spread means the category Ireland leads is also its lowest score of the four. Its finance score of 76 sits just below its material wellbeing result. The pattern shows that a first-place category finish measures Ireland against other countries. It does not mark the country's strongest area in absolute terms.

In the overall standings, Ireland finishes two points behind Norway. Its finance lead does not close that gap. Norway posts higher scores on material wellbeing and quality of life.

Ireland's position marks a long climb. In 2013, the country placed 25th overall. Today only Norway ranks above it.

In Ireland, benefits from the mandatory pension scheme are not tied to how much a person earns. A self-employed worker can expect the same benefits as an employee with a contribution record of the same length.

The country also has a means-tested State Pension (Non-Contributory). It can be paid as an alternative to the contributory pension.

The Netherlands ranks third in the 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index, with an overall score of 79%. It moved up three places in this edition, reaching its best position since the index began.

The index scores each of its 44 countries in four categories: health, finances in retirement, material wellbeing and quality of life. The Dutch results are 88 for health, 67 for finances in retirement, 80 for material wellbeing and 81 for quality of life.

The Netherlands leads none of the four categories. It reaches third place with three scores in the 80s and no first-place result.

Health is the highest Dutch score, at 88. Norway and Ireland each post 92 on health, and Denmark, fifth overall, posts 86.

Finances in retirement is the lowest of the four Dutch scores, at 67. Ireland leads that category at 76. Switzerland scores 74 and Norway scores 70. Denmark is lower than the Netherlands on this measure, at 61.

Material wellbeing is a stronger area for the Dutch. Denmark leads that category at 83. Norway scores 81, and the Netherlands follows at 80.

The Dutch quality of life score of 81 sits just below Ireland and Switzerland, which each post 82. Norway scores 90 in that category, and Denmark scores 87.

Switzerland, fourth in the ranking, posts the same overall score of 79%. The two countries reach that total by different routes. Switzerland is stronger on health, at 93, and on finances in retirement. The Netherlands is stronger on material wellbeing, where Switzerland scores 68.

The U.S. posts the same finances in retirement score as the Netherlands, at 67%. The gap between the two countries shows up in the other three categories, where every Dutch score is higher.

Part of the Dutch retirement setup is decided at the workplace. Employees in the Netherlands are typically required to take part in the pension scheme their employer offers.

Denmark places fifth overall in the 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index, with a score of 78%. It leads all 44 countries in the index on material wellbeing, with a category score of 83.

Material wellbeing is one of four categories that Natixis Investment Managers and CoreData Research combine into each country's overall result.

The other countries in the index's top five all score lower in this category. Norway, first overall, scores 81 on material wellbeing. The Netherlands, third overall, scores 80. Ireland, in second place, scores 77. Switzerland, ranked fourth, scores 68, the lowest material wellbeing result among the index's top five.

Denmark outscores Norway in this category, even though Norway finishes higher overall.

Quality of life is Denmark's other strong category. Its score of 87 is the second highest among the index's top five countries. Norway, at 90, is the only one of the five to score higher. Switzerland and Ireland each post 82 on quality of life, and the Netherlands posts 81.

Health is a weaker area for Denmark. Its health score is 86, the lowest among the five top-ranked countries. Switzerland scores 93 on health, Norway and Ireland each score 92, and the Netherlands scores 88.

Finances in retirement is Denmark's weakest category. Its score of 61 is the lowest of any country in the index's top five. Norway scores 70 on finances, and the Netherlands scores 67. Switzerland scores 74, and Ireland leads the whole index in this category at 76.

The pattern shows how the index's categories offset one another. Denmark holds the top material wellbeing score of any country, yet it has the lowest health and finance scores among the top five.

Some basic services in Denmark come from the state. Older people in Denmark receive a state pension as well as help with care. The country's health system is funded through taxes. Patients pay nothing for it.

The U.S. ranks 24th of 44 countries in the 2026 Natixis Global Retirement Index, down from 21st a year earlier. Its overall score fell 2 points, from 70% to 68%.

That is a three-place drop in a single edition. Three of the four category rankings moved down, and only one improved.

Finances in retirement produced the steepest fall. The U.S. now ranks 18th in that category, with a score of 67%. Inflation was one weak point, and the country slid to 30th on that measure. Government debt was another. The U.S. ranks 40th of 44 on debt, near the bottom of the index.

Tax pressure moved the other way. The U.S. climbed one place on that measure, to 11th.

Health also slipped. The U.S. fell one place to 25th in the health category, with a score of 80%. That leaves its health ranking one spot below its overall position.

Material wellbeing is the weakest of the four U.S. category scores, at 55%. The country dropped four places in that category to 28th. Income per person held steady at sixth. That leaves a 22-place gap between the country's income ranking and its material wellbeing ranking.

Quality of life was the one category where the U.S. gained ground. It rose three places to 22nd, with a score of 72%. Environmental scores drove most of that improvement.

Natixis survey data shows how American investors view some of these pressures. The firm's 2025 Global Survey of Individual Investors covered 7,050 investors in 21 countries, each with at least $100,000 to invest. Among U.S. respondents, 76% said they expect public debt to cut their retirement benefits. Health costs were a bigger worry in the U.S. than elsewhere. In the survey, 35% of U.S. investors said they worry about going broke paying for health and long-term care, against a global average of 24%.

Natixis's 2026 advisor survey pointed to a separate risk, one that sits with savers. Advisors named unrealistic return expectations as the biggest mistake investors make. Investors expected returns of 10.7% a year above inflation. Advisors put a more realistic figure at 8.8%.
