Seven things Solak thinks for NFL Week 11: Takes on four QBs, Lions, Rams, morehttps://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/50505355/week-11-seven-things-solak-thinks-burrow-mvp-rams-lions

I think … Vikings-49ers is the coolest game of the seasonThe Vikings have had a fraught three weeks. Everyone was excited to see them face the Lions in Week 8 — the best offense they'd faced in a while. Detroit stuck it to 'em with a sick schematic wrinkle for which the Vikings were not prepared — multiple tight ends lined up in the backfield as quasi-fullbacks to pave open rushing lanes.…This is quite literally the best against the best. The 49ers lead the league in points per drive (3.0), success rate and EPA per play on offense. The Vikings lead the league in points per drive (1.24), success rate and EPA per play (if you eliminate the Bucs' rainstorm game against the Falcons, which is cheating, but I don't care) on defense. It does not get better for offensive football than what the 49ers are doing right now. It does not get better for defensive football than what the Vikings are doing right now. Snap the pool stick, toss it down at midfield and let's see who survives.

Not only are these the most effective units on their respective sides of the ball — there's actually some structural similarity between the two. The Flores defense has been exhaustively covered this season, but if you've missed them to this point of the season: The Vikings are blitzing on 72.3% of opposing dropbacks through 10 weeks. I shouldn't need to tell you where that ranks among league defenses, but just in case: It's first.

To blitz that much sounds deranged (and likely is), but Flores has greatly deepened the meaning of blitz packages in the modern NFL. He doesn't blitz to get the quarterback down, but blitzes to force the ball out fast to predictable hot reads. He blitzes to keep the back and tight end in protection and limit the pass-catching options the opponent can deploy. For many defensive coordinators, the blitz is an end to itself — they want to get to third-and-long so they can blitz. For Flores, the blitz is a means to many different ends depending on the opponent and context.

Let's consider the route distributions. Opposing offenses get all five routes into the concept 31.2% of the time against the Vikings, which is the lowest number in any season as far back as I have data (2017). The aggressive blitz fronts — with five or six (or seven or eight) bodies on them — keep backs and tight ends in the formation to protect, which makes the Vikings all the more dangerous when they drop bodies into coverage and only bring four on the rush. Against the Vikings' four-man rush, opposing quarterbacks are averaging an eternity to throw the ball (3.34 seconds, highest in the NFL Next Gen Stats database) and still suffer a 44% pressure rate, which leads the league.

Flip the coin to the other side, and you'll discover that no offense gets five routes into the concept more often than the San Francisco 49ers. On 72.5% of Brock Purdy's dropbacks this season, the 49ers have five eligible in the concept — that's tied for the highest rate of any offense over the past two seasons with … the 2025 49ers.

Why so many players out in the concept? It is indicative of a change that's been underway in San Francisco's offense since the Christian McCaffrey trade in 2022.

When you employ McCaffrey, you don't keep the back in to protect. Are you out of your mind? Have you seen this guy catch passes? The only season of the past five years in which the 49ers have been below 72.5% in five-out rate was 2024, when McCaffrey was hurt for much of the year. When the Cowboys called up a Cover 0 blitz in Week 10 in a got-to-have-it spot late against the 49ers, San Francisco had no issue getting McCaffrey into the route and quickly throwing him the ball against a defensive end (Von Miller) peeling off into coverage. A 74-yard touchdown later, the game was over.

This peeling defensive end is a big part of how the Vikings like to deal with pass-catching backs, mind you. No edge rusher drops into coverage more often than Andrew Van Ginkel. But for as special of a Swiss Army knife as the Gink is … do you like him dropping with McCaffrey in space?

Much as Flores throws additional bodies at the offensive core to force players into protection, the 49ers throw as many eligible bodies as possible into the route to force defenders to win in coverage. Because the ball can go to George Kittle and McCaffrey at high volumes, it's hard to play man coverage against 49ers coach Kyle Shanahan — your linebackers and safeties will be victimized — so you have to drop into zone, which pulls players out of your blitz packages. Purdy has been blitzed on 29.1% of his dropbacks, 27th among 37 qualifying quarterbacks this season.

When I say "five receivers in the route," you might instinctively think about spreading the defense out — and you'd be right. But the 49ers, critically, do not play from spread formations. No offense has been more condensed in the entire Next Gen Stats era than the 2026 49ers, who have an average formational width of 18.9 yards — a full yard tighter than second place (the 2025 49ers at 19.9 yards).

This has been another big leap in the 49ers' offense since they acquired McCaffrey. From 2017 to 2021 — B.C, or Before Christian — the 49ers were in condensed sets on 42.3% of their offensive snaps. After the McCaffrey trade, that number leaps to 61.2% — easily the highest among offenses.

The condensed sets serve several purposes. They allow the 49ers to get extra blockers into the run concept more easily — they wouldn't have nearly as complex of a running game otherwise. They allow pre-snap motions to hit faster across the formation, which strains how well a defense communicates — linebackers have to see a lot more when all 11 players are in the offensive picture. They all but guarantee opposing cornerbacks have outside leverage on the 49ers' wide receivers (otherwise out-breaking routes would be far too easy to complete), and that outside leverage opens up runways for the deep crosses, posts and in-breakers than Shanahan tends to favor.

The McCaffrey of it all is very simple: He's the 49ers' slot receiver. He's their player to win on option routes inside the numbers, to run little safety-valve routes through the middle of the field. But because he lines up in the backfield, the 49ers don't actually need to create a slot alignment with a nice wide formation. It would be a waste of space.

Condensed sets have drawbacks, and the first is the blitz issue. When all 11 offensive players are pulled into the core of the formation, all 11 defensive players follow. For a coach like Flores, who wants to (and has shown the ability to) blitz every single player from every possible alignment, condensed sets are a dream. It's far easier to disguise a blitz when all of the bodies are close together anyway, and the path to the quarterback is inherently shorter for cornerbacks and safeties — the most surprising and often most devastating of the potential blitzers. The league is blitzing condensed sets at a 38% clip this season relative to 29.6% for all other formations. To condense against Flores feels like playing into his hand.

Because Flores blitzes so much, his defense is also the most condensed defense, just as the 49ers' offense is the most condensed offense. He wants as many players close to the ball as possible so that the blitz paths are shorter, just as Shanahan wants as many players close to the ball as possible so that the motion paths are shorter. Flores has at least five men in his defensive front on 79.2% of his snaps this year (highest in the Next Gen Stats era) and at least six men in the defensive front 35.8% of his snaps, second only to the 2025 Vikings. It doesn't matter if these bodies are defensive tackles or safeties or anything in between — Flores crowds your space.

This is why the 49ers/Vikings game is so interesting to me. Shelve the actual efficiency stats for now. No two units put as much formational strain on their opponent pre-snap as these two units. And no two units throw as many bodies at their opponents as these two units. Shanahan and Flores are dictators — not in the political despot sense, but in the playcalling sense. Elite offense against elite defense is often cast as unstoppable force against immovable object, but this matchup is more unstoppable force against unstoppable force.

This will be a true clash of football minds with six, seven layers of counterpunches and curveballs. I have absolutely no idea who will win. Play ball!

How a rocket scientist is fueling the NFL's best defensehttps://www.nbcnews.com/sports/nfl/rocket-scientist-fueling-nfls-best-defense-rcna610015

The Minnesota Vikings' defense is the type of unit that unleashes pure terror on its opponents, a whirring buzz saw of blitzes coming from all levels of the field and entirely unpredictable.

It leads the NFL in total touchdowns allowed (just 12 scores this season), average points allowed per game (14) and total number of sacks (37) — a testament to making life quite miserable for opposing quarterbacks.…But one of the untold secrets of their success might be that acclaimed defensive coordinator Brian Flores happily accepts input from all corners, and that includes rocket scientists. In this case, it's the team's assistant defensive backs coach, Chenzo Funari, who until a few years ago was working on SpaceX's combustion devices group and the company's Starship system.…The Cleveland native — whose first love was football until he suffered several significant knee injuries as a teenager (and came to grips with his 5-foot-5 frame) — first left SpaceX for a job at TaylorMade Golf, then a couple of months later applied for a volunteer coaching position at Youngstown State University in Ohio.

It involved plenty of nights sleeping on air mattresses and a complete lack of certainty about what came next in his professional journey. But Funari said he saw one thing clearly: Working on defensive schemes was similar to working on rockets. And his background allowed him a unique perspective to deliver some valuable insight to teams.

"Something starts as an idea, a concept," he said. "Then you design it on the computer, you get it manufactured, built and then you test it. So there's a big process of the idea and the final product that you see on the rocket."

"And to me, I view it as the same [with football], what these guys are able to do and the life that they bring to our defense," he added. "Everyone calls it chaos, and schematically it's, 'How do we take those ideas and communicate them in a way that allows us to bring that to fruition?'"…As for Flores, the defensive guru known for his exotic blitzes and frenetic schemes that catch teams completely off guard, he was taken aback a couple of years ago when the fresh-faced analytics hire with a science background handed him a research packet on an opposing quarterback.

"I think some of the information can be overwhelming and hard to really drill it down," Flores said. "But when I got this information from Chenzo, it made a lot of sense. He's got a football background, as well, and he's able to tie the whole thing together."

Flores said now whenever there's a situation that involves running numbers, whether it's defensive fronts or coverages, he puts it through the "Chenzo Filter" for a discerning look.

"We're always looking for an advantage. We're always looking for an edge," Flores said. "And so, from that day on, it was like, 'Chenzo, you're in these meetings every week.'"

Harrison Smith, now in his 15th NFL season, all with the Vikings, is the team's longest-tenured player and a safety who works directly with Funari on a daily basis.

Smith said that Funari knows the game "inside and out at a high level" and that his story is one that could be instructive to others on the importance of crafting your own pathway to success.

"I think kind of the fairy-tale type of takeaway is to follow your dreams," Smith said. "But I think there's a reality within that that he also built himself for this dream. He's qualified for this dream. He has the skill set for this dream. And I imagine building rockets was another dream that he had, as well."

Now an assistant defensive backs coach, Funari (and his many supporters) says the sky is the limit for where he goes from here.

He said that could mean being elevated to a position group coach or, perhaps one day, a coordinator or even an NFL head coach.

"It's never too late to do what you think will kind of bring you joy in life," Funari said. "And always chase a passion."
