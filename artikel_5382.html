10 ways to tell if an outage is real or just a problem on your end

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How to check if an outage is real, with 10 tests from status pages and Downdetector to DNS changes, ping and HTTP error codes

A service that refuses to load can fail at any of six layers. Those layers are the device, the app or browser, the home network, the internet provider, the domain name system and the service itself. Each layer leaves different clues behind. Reading those clues in the right order turns a guess into a diagnosis.

The question behind every one of these checks is simple: is it down for everyone or just me? The answer decides what to do next. A real outage on the company's side means waiting. Nothing at home will fix it. A fault with the internet provider calls for a check of its outage notice or a call to support. A local fault means the fix is in your own hands.

Knowing how to check if an outage is real starts with outside evidence. Company status pages show what a provider has confirmed. Those pages are updated by the company itself, and they can lag behind what users see. Downdetector charts problem reports submitted by users. That makes it a useful signal but not proof.

The next set of checks isolates your own equipment. Trying a second device, switching from Wi-Fi to cellular data and restarting the modem and router each remove one possible cause. If the service still fails after all three, the fault probably lies beyond your home.

The last group is more technical, though none of it requires special software. Changing the DNS resolver, running a ping test and reading the HTTP status code on an error page can separate a name-lookup failure from a server-side failure. Turning off a VPN or browser extension rules out software sitting between you and the site. Cloudflare Radar adds a wider view when many unrelated sites fail at once.

The 10 methods below are a selection, not an exhaustive troubleshooting manual. Each one stands on its own, and a reader can jump to the test that fits the moment. Together they run from quick outside checks to more targeted diagnostics. They also help answer two related questions: how to tell if the internet is down and how to check if a website is down.

A service's official status page is the quickest way to confirm a problem the company itself has acknowledged. If it lists an active incident for the feature you are using, the trouble sits on the company's side. A green indicator, however, does not prove everything is working.

Most large technology providers publish a status page that lists current incidents. The company runs the page. Its contents reflect what the provider has confirmed rather than what users are reporting. That makes it the most authoritative source on a confirmed outage. It also makes it the slowest to change.

The AWS Health Dashboard covers Amazon $AMZN Web Services. Its public view shows the health of AWS services by region. Customers who sign in can also see events tied to resources in their own accounts. Many websites and apps run on AWS. A problem listed there can explain failures in services that carry a completely different brand name.

Downdetector collects problem reports from users and charts them over time. A sudden spike for a service suggests that many people hit the same failure at once. That pattern points to a shared problem rather than a local one.

The chart works as a crowd signal. It stands apart from any statement the company behind the service publishes. That makes it an outside view of what people are experiencing in the moment.

Reading the chart is mostly about shape. A low line with a few scattered reports is normal background noise for a large service. A steep climb within a short window is the pattern that matters. When reports rise sharply and keep rising, the odds that the trouble is yours alone go down.

The chart has limits. It reflects only what users chose to submit. Those users are reporting symptoms, not causes. A person whose app will not load cannot tell whether the app, their phone carrier or a hosting company behind the app has failed. They report the name they recognize.

If a service loads over mobile data but fails on home Wi-Fi, the fault lies in the home network or with the internet provider. If it fails on both connections, the service itself becomes the stronger suspect.

The test works because the two connections take different routes to the internet. Home Wi-Fi runs through a router, then through the broadband line supplied by the internet provider. Cellular data skips all of that. It travels from the phone to a nearby cell tower and through the mobile carrier's own network.

That separation makes the result easy to read. A site that opens on cellular but spins on Wi-Fi is not down for everyone. Something in the home network or on the provider's line is blocking the path. The next step is usually to look at the router or contact the broadband provider.

A failing DNS lookup can make a working site look down. Switching to a public resolver, such as Cloudflare at 1.1.1.1, Google $GOOGL Public DNS at 8.8.8.8 or Quad9 at 9.9.9.9, shows whether your original resolver was the fault.

DNS, the Domain Name System, translates a name like example.com into the numeric IP address that computers use to connect. A browser has to finish that lookup before it can request a page. If the lookup fails, the browser never reaches the site.

Most homes use the resolver that the internet provider assigns automatically through the router. When that resolver slows down or stops answering, many sites can appear unreachable at the same time. The sites themselves may be running normally throughout.

HTTP status codes in the 5xx range mean the server received a request but could not complete it. When a page shows one of these codes, the fault usually sits with the service, not with your device or connection.

Every web server answers a request with a three-digit status code. The first digit sets the class of response. The HTTP standard, maintained by the Internet Engineering Task Force, defines these classes. Codes in the 4xx range describe a problem with the request itself. Codes in the 5xx range describe a failure on the server side.

A 500 internal server error is the general catch-all. It means the server hit an unexpected condition and could not fulfill the request. The code says little about the cause. It does show that something on the server side was reachable and able to answer.

Cloudflare Radar is a public website from Cloudflare that charts internet traffic trends and tracks outages and disruptions. When several unrelated sites stop working together, it can show whether the failure reaches beyond one home to a provider, a region or shared infrastructure.

One broken app usually means a problem with that app. A banking site, a news site, a streaming service and an email account failing at the same moment point somewhere else. Those services rarely share an owner or a data center.

When they all stop responding together, the common factor is more likely the connection carrying the traffic. That could be the internet provider, a regional network or a piece of shared infrastructure.

Radar is built to show that wider picture. Cloudflare runs a large global network that sits in front of many websites. Radar draws on traffic passing through that network to show how internet activity rises and falls over time.

Microsoft $MSFT 365 service health sits inside the Microsoft 365 admin center. It lists incidents and advisories for apps such as Outlook, Teams and SharePoint. Viewing it requires an admin role. Employees without that access may need to ask their IT department what the page shows.

The Google $GOOGL Workspace Status Dashboard is open to anyone. It lists the current state of services including Gmail, Google Drive, Google Calendar and Google Meet.

Look past the summary banner at the top of any status page. Many pages break a service into separate components, such as login, messaging or file storage. A single component can fail while the rest of the service works normally. Also check whether the incident names specific regions. A problem in one region may not touch another.

The incident history deserves a look as well. An issue marked resolved earlier in the day can return. The timestamps on each update show how recently the company posted new information.

Delays are built into how these pages work. Staff typically confirm the scope of a problem before posting it publicly. Users can therefore run into failures before the page changes color. If the page shows everything operational while the service keeps failing, check it again after a short wait. In the meantime, other tests can narrow down the cause.

That confusion can send reports to the wrong place. When a widely used underlying service fails, the services that depend on it can appear to spike together. A carrier problem can also show up as reports against popular apps. Those apps are what customers notice first.

A spike says little about scale on its own. Report volume tracks how many people noticed a problem and took the time to log it. That number depends partly on the size of a service's user base and the hour of the day. A smaller service can have a real outage with only a modest bump.

For those reasons, Downdetector works best as a signal rather than as proof. It answers a narrow question — whether other people are complaining at the same moment — rather than confirming what broke or why.

A quiet chart carries information too. If a service shows no unusual activity while it fails for you, the problem is more likely to sit somewhere between your device and the service. That is the cue to start isolating causes closer to home.

Opening the same service on a second device connected to the same network separates a problem with one gadget from a problem everyone shares. When the phone fails but the laptop works, the first device or its app is the likely culprit. When both fail, the network or the service moves up the list.

The test works because it changes only one thing. Both devices share the same Wi-Fi, the same router and the same internet provider. The only variables left are the hardware and the software running on it. Keep both devices on the same connection for this check. Bringing in a cellular connection at the same time blurs the result.

Pick a device that runs a different operating system where possible. An iPhone and a Windows laptop, or an Android phone and a Mac, share very little code. A failure that appears on only one platform often points to a bug in that platform's version of the app, not a service-wide outage.

Compare the app with the website. Many services, including Netflix $NFLX, Spotify $SPOT and Gmail, offer both a dedicated app and a browser version. Suppose the app fails on a phone while the web version loads on a laptop. That means the service's servers are answering. The fault sits with the app or the phone running it.

An outdated app is a common reason for this split. Services change how their servers communicate with apps, and older versions can stop working after such a change. Checking the App Store or Google $GOOGL Play for an update is a reasonable next step. Restarting the device clears a frozen app or a stuck background process. Deleting and reinstalling the app goes a step further.

Check the device's date and time. A clock that is badly off can make secure websites fail with certificate errors. HTTPS certificates are valid only for a set period. A wrong clock makes a valid certificate look expired or not yet active. Chrome shows this as NET::ERR_CERT_DATE_INVALID. Setting the clock to update automatically typically clears it.

Sign in with the same account on both devices. A login that fails on both may trace back to the account rather than to the service as a whole. A password change, a lapsed subscription or a locked account can each cause it. A different account working on the same device supports that reading.

Running the test takes only a few taps. Turn Wi-Fi off entirely in the phone's settings rather than just moving away from the router. Then confirm the status bar shows a cellular indicator such as 5G or LTE before trying the app or site again.

Turning Wi-Fi off matters because phones can blur the two connections. The iPhone has a setting called Wi-Fi Assist, which switches to cellular data automatically when a Wi-Fi signal is weak. Many Android phones offer similar behavior. With Wi-Fi still on, it can be unclear which network actually carried the request.

A laptop can run the same comparison. Most smartphones can share their cellular connection as a personal hotspot. Joining that hotspot puts the laptop on the carrier's network without changing anything else about the machine.

A failure on cellular needs one more check before blaming the service. Open a few unrelated sites over mobile data. If those load, the carrier connection is working and the original service is the likelier problem. If nothing loads, the phone may have a weak signal or the carrier itself may be having trouble.

The same logic applies away from home. Public Wi-Fi in hotels, airports and cafes often uses a captive portal. That is a sign-in page that blocks other traffic until a user accepts the terms. A service that seems down on that network may be waiting behind the portal. Switching to cellular shows whether the venue's connection is the obstacle.

Restarting the modem and router clears a stuck state in the hardware and forces a fresh connection with the internet provider. When every device in the home loses access at once, power cycling this equipment is a quick hardware check before calling anyone.

The two boxes do different jobs. The modem links the home to the internet provider's network over a cable, fiber, DSL or other line. The router takes that single connection and shares it among phones, laptops, TVs and other devices. It does this over Wi-Fi and Ethernet cables. Many providers supply a single gateway that combines both functions in one unit.

Like any computer, this equipment runs software that can hang. A router can stop handing out local addresses to devices. It can lose track of what is connected. It can also hold onto a broken session with the provider. Cutting power wipes that temporary state and makes the hardware start clean.

The order of the restart matters. Unplug the power cord from both the modem and the router, wait briefly, then plug the modem back in first. Let it finish starting up and reconnect to the provider before powering on the router. With a combined gateway, unplugging and replugging the single unit does the same job.

Use the power cord or power button, not the small recessed reset button found on many units. Holding that button typically restores factory settings. That can erase the Wi-Fi network name, the password and any custom configuration.

Once the equipment is back up, the indicator lights tell part of the story. Most units have lights for power, for the internet or online connection and for Wi-Fi. A steady internet light generally means the modem has reached the provider. An internet light that keeps blinking long after startup, stays dark or turns red suggests the line itself is not connecting. Colors and meanings differ by manufacturer. The label on the device or its manual is the reference to check.

If the internet light still shows a problem after a restart, the fault may sit outside the home. Check the internet provider's outage notice before spending more time on the hardware. Many providers post service alerts on their websites, in their apps or on customer account pages. A phone on mobile data can reach those pages while home service is down.

A restart that restores the connection points to a local hardware hiccup. A restart that changes nothing, paired with a posted outage notice, means the problem is on the provider's side.

The browser error often gives the first clue. In Google Chrome, the message DNS_PROBE_FINISHED_NXDOMAIN means the name lookup failed. NXDOMAIN is the DNS response for a domain that does not exist. A mistyped web address triggers the same error. Check the spelling before changing any settings.

To test a different resolver, open the network settings on a computer or phone and enter a public DNS address manually. On Windows, the option sits in the network adapter's IP settings. On a Mac, it appears in the DNS section of the network details. Changing the DNS setting on the router applies it to every device in the home at once.

Each of these services also publishes a secondary address for backup. Cloudflare uses 1.0.0.1 as its second address. Google Public DNS uses 8.8.4.4. Quad9 uses 149.112.112.112. Quad9 is also designed to block domains on its lists of known malicious sites.

Devices store recent lookups in a local cache. A stale entry can keep a failure in place even after the settings change. On Windows, the command ipconfig /flushdns clears that cache before you retest.

If the site loads after the switch, the original resolver was the problem. The service itself was never down. You can keep the public resolver in place or switch back once the provider's resolver recovers.

If the site still fails on a public resolver, a lookup problem on your side becomes less likely. The fault may lie with the service's own DNS records or with its servers.

A ping test shows whether your computer can reach another machine on the internet and how long the round trip takes. Pinging a numeric IP address and then a domain name tells you whether the fault is basic connectivity or a failed name lookup.

The ping command is built into Windows, macOS and Linux. It sends small packets called ICMP echo requests to a target and waits for echo replies. Each reply line reports the delay in milliseconds. A summary at the end shows how many packets were sent, how many came back and the percentage lost.

On Windows, open Command Prompt and type ping 8.8.8.8, the address of Google $GOOGL Public DNS. Windows sends four requests by default and then stops. On a Mac, the same command runs in Terminal and keeps going until you press Control-C. Most Linux distributions behave the same way.

Start with the IP address. Replies from 8.8.8.8 mean your device has a working path to the wider internet. If every request times out or reports the destination as unreachable, the problem sits closer to home — the device, the local network or the internet provider.

Next, ping a domain name such as google.com. This step adds DNS to the test. The computer must first translate the name into an IP address before it can send anything. If the IP address replies but the name returns an error saying the host could not be found, the connection works and the name lookup is failing. That pattern points to DNS rather than a service that is down for everyone.

High delay or partial packet loss points somewhere else. Replies that arrive slowly, or only some of the time, suggest congestion or an unstable link rather than a full outage.

Ping has a known limit. Some servers and firewalls are configured to ignore ping requests on purpose. A site that never answers a ping may still load normally in a browser. Silence from a single host is not proof that it is down. Treat ping as one clue alongside what the service itself shows.

A 502 bad gateway and a 504 gateway timeout both involve an intermediary. Many large sites pass traffic through a gateway or proxy before it reaches the server that builds the page. A 502 means that gateway got an invalid response from the server behind it. A 504 means the gateway waited and never received a timely response.

A 503 service unavailable means the server cannot handle the request at that moment. The standard ties this code to temporary overload or scheduled maintenance. A site may return it while it is overwhelmed by traffic or taken offline on purpose for upkeep.

Not every error signals an outage. A 404 not found means the server is working but cannot locate the requested page. A mistyped address, a deleted article or a broken link is the usual explanation. A 403 forbidden means the server understood the request and refused to fulfill it. That points to a permissions or access issue rather than a service failure.

The code also needs to be read in context. Some apps hide raw codes behind generic messages. In those cases the number may appear only in a browser tab or on an error screen. A page that spins and eventually times out with no code at all is a different case. With no response from the server, the cause may sit closer to home, in the device or the local connection.

A 5xx code on one page does not always mean the whole service is down. Reloading after a short pause, or opening a different section of the same site, shows whether the failure is isolated or widespread.

A VPN, an ad blocker or stale browser data can break a site that works for everyone else. Turning off the VPN and opening the site in a private or incognito window is a quick way to tell whether the problem lives in your own software.

A VPN sends your traffic through a remote server run by the VPN provider. If that server is overloaded or offline, many sites can appear unreachable at once. That pattern can look like a wider internet outage when it is not.

Some websites also block traffic from IP addresses linked to VPN services. In that case, the site may show an access error or a repeated verification challenge instead of the normal page. Disconnecting the VPN and reloading the page tests both possibilities. A work VPN can cause the same symptoms on a company laptop.

Opening the site in a different browser is another clean test. Each browser keeps its own extensions, cache and settings. If the site loads in Firefox but not in Chrome, the fault sits with Chrome's setup.

If the site works in any of these tests, the service itself is not down. Re-enabling extensions one at a time can identify the one causing the break.

Visitors can view traffic by country and by individual network. Each internet provider runs one or more networks identified by an autonomous system number, usually shortened to ASN. Looking up a provider's network can show whether its traffic has dropped below its normal pattern.

Radar also has a section devoted to outages and disruptions. It lists reported internet disruptions and describes their scope, along with a likely cause where one is known. Causes in that tracking include power failures, damaged cables, technical faults and government-ordered shutdowns.

A sharp dip in traffic for a country or provider can line up with the moment a home connection failed. That timing suggests the problem is not confined to one household. Restarting equipment or changing settings is unlikely to fix it. The next step is to follow the provider's outage notices until service returns.

The tool has limits. Radar reflects the traffic Cloudflare's network can see, not every connection on the internet. It works at the level of countries and networks. It will not show a fault on a single street or inside one apartment building. A small local outage may never appear as a visible dip.

Radar does not track the internal health of individual apps either. A login failure at one service, or a bug that breaks a single feature, will not register as a traffic disruption. The company's own status page and user-report sites give a clearer signal for that kind of problem.

A normal-looking Radar chart is one piece of evidence, not a final verdict. Combined with tests on a second device or a second network, it helps settle whether a disruption belongs to one household, one provider or the wider internet.
