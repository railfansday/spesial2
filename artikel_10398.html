NASA wanted to know if humans could handle life on Mars—4 volunteers just put the idea to the test

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

One of NASA’s strangest, most daunting missions just wrapped up, and its crew didn’t even leave Earth’s atmosphere. 

Four crew members emerged over the weekend after spending a full year cooped up inside a 1,700-square-foot habitat designed to simulate life on Mars. The simulation at Johnson Space Center in Houston represented the next best thing to a real mission to Mars, giving scientists an opportunity for a lower stakes run-through without the extreme risks posed by the Red Planet.

The Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog (CHAPEA) mission kicked off on October 19, 2025, and was the second of its kind, following a previous isolation mission that ended in 2024. This time around, the crew spent a total of 378 days in a facility extruded by an enormous 3D printer and crafted to resemble a real-life Martian habitat, complete with living areas, workspaces, and even the capability for simulated spacewalks.

The brave souls willing to boldly go into confinement in the name of science included medical officer Ellen Ellis, mission commander Ross Elder, science officer Matthew Montgomery, and flight engineer James Spicer.

“To my crewmates, it’s been a hell of a year—analog astronauts living in captivity of our own free will,” Ellis said in a press conference after emerging from the habitat.

And their work isn’t done yet. The crew members are scheduled to spend two weeks being debriefed and undergoing evaluations after the unusual experience.

Staying busy on Mars

While the CHAPEA mission lacked the array of life-threatening complications astronauts would encounter on a real trip to Mars, the team at NASA simulated those too. 

At various points during the yearlong staycation, the crew faced “environmental stressors such as resource limitations, isolation, equipment failure, and significant workloads”—plenty to keep a crew of four busy for a year in a faux Martian habitat.

“We are extremely proud of the crew for their commitment and achievements over the last year on such a fast-paced, highly demanding mission,” CHAPEA principal investigator Grace Douglas said in a press release prior to the “egress ceremony” on October 31. 

The isolation might have been the most daunting task of all. During their time in the habitat, the crew members were able to communicate with their families only over email, and even that process came with a built-in delay meant to simulate real communication times between Earth and Mars, which are roughly 140 million miles apart.

Future astronauts sent to Mars will also grapple with the planet’s toxic carbon dioxide-rich atmosphere and its freezing surface conditions, among the myriad other logistical challenges and deadly resource puzzles inherent to sustaining human life beyond our pale blue dot.

“This mission has been incredibly valuable in starting to teach us how to understand requirements for yearlong missions, including medical and psychological care, via communication delays,” Michael Stenger, chief scientist for NASA’s Human Research Program, said as the mission neared its end. “When combining this Mars-realistic mission analog with a suite of physiological, psychological, and performance measures in a rigorously controlled environment, we’ll be able to translate the knowledge from these experiences to moon base and beyond.”
