Iraqi Airways Is Selling Saddam Hussein’s 2 Boeing 747s After 35 Years In The Desert

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Iraqi Airways is looking for buyers for two Boeing 747s that have been grounded since the Gulf War in 1991. The planes, once in the service of Saddam Hussein, have been sitting in the Tunisian desert at Tozeur-Nefta International Airport (TOE) since then.

The jets have been parked for longer than they were in service, with the jets initially grounded to keep them safe in the Gulf War. It isn't known if these jets are airworthy or how much work would be needed.

While these jets have been idle, the sales team at Iraqi Airways has not. The planes, a 747-200 (YI-AGP) and a 747SP (YI-ALM), have been up for sale before, without any luck. The first attempt was made in 2010. During the second sales attempt in 2023, the jets were valued at $494,000 each. AeroTelegraph reports they are listed at $900,000 each, meaning the jets cost less than some new hypercars. What has changed to nearly double the jets' value is not clear. There is also flexibility, as prospective buyers can purchase one or both of the aircraft.

The Iraqi Embassy in Tunisia is acting as the broker for the sale. It thinks a deal can be completed before January 7, 2027. It may need to be even more flexible on pricing to sell the aircraft in such a short time. Yet, Iraqi Airways has refused to confirm whether the jets are airworthy.

Whoever buys them will need to invest a lot of money if they have plans to fly the aircraft. They will also have to deal with the plane's transport, storage, customs paperwork, and other associated costs.

The jets came to Tunisia in the early 1990s to protect them during Operation Desert Storm, which saw much of Iraq's aviation capabilities bombed to oblivion. 35 years later, the planes are still stored at TOE at monumental expense to the Iraqi government.

In 2020, aeroTELEGRAPH reported that the Iraqi government and the TOE airport authorities had signed a deal for a payment of approximately $5.4 million in parking fees and other outstanding charges. It isn't known what other payments Iraq may have made to the airport or the Tunisian government over the three decades.

Tunisia supported Iraq in the period around the Gulf War, refusing to attend the 1990 Cairo Summit at which nations voted to condemn Saddam's regime and the Iraqi state. The initial storage agreement may have been a gesture of goodwill or part of another diplomatic arrangement. When the jets arrived in Tunisia, they were painted white to remove the Iraqi Airlines livery. In 2012, reports from Tunisia suggested that the harsh desert conditions had begun to strip away the paint, and that the old green livery was starting to show, MiG Flug has revealed.

Which aircraft will fill the gap left by the iconic Boeing 747?

The Gulf War did not last long, but the 747s could not go home. This was because of an international embargo, parking charges, and a lawsuit, the latter of which was the most significant. Kuwait Airways Corporation sued Iraq and Iraqi Airways in London because 10 airliners with Iraqi Airways crews had flown out of Kuwait after the invasion. Kuwait Airways said this amounted to theft and demanded $1.8 billion for the jets and the spare parts inventory loaded onto them.

Six of these aircraft were in Iran for safekeeping. The remaining four jets were destroyed during Mosul air raids. The case became the English Commercial Court's longest ever. It led to a worldwide freezing order being made against Iraqi Airways, preventing the 747s from flying back to Iraq.

The case was finally settled in 2012. Iraqi Airways agreed to pay Kuwait Airways $500 million, far less than Kuwait Airways claimed the stolen inventory and jets were worth.
