Investors: If a Market Correction Is Coming, This 1 Move Will Make or Break Your Portfolio

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Don't panic and sell everything if the market crashes.

It's been more than four years since the last bear market ended on Oct. 12, 2022. But the average bull market lasts roughly four to five years, suggesting the next downturn could be right around the corner. The Iran war, elevated interest rates and Treasury yields, and other macro headwinds could certainly trigger a market crash over the next 12 months. But when that happens, one move will either make or break your portfolio.

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -1.92%) has generated an average annual total return of more than 10% since its inception in 1957. Yet during those seven decades, the S&P 500 experienced 12 bear markets -- and each of those downturns shook out plenty of nervous investors.

Peter Lynch, who led Fidelity's Magellan Fund to beat the S&P 500 for over a decade, famously said: "Everybody in the world is a long-term investor until the market goes down." Warren Buffett also once said that if investors buy a stock, they need to be prepared for it to "go down 50% -- or more -- and be comfortable with it." But it's easier to recite those mantras than to follow them.

Many of the market's biggest crashes occurred after periods of "irrational exuberance". That term, coined by former Fed chairman Alan Greenspan in 1996, was originally a warning about rising stock market valuations during the dot-com boom.

At the peak of the dot-com bubble in March 2000, the S&P 500's trailing price-to-earnings ratio exceeded 30 as its forward P/E ratio exceeded 60. It subsequently experienced a peak-to-trough decline of nearly 50% before bottoming out in Oct. 2022. When it finally reached its nadir, it was trading at about 14 times and 29 times its trailing and forward P/E ratios, respectively.

Over the past several decades, the S&P 500 has traded an average trailing P/E ratio of 18-19 and a forward P/E ratio of about 17. As of this writing, the S&P 500 trades at 26 times trailing earnings and 18 times forward earnings. So while the S&P 500 looks historically expensive, it hasn't quite reached dot-com bubble levels yet.

If the market crashes, investors should watch the S&P 500's trailing multiple. If it sinks to the low teens, it makes more sense to hold on -- or buy more stocks -- and wait for a rebound. Doing so would be the one move that could either make or break your portfolio.

That said, staying invested during a downturn doesn't mean you should blindly hold your losing stocks and hope they bounce back. The S&P 500 only keeps growing because it rebalances its holdings every quarter to only include the 500 most prominent companies in the United States.

By continuously replacing the losers with the winners, the S&P 500 will keep growing as long as the U.S. economy expands. Investors should adopt a similar pruning strategy with their portfolios so they don't get stuck with long-term losers.

Stocks like Plug Power and Tower Semiconductor (TSEM -9.17%) are still trading below their 2000 highs. Meanwhile, it took Cisco and Intel 25 and 26 years, respectively, to exceed their dot-com highs.

During the next market crash, many AI stocks that generated multibagger gains over the past few years could experience similar crashes. Therefore, if you have any speculative stocks that are trading at unsustainably high valuations and aren't profitable yet, it might be smarter to replace them with some more reliable blue chip stocks trading at more reasonable valuations.

Whenever a bear market occurs, you'll likely hear countless financial analysts and journalists tell you that "this time is different." But even after a dozen bear markets, the stock market has continued to outperform cash, bonds, and commodities -- as well as many other assets. Therefore, the worst thing you can do is panic and sell everything when the market pulls back.
