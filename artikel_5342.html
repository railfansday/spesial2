I’m a luxury watch CEO, and in San Francisco, buyers are spending an average of $14,350 per order, and the IPO wave is just starting

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Quaid Walker is the chief executive officer and co-founder of Bezel, the marketplace for authenticated luxury watches. The San Francisco sales, order-size and pricing figures in this piece come from Bezel’s own marketplace data.

This week Anthropic goes public at an expected valuation of up to about $1.4 trillion, closing its order book with bids for about six times the shares available. With the inclusion of a private placement, they’re looking to raise up to about $90 billion and, if they’re successful, it could be the largest amount raised from an IPO ever (beating SpaceX’s record raise only five months ago).

This is an unprecedented wealth creation window. It’s estimated that Anthropic’s IPO will make 4,250 employees millionaires (about 80% of its employees) and well over 1,000 deca-millionaires. SpaceX created about 4,400 employee (or ex-employee) millionaires on their IPO day in June and OpenAI’s 2025 and 2026 tenders likely created hundreds.

People in tech, specifically AI, are making more money than ever, faster than ever. I cut my teeth at Google before starting a luxury watch marketplace (Bezel), so I spend a lot of time thinking about what they’ll do with the money. The predominant narrative we hear is that it goes towards new homes and charitable donations. Both seemingly very true: more than 210 SF homes have sold for $1 million or more over list this year, per Bloomberg, and about half of Anthropic employees pledged to donate a portion of their equity stakes to charitable causes. But I have a thesis that this class of newly minted technologists and investors will have a disproportionate interest in buying things that feel rare. They’ll build their wealth in AI and ultimately make outsized purchases on things AI can’t make, far more than the tech elite that came before them.

AI is making software, content and services cheap and abundant. It’s never been a more empowering time in tech, but AI is also making us crave things that feel distinctly human made, nostalgic, and grounded. When abundance in these technology circles becomes the default and spending power balloons, status moves to whatever is “unsloppable.”

At Bezel, we’re already seeing the effects. Our San Francisco sales have more than doubled this year and have grown about sevenfold in two years. These buyers are disproportionately trading up into expensive watches, not just buying more. In 2024, a San Francisco order was about 30% smaller than our average elsewhere. This year it’s about $2,000 bigger at a $14,350 average order value. That’s largely because about 14% of San Francisco orders are over $25,000, twice the rate of everywhere else. These buyers don’t just want standard luxury watches, they want the most exclusive offerings.

You feel it in tech culture too. I’m in several “Founders x Watches” group chats that get 100+ notifications a day, and I’m a recurring guest on TBPN to talk about what notable execs are wearing. Earlier this year, Sam Altman commissioned seven six-figure custom OpenAI watches from Vanguart, a Swiss independent brand that’s made fewer than 200 watches in its history. Mark Zuckerberg has rapidly built one of the most impressive collections of hard-to-find watches. The Patek Philippe Aquanaut has become the unofficial first purchase for recently exited founders (kind of a joke, but the price of a steel 5167 is up about 35% this year, per Bezel’s pricing data).

But it’s not just San Francisco. The broader market has an insatiable interest in the objectively rare, higher priced items that signal taste and access. From August to October, we broke our individual sale record every month, ending in an October sale of a $4.1 million Richard Mille. Earlier this month Phillips held the biggest watch auction in history: $112.3 million, with the top lot being a $10.59 million F.P. Journe. According to the Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry, export volumes have actually fallen about 43% since 2016, but export value has risen about 32%. Fewer watches, higher prices, longer waitlists.

This is happening in all verticals nestled in the upper crust of luxury: playoff tickets, fine art, old wine, graded collectibles, waitlisted cars and bags. During June’s NBA Finals, the average Knicks ticket at Madison Square Garden hit about $6,500, according to SeatGeek, more than triple the previous Finals record. Christie’s, Sotheby’s, and Phillips grew auction sales 71%, 59%, and 60%, respectively, in the first half of 2026.

I think it’s just getting started (plenty of late-stage IPO candidates next year like OpenAI, Databricks, Stripe, Anduril, etc.) and the luxury market is yet to really price it in. If Thursday, and the months that follow, go the way insiders hope, it will continue to mint new buyers and act as a major catalyst for secondary markets. The more we interact with AI, the more we’ll pay for what it can’t make.
