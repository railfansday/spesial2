Most states hide agent discipline on license lookups, CPC says

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

A CPC report reviewed state real estate license search portals and found most bury or omit disciplinary actions. Only Delaware, Idaho, and Illinois flagged discipline on the first page, while 17 states showed no actions in license lookups  AI Summary

Only a handful of states make it easy for consumers to see disciplinary actions against real estate agents, and many states either bury or omit that information from public license lookups, according to a new report from the Consumer Policy Center (CPC).

The report, Evaluating State Disclosure of Disciplinary Actions Against Real Estate Agents, audited how all 50 states present discipline on their official real estate license search portals.

Author Wendy Gilch, a CPC fellow, reviewed each state’s online license lookup between September and November 2026, examining what a consumer would see when checking an agent’s record. The study looked at whether disciplinary actions are easy to find, easy to understand and whether past brokerage employment is disclosed.

Across 47 states with usable results, only three, Delaware, Idaho and Illinois, flagged a disciplinary action on the first page a consumer sees, according to the report. In 27 states, a consumer would see a clean summary page and only discover an action after clicking deeper into the profile or scrolling down.

In the remaining 17 states, consumers could not find the known disciplinary action on the license lookup at all, even when the state had taken public action in separate documents or newsletters. Only eight states described violations in plain language, and only Illinois both clearly flagged and explained the discipline. Just two states, Arizona and California, showed an agent’s prior brokerage affiliations.

The findings highlight a gap between state real estate commissions’ stated mission to protect the public and the tools they give consumers to vet agents.

“State regulators need no additional authority to increase consumer access to important information about agents they may employ,” Gilch said in the CPC announcement. “Regulators need only a stronger commitment to their statutory obligation to protect home buyers and sellers.”

The report details multiple ways information is obscured, including “hiding” it behind extra clicks, separating the information from the license record and contradictory information fields.

States that the report said hide this information behind extra clicks and webpages include Oregon, Michigan and Kansas, while states like Arkansas, Hawaii, Maryland, Minnesota, Missouri, Nevada, New Jersey, South Dakota and West Virginia publish disciplinary actions in newsletters or batch postings but do not link them to the individual license lookup record.

Additionally, in New Hampshire, a licensee previously disciplined for unprofessional conduct was still marked “no” for disciplinary action on the new system, and another licensee’s violation appeared next to a green checkmark labeled “disciplinary action.” Other states bury references to discipline under education or employment history.

Beyond this, only Arizona and California show prior brokerages, the report said, leaving consumers unable to see if an agent has moved repeatedly between firms — a potential signal of performance or conduct issues.

The report also flags the use of nonpublic letters or warnings in place of formal discipline. In Oregon, “Educational Letters of Advice” for minor violations are not treated as discipline, are excluded from disciplinary records and are expunged after six years, according to the state real estate commissioner’s public explanation cited in the report. CPC found Oregon issued 106 such letters in 2025, nearly three for every one of its 39 public disciplinary actions.

Gilch argues that even “minor” mishandling of a transaction is relevant to consumers choosing an agent and that buyers and sellers can distinguish between lesser and more serious violations if given the information.

CPC’s analysis extends to how states handle agents facing felony or other serious criminal charges. The report contrasts aggressive use of emergency suspension powers in some cases with long delays or inaction in others.

In Texas, for example, the report notes that a licensee charged with possession of child sexual abuse material remained licensed and able to work with families in their homes for more than three years after charges were filed. The Texas Real Estate Commission did not revoke the license until September 2026, and the public lookup showed an active license with no disciplinary history during that period, based on the report’s review.

The report also cites a Texas licensee indicted for COVID-19 relief fraud in February 2024 who kept a license until April 2026, even though she was sentenced in December 2025. At the same time, Texas law allows a three-member panel to temporarily suspend a license without a conviction when continued practice would be a “continuing threat to the public welfare.” Similar inconsistencies were documented in Tennessee and Louisiana, where timing and severity of interim measures differed from case to case.

The report stresses that presumption of innocence still applies and notes that second chances have a place in licensing. But it concludes that charges involving children, violence or financial fraud pose risks too serious to leave undisclosed or unaddressed while cases are pending.

CPC also tested whether artificial intelligence tools could compensate for weak state disclosure. Using an AI model to ask whether specific agents had been disciplined, the report found that in 34 of 47 states tested the model returned no record of any action.

When AI did find discipline, it pulled from commission meeting minutes, orders and newsletters — not from the consumer-facing license lookups regulators direct the public to use. That suggests current website structures and search limitations make it difficult for AI systems to surface relevant information, undercutting the idea that technology alone can close disclosure gaps.

The report offers several specific steps states can take without new statutory authority, including flag any disciplinary action prominently on the main license page or initial search results, using plain language to describe what happened, making records permanent and searchable, including letters and warnings, disclosing housing- and consumer-protection-related violations, not just criminal convictions and showing prior brokerage employment. The CPC also suggested regulators structure online records so search engines and AI tools can access and surface disciplinary actions.

For housing professionals, the report is a reminder that brokerages, lenders and MLSs cannot rely solely on state lookups to vet agents and associated licensees. Firms may need to build additional compliance workflows, including direct review of commission orders and newsletters where available, and reassess how they monitor for new criminal charges.

“Consumers spending hundreds of thousands of dollars on a purchase should be given information about serious state or federal charges against agents on whom they depend,” Gilch said. “Consumers should have the ability to determine whether the information will affect their choice of agent.”
