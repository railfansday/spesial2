Webb captures a galaxy still reeling from an ancient collision

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Webb’s infrared view of the Cartwheel Galaxy reveals how an ancient collision reshaped its structure and sparked waves of star formation.

Today’s Image of the Day features the Cartwheel Galaxy, where a bright ring of stars surrounds a glowing center and curved strands of dust stretch between them like spokes.

The striking shape emerged from a violent collision that disrupted a spiral galaxy hundreds of millions of years ago.

New stars now shine along the outer ring, while traces of the galaxy’s earlier structure remain woven through the scene.

The view comes from the James Webb Space Telescope, which combined observations from two infrared instruments to reveal stars and dust in extraordinary detail.

Released on August 2, 2022, the image also includes two smaller companion galaxies and a background scattered with more distant galaxies.

Located roughly 500 million light-years away in the constellation Sculptor, the Cartwheel belongs to an unusual class known as ring galaxies.

Its appearance differs sharply from the familiar spiral arms of our Milky Way.

Astronomers attribute that shape to a high-speed collision between a large spiral galaxy and a smaller galaxy.

The smaller galaxy responsible for the impact is outside this image, so neither of the two prominent companions should be mistaken for the intruder.

The encounter sent disturbances outward through the larger galaxy, producing a bright inner ring and a much broader outer ring.

NASA compares their expansion to ripples spreading across a pond after a stone hits the water.

Those enormous ripples have reshaped the galaxy over hundreds of millions of years. What appears to be a finished wheel is still undergoing change.

The outer ring marks a region where the collision’s effects continue to unfold. As it expands into surrounding gas, it triggers the formation of new stars.

NASA estimates that this outer structure has been expanding for about 440 million years.

Star formation and supernovas, the explosions of dying stars, help make it an especially active part of the galaxy.

The center has its own concentrations of activity. Hot dust fills the bright core, and its most luminous areas contain enormous clusters of young stars.

Together, these regions show how a galactic collision can do more than rearrange existing material.

It can also change where new generations of stars emerge, leaving a pattern that remains visible long after the original encounter.

Earlier observations, including images from the Hubble Space Telescope, had already revealed the Cartwheel’s unusual structure. But dust obscures parts of the galaxy when viewed in visible light.

Webb observes infrared wavelengths, allowing astronomers to examine stars that are harder to distinguish through that dusty veil.

Its Near-Infrared Camera, or NIRCam, reveals points of starlight and pockets of star formation.

It also helps distinguish the smoother distribution of older stars from the clumpier regions associated with younger populations.

The Mid-Infrared Instrument, known as MIRI, adds a different perspective by detecting emission from dust and revealing material rich in hydrocarbons and silicate compounds.

Combining the instruments brings the stellar populations and dusty structures into the same view.

Each contributes information that would be harder to recognize from the other instrument alone.

The image’s colors translate infrared observations into a form human eyes can see. They help separate different features rather than reproduce the view an astronaut would see through a window.

Blue points mark individual stars or regions of star formation.

Bright red strands trace glowing dust, including the curved spokes that connect the inner and outer portions of the galaxy.

Those spokes preserve something of the spiral galaxy that existed before the collision.

Despite the disruption, the Cartwheel retained rotating arms, linking its present appearance to its earlier form.

Similar glowing dust also appears in the companion spiral galaxy toward the upper left, giving viewers another structure to explore beyond the dominant ring.

The Cartwheel’s eventual shape remains uncertain.

Its expanding rings and surviving spiral features capture a galaxy in transition, with its past still visible inside the changes set in motion by the collision.

That makes the image more than a portrait of an unusual object.

Across its bright rim and dusty spokes, the Cartwheel records how one encounter can influence a galaxy for hundreds of millions of years.

The collision has passed. The galaxy is still responding.
