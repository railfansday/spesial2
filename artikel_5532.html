How 10 countries celebrate the winter solstice

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Winter solstice celebrations around the world range from yuzu baths in Japan and poetry-filled Yalda nights in Iran to sunrise vigils at Ireland's Newgrange and Bolivia's Tiwanaku

The winter solstice is a single astronomical moment. In the 10 countries on this list, it is marked with citrus floating in bathwater, bowls of red bean porridge and poetry read aloud late into the night. It is also marked by a narrow beam of sunlight crossing the floor of a stone tomb. Each practice responds to the same fact: for one night, darkness lasts longer than at any other point in the year.

The solstice happens when one hemisphere reaches its greatest tilt away from the sun. On that day the sun traces its lowest arc across the sky, and daylight reaches its yearly minimum. After it, the days begin to lengthen again. That turning point helps explain why so many winter solstice traditions focus on light, warmth, renewal and the return of the sun.

The date depends on the hemisphere. North of the equator, the longest night of the year falls on December 21 or 22. South of the equator, it falls on June 20 or 21, when the northern half of the planet is in summer. Three of the countries here — Peru, Bolivia and Australia — sit in the Southern Hemisphere. Their solstice observances take place in June. Some traditions follow the astronomical date, while others hold to a fixed day on the calendar.

The customs in this selection of winter solstice celebrations around the world fall into roughly three groups. The first centers on the household and the table. In East Asia and Iran, families gather for specific foods that tradition links to health, protection or togetherness. Those foods range from kabocha squash in Japan to pomegranates during Iran's Yalda Night. The second gathers people at ancient stone sites in Ireland, England and Bolivia to watch the solstice sun. The third includes ceremonies that have been revived or newly created. Examples include a reenactment of an Inca sun festival in Cusco and a contemporary arts festival in Tasmania.

The Hopi ceremony of Soyal in northern Arizona stands somewhat apart. It is a living religious observance, and much of it is closed to outsiders.

The 10 entries that follow are a selection, not a ranking or a complete survey. Each slide covers one country, one central practice and the meaning attached to it. Any entry can be read on its own.

On Toji, the Japanese name for the winter solstice, many households soak in yuzu-yu, a hot bath with whole yuzu fruits floating in it, and eat kabocha squash. Both customs are traditionally linked to staying healthy through the coldest months of the year.

Toji is one of the 24 solar terms of the traditional East Asian calendar. That system divides the year according to the sun's position. Japan adopted it from China centuries ago. In the Japanese sequence, Toji falls between Taisetsu, or "great snow," and Shokan, or "lesser cold."

Yuzu is a citrus fruit roughly the size of a mandarin orange, with a bumpy yellow rind and a strong, sharp aroma. Its juice is too sour to eat the way people eat an orange. Cooks in Japan use the zest and juice instead. They flavor sauces such as ponzu, as well as soups and sweets. Kochi Prefecture, on the island of Shikoku, is the country's largest yuzu producer.

For the bath, whole fruits go into the hot water, sometimes cut or pricked first to release more scent. Tradition holds that a yuzu bath on Toji helps ward off colds through the winter. The custom also rests on wordplay. Toji can be heard as a word for a curative soak at a hot spring, and yuzu echoes a word meaning flexible or accommodating.

Public bathhouses, known as sento, often fill their tubs with yuzu on the solstice. That carries the custom beyond private homes and into neighborhood life.

Kabocha is a winter squash with dark green skin and dense orange flesh. In English it is often called Japanese pumpkin. It is harvested in late summer and autumn and keeps for months in storage. In earlier times, that made it one of the few vegetables still on hand in midwinter. Eating it on Toji is traditionally associated with avoiding illness during the cold season.

Kabocha also fits a separate solstice custom. Some families eat foods whose names contain the sound "n," which is linked to un, a Japanese word for luck. Another name for kabocha is nankin, which carries the sound twice. Other foods in this group include renkon, or lotus root, ninjin, or carrot, and ginnan, or ginkgo nuts.

A common home dish for the day is itoko-ni, kabocha simmered with sweetened adzuki beans. In Japanese tradition, red adzuki beans are believed to keep bad luck away. That belief gives the dish a protective meaning at the start of the coldest season.

The Dongzhi Festival is China's traditional winter solstice holiday, and families usually mark it with a shared meal. Households in southern China customarily serve tangyuan, while families in the north tend to make jiaozi dumplings.

The name Dongzhi translates roughly as "winter's arrival" or "the extreme of winter." It is one of the 24 solar terms in the traditional Chinese calendar. That system divides the year according to the sun's position. It has long guided the timing of farm work. Dongzhi is the term that begins on the shortest day.

Traditional Chinese cosmology treats the solstice as a turning point between yin and yang. Yin, associated with darkness and cold, reaches its peak on the longest night. Yang, associated with light and warmth, begins to grow again from that point. In that view, the festival marks both the depth of winter and the start of the sun's return.

Tangyuan are balls of glutinous rice flour served in a sweet soup or broth. Some are plain, and others are filled with black sesame, red bean paste or peanuts. Their round shape is linked to family togetherness. The name also sounds close to tuanyuan, a Chinese word for reunion. That echo ties the dish to the holiday's focus on gathering relatives at one table.

Jiaozi are crescent-shaped dumplings, usually boiled, with fillings such as pork, cabbage or mutton. A northern folk saying holds that anyone who skips dumplings on Dongzhi will get frostbitten ears. The saying is tied to a legend about Zhang Zhongjing, a physician of the Eastern Han dynasty. In the story, he made ear-shaped dumplings stuffed with mutton and warming herbs. He gave them to villagers whose ears had frozen in the cold.

The north-south split is a broad pattern, not a rule for every household. Many families eat both dishes, and regional specialties vary from province to province. Some households also make offerings to ancestors as part of the day.

An old saying, "Dongzhi is as important as the new year," reflects how seriously many families once treated the holiday. The festival is also observed in Taiwan and by Chinese communities across Southeast Asia. Tangyuan are a common part of the meal in many of those places. Other East Asian communities mark the date as well.

Koreans mark Dongji, the winter solstice, by eating patjuk, a thick porridge made from red beans. Tradition holds that its deep red color keeps bad spirits and misfortune away from the household.

The beans are pat, the small red beans known in English as adzuki beans. Cooks boil them until soft, then mash or strain them into a smooth paste. The paste is thinned with water and simmered with rice into a dense, earthy porridge. Most bowls are seasoned with a little salt, and some people add sugar at the table.

The dish is rarely served plain. Cooks roll glutinous rice flour into small, chewy balls called saealsim and drop them into the pot. The name roughly translates as "bird's egg," a reference to their size and round shape. A common household custom has each person eat as many saealsim as their age.

The protective meaning of the porridge rests on color symbolism. In Korean folk belief, red is linked to yang energy and is thought to repel malevolent spirits. Those spirits were believed to be strongest on the longest night of the year. Families traditionally set out a bowl at the household shrine before eating. Some also spread spoonfuls of patjuk on gates, walls or doorways to guard the home.

Dongji also carries the nickname "little New Year," or jageun seol. The name reflects an older view of the solstice as the turning point of the year, after which the days begin to lengthen again. Some families still say that finishing a bowl of patjuk makes a person one year older.

The lunar calendar changes the custom in some years. When Dongji falls in the first 10 days of the 11th lunar month, it is called aedongji, or "child's Dongji." Tradition holds that patjuk is unlucky for children in those years. Many households serve red bean rice cake instead.

The solstice was also tied to timekeeping in the past. During the Joseon dynasty, the royal court marked Dongji by handing out calendars for the coming year.

Patjuk is not limited to a single night. Porridge shops and traditional markets across South Korea sell it through the colder months. Some people now buy a bowl on Dongji itself rather than cooking the beans at home.

On Shab-e Yalda, the longest night of the year, Iranian families stay up late around bowls of pomegranates, watermelon, nuts and dried fruit. They also read poetry aloud, most often the verses of Hafez.

The night goes by several names. Yalda is a word borrowed from Syriac, where it means birth. Many Iranians link it to the idea of the sun being reborn as the days start to lengthen again. The other common name, Chelleh, comes from chehel, the Persian word for 40. It refers to the first 40 days of winter, which begin after this night.

On the Iranian calendar, Yalda falls on the last night of the month of Azar, just before the month of Dey begins. Gatherings usually take place at the home of the oldest relatives, often grandparents. Extended family members often travel to be there. The evening runs well past midnight, and staying awake through the long night is part of the custom.

Food carries much of the symbolism. Pomegranates and watermelon are both red inside. Many families associate that color with dawn and the warmth of the sun. Folk tradition holds that eating watermelon on Yalda helps keep a person healthy through the cold months. Pistachios, almonds, walnuts, dried figs and raisins round out the table, often alongside tea and sweets.

Poetry is the other centerpiece. Hafez was a 14th-century poet from Shiraz. His collected works, the Divan, sit on the shelf in many Iranian homes. A popular Yalda custom is fal-e Hafez. A person makes a silent wish, opens the Divan at random and reads the poem on that page as a kind of answer. An older relative often does the reading and interpreting for the rest of the family.

In some homes, the gathering still takes place around a korsi. This is a low table set over a heat source and covered with heavy blankets. People sit around it and tuck their legs underneath to stay warm.

The tradition reaches beyond Iran. Communities in Afghanistan, Central Asia and the Persian-speaking diaspora also keep the night. In 2022, UNESCO added Yalda/Chelleh to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Iran and Afghanistan submitted the nomination jointly.

At Newgrange in County Meath, the rising sun around the winter solstice shines through an opening above the entrance and lights the tomb's inner chamber. The beam lasts roughly 17 minutes before it withdraws.

Newgrange is a Neolithic passage tomb about 5,000 years old. That makes it older than Stonehenge in England. It stands in the Boyne Valley as part of Brú na Bóinne, a complex that also includes the passage tombs of Knowth and Dowth. UNESCO inscribed Brú na Bóinne as a World Heritage Site in 1993.

The structure is a large circular mound built over a long stone passage. That passage leads to a cross-shaped chamber deep inside the mound. Large kerbstones ring the base, and the stone at the entrance is carved with spirals and other geometric patterns.

The opening that admits the light is known as the roof box. It is a rectangular gap set above the doorway. The passage climbs toward the chamber, and the raised opening lets the low midwinter sun clear that slope. Through the doorway alone, the light would not reach the back of the tomb.

Archaeologist Michael J. O'Kelly, who led excavations at the site, recorded the effect in modern times. He watched the sunlight enter the chamber in 1967. His team also found burned and unburned human remains inside.

The alignment shows that the builders tracked the sun's yearly path closely. They framed a single seasonal moment in stone, thousands of years before writing reached Ireland.

Spaces inside the chamber on the solstice mornings are handed out through an annual lottery. The chamber is small. Only a limited group can stand inside at once. Everyone else can follow the event through a live stream, and people also gather outside the mound at dawn.

Winning a place does not guarantee the view. December skies in Ireland are often overcast. When cloud covers the sunrise, no beam enters the passage, and the chamber stays dark.

Stonehenge, the prehistoric stone circle in Wiltshire, lines up with two solar events: sunset on the winter solstice and sunrise on the summer solstice. English Heritage, which manages the site, opens the circle to the public around the solstices, and crowds gather among the stones.

The monument's main axis runs along a single line. In one direction it points northeast toward midsummer sunrise, and in the other it points southwest toward midwinter sunset. One layout can serve both events because the two points sit at opposite ends of the same line. A person standing inside the circle at the close of the shortest day can watch the sun go down along that axis.

Which of the two moments mattered more to the builders remains an open question. Researchers continue to debate whether the winter sunset or the summer sunrise was the primary target, or whether both carried meaning. The physical alignment is not in dispute. The intention behind it is.

The circle is built from two kinds of stone. The large sarsens are a local sandstone, while the smaller bluestones were brought from the Preseli Hills in west Wales. An earthwork route known as the Avenue leads away from the monument along the same solstice axis and eventually reaches the River Avon.

On most days, visitors follow a path that keeps them outside the ring of stones. Solstice access works differently. During these managed openings, people can walk among the stones themselves. The events draw a mix of tourists, local residents, druids and modern pagans. Some arrive for ceremony, and others come simply to watch the light.

The summer solstice sunrise is the larger and better-known of the two gatherings. The winter event falls in the cold and dark of December, and winter visitors often gather for the sunrise as well as the sunset.

Stonehenge is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site together with the nearby Avebury monuments.

Inti Raymi, the festival of the sun, is held in Cusco every June 24 as winter begins in the Andes. It honors Inti, the Inca sun god, and today it is a staged reenactment rather than an unbroken religious tradition.

The date falls a few days after the June solstice. The festival's name comes from Quechua, the language of the Inca state. In Quechua, inti means sun and raymi means festival.

Cusco was the capital of the Inca Empire, and many of its streets still run past walls built on Inca stone foundations. Inti Raymi was one of the most important ceremonies of the Inca year. After the Spanish conquest, colonial authorities suppressed it. The public celebration then disappeared for centuries.

The version staged today dates from a 1944 revival. Its organizers rebuilt the ceremony largely from written accounts of the colonial era. The best known of these is by Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, a chronicler born in Cusco in the 16th century. The costumes, script and staging are a modern production informed by those records. They are not a ritual passed down unchanged from the Inca.

The central role belongs to an actor who plays the Sapa Inca, the title given to the Inca ruler. Much of the spoken performance is delivered in Quechua. The production moves through the city in stages over the course of the day.

It begins at the Qorikancha, the Inca temple of the sun. Spanish builders later raised the Church and Convent of Santo Domingo on top of its walls. The performance then continues in the Plaza de Armas, the city's main square.

The procession ends at Sacsayhuamán, an Inca complex on a hill above Cusco. The site is known for massive stone blocks fitted together without mortar. The main ceremony takes place there. It includes offerings to the sun and a llama sacrifice that is only simulated in the modern staging.

June 24 is also Cusco's city day. That gives Inti Raymi a civic role as well as a historical one. Residents and visitors fill the streets along the procession route to watch the performance move from the temple to the hilltop.

Bolivia's Aymara communities welcome their new year, Willkakuti, at the June solstice by gathering before dawn at the Tiwanaku archaeological site to greet the first sunrise. In Aymara, the name is usually translated as the return of the sun.

The holiday sets the start of the year by the sun rather than by January 1. For farming communities on the high plains, the solstice marks the start of a new agricultural cycle. Willkakuti treats that turn as the moment the year begins. Bolivia observes the Aymara New Year as a national holiday.

Tiwanaku sits on the Altiplano, the high plateau of western Bolivia, a short distance south of Lake Titicaca. It lies roughly 70 kilometers west of La Paz, the country's seat of government. The site was the center of a civilization that flourished centuries before the Inca Empire. UNESCO added it to its World Heritage List in 2000.

The usual gathering point is the Kalasasaya, a large rectangular enclosure walled with tall stone pillars. The Gateway of the Sun, a doorway carved from a single block of andesite, stands in the same part of the complex. The Akapana, a terraced platform mound, rises nearby.

Crowds arrive in darkness and wait in the cold. June is winter on the Altiplano, and nights at that altitude regularly fall below freezing. Aymara ritual specialists, known as amautas, lead prayers and burn offerings to Pachamama, the Andean earth mother, while the sky brightens.

When the sun clears the horizon, many people raise their open palms toward it to receive the first rays. In Aymara tradition, the gesture takes in the energy of the returning sun as the new year begins.

The ceremony draws Aymara families alongside other Bolivians and foreign visitors. The Aymara people also live in neighboring Peru and Chile. Their language is still spoken widely around Lake Titicaca. Willkakuti keeps an Andean way of reckoning time in public use alongside the Gregorian calendar.

The Hopi of northern Arizona hold Soyal at the winter solstice to welcome the sun back after the longest night of the year. The ceremony also opens the Hopi ceremonial calendar and sets the cycle of observances that follows through the rest of the year.

The Hopi live in villages on and around three mesas in northeastern Arizona, known as First Mesa, Second Mesa and Third Mesa. The Hopi Reservation is surrounded by the Navajo Nation. Soyal is held in these villages, and much of it takes place in kivas. Kivas are underground ceremonial chambers that serve as gathering places for religious societies.

The central idea of Soyal is the sun's return. At the solstice, the sun rises and sets at the southernmost points of its yearly path along the horizon. In Hopi tradition, the ceremony helps turn the sun back toward its summer course. It also renews the community for the year ahead.

Soyal also marks the time when the kachinas, known in Hopi as katsinam, are traditionally understood to return to the villages. Kachinas are spirit beings in Hopi belief, associated with rain, growth and well-being. Their arrival at the start of the ceremonial year opens a season of kachina observances. That season closes in summer with the Niman ceremony, often called the Home Dance, when the kachinas are understood to depart.

Prayer sticks, called pahos, are a widely documented part of Soyal. They are carved from wood and decorated with feathers. Pahos are made to carry prayers for health, rain and good harvests. They are offered on behalf of family members, neighbors, crops and animals.

Public detail about Soyal is limited by design. Outsiders are not admitted to many Hopi ceremonies, including much of this one. The Hopi Tribe also restricts photography, recording and sketching in its villages. Accounts written by visitors in the past sometimes described rites the community considered private. Published descriptions therefore tend to cover the ceremony's purpose and its best-known symbols rather than its inner workings.

Dark Mofo is a contemporary arts festival that marks the winter solstice in Hobart, the capital of Tasmania. The Museum of Old and New Art, known as MONA, started it in 2013 and holds it in June, around the shortest day of the year.

Hobart is Australia's southernmost state capital. Its midwinter days are shorter than those in mainland cities farther north, such as Sydney or Brisbane. The festival's program is built around those long hours of darkness.

Dark Mofo is not an ancient rite or a revival of one. It is a modern event created by a private museum. It uses the longest night as material for art, music, food and public gatherings. It has no religious ceremonies handed down over generations.

MONA opened in 2011 on a peninsula beside the River Derwent, north of central Hobart. Its founder is the collector David Walsh. A summer festival of music and art, MONA FOMA, often shortened to Mofo, predates the winter event. The name Dark Mofo marks the June festival as its cold-season counterpart.

The Nude Solstice Swim is one of the festival's regular events. Participants undress and wade or dive into the River Derwent at dawn on a morning close to the solstice. The water is cold at that time of year. Swimmers enter together rather than one by one.

Fire is another recurring element. Dark Mofo has staged a large public bonfire, and several editions have burned an ogoh-ogoh. That is a towering effigy adapted from a Balinese tradition, on which visitors write down their fears before it goes up in flames.

Food is a third strand. Winter Feast, an evening food market on the Hobart waterfront, runs alongside the arts program during the festival. It gives the event a regular outdoor gathering point after dark.

The program changes from one edition to the next, so specific artworks, performances and venues vary. The setting stays the same: a small southern city on the Derwent, a museum-run festival and a fixed place on the calendar at midwinter.
