United Pilots Squawk Hijack Code As Passenger Tries To Storm Cockpit, Says He Has A Bomb

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

This article was updated on Wednesday, November 11, 2026, to include details on the passenger's arrest and potential federal charges. It was originally published on Tuesday, November 10, 2026.

A United Airlines flight from Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR) to Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport (MSY) took an eventful turn on Monday evening after a passenger reportedly tried to storm the cockpit. The alarming occurrence led the pilots to alert other United flights to the 'Level 4' threat, the most severe threat level indicating an attempted or actual breach of the cockpit, via the ACARS messaging system.

The flight ultimately landed safely in New Orleans. The response of the crew, which included issuing the squawk code 7500 to indicate unlawful interference, shows how seriously such threats are taken. It also comes just weeks after another United aircraft diverted following reports of a passenger threatening to hijack the plane.

United Airlines flight UA2174 is a regularly scheduled evening flight from Newark to New Orleans. On Monday, November 9, flight tracking data shows it departed Newark at 9:26 PM and touched down in New Orleans at 11:07 PM. However, during the flight, as flagged by FlightDeck on X, a male passenger tried to storm the Boeing 737 MAX 9's cockpit.

The pilots squawked 7500 to alert air traffic control to the threat, and later reported that he was carrying a backpack that he said contained a bomb. In an ACARS message to other United jets, they said:

"We had an attempted level four, no other details are known at this time."

7500 was first squawked as the jet descended through 20,000 feet, and it landed safely. Simple Flying reached out to United Airlines for further information on this incident, with a spokesperson confirming that there were 46 passengers and six members of crew on board. They added that:

"United flight 2174 from Newark to New Orleans landed safely following a disruptive incident involving a passenger onboard. Law enforcement met the aircraft upon arrival. "

Further details of the incident have since emerged. According to local police, the 34-year-old man — identified as Honduran national Rony Alexander Gomez-Alberto — began acting irrationally during the flight, reportedly telling crew members that he had a bomb. The DHS also stated that Gomez-Alberto had attempted to enter the cockpit.

Gomez-Alberto, who authorities said was living in New York, remains in custody without bond. It has also been confirmed that he is wanted by ICE for immigration offenses, having previously been deported from the US twice. If convicted, making a bomb threat on an aircraft is a federal crime punishable by up to ten years in prison.

After the aircraft landed in New Orleans, police arrested him, and he was later charged with communicating false information about planned arson and terrorizing. The FAA has confirmed that it will investigate the incident, while United has referred further questions to local authorities.

By the time N17422 — the Boeing 737 MAX 9 operating United Airlines flight UA2174 — had arrived at its gate in New Orleans on Monday night, the passenger was said to be at the back of the aircraft, sitting with cabin crew. Few other details are known at this point. However, given the serious nature of his actions on board, police officers were likely waiting for him.

It was only last month that one of United's planes had to be diverted and locked down after a flight attendant spotted a passenger sending texts about hijacking the plane. In this instance, the jet was a Boeing 737 MAX 8 (as seen above) flying from Las Vegas to Newark.

With the suspicious messages spotted shortly after takeoff, the aircraft returned to Harry Reid International Airport (LAS), where it was met by law enforcement, rather than continuing to Newark. According to People, the texter, a 25-year-old male passenger, was under the influence of alcohol and was later arrested for making false threats regarding terrorism.

A Las Vegas flight turned back after cabin crew spotted a disturbing text and officers met the aircraft on return.

In recent times, 'squawk 7500' has become an increasingly common phrase in coverage of such alarming incidents as these. Arguably the most notable this year was the attempted hijacking of flydubai flight FZ1073 on its way from Dubai International Airport (DXB) to Tel Aviv Ben Gurion Airport (TLV). This flight issued the squawk codes 7700 and 7500, but what do they mean, and why are they so important?

Squawk codes are essentially four-digit numbers that pilots can set their aircraft's transponder to in order to indicate their status to ATC. 7700 is used as a general emergency indicator, highlighting that a plane needs priority treatment. 7500 indicates a hijacking or unlawful interference, and it is a way of alerting ATC non-verbally without the hijackers knowing that controllers are aware of the situation.
