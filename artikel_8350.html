Between the Lines: Writer in the Woods

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

“Hey Snacks!” Show calls to me from where I can’t see him over the other side of a small hill. I’ve been hiking a little behind him this morning.

“What?” I yell back as I quicken my pace. Cresting the hill, I see him standing with three other people. One of them is around our age and tall with long brown hair, a long brown beard, and a big smile. He’s wearing a printed buttondown shirt. Immediately I recognize him.

“No way!” I say, trotting down the hill to reach the four of them.

If you’re not aware, Jolly is the owner of Jolly Gear, a hiking apparel brand popular in the thru-hiking community for their funky patterned buttondown shirts. We introduce ourselves and learn that he’s out hiking for a few days in Georgia with his parents. When I tell him my name is Toddler Snacks, Jolly replies, “fruit snacks?”

I laugh, and say “yes, exactly!” You’d be surprised how many people don’t guess Goldfish or fruit snacks right off the bat.

Jolly’s dad tells us his trail name is Power Slide, something to do with falling and sliding in the mud. “At least they didn’t call you Skidmark,” Show quips, and we all laugh.

Jolly’s mom suggests she get a picture of the three of us. I ask her if she can take one on my phone too, and she obliges.

I wasn’t even wearing my Jolly Gear shirt at the time… embarrassing.

I wasn’t there for this part, but heard later that Show hadn’t recognized him right away. When he passed by he saw the shirt Jolly was wearing and commented, “Nice shirt! I have the same one in blue!” to which Jolly’s dad replied, “Well, this is Jolly!” It sounded like a humorously heartwarming interaction. Looking back, I’m still so thrilled we just so happened to run into him out there. What are the chances? He certainly lived up to his name; both Jolly and his parents were super friendly people.

Side note, I LOVED the shirt he was wearing. It was the print that has graphics from all three of the triple crown trails but in a never released (to my knowledge) color: a rust brown. Brown is one of my favorite colors, perhaps my all time favorite when it comes to clothing. I’m patiently waiting to see if he’ll end up putting this one on his website or not, because I would love to have in it my collection. If not, maybe I’ll reach out to him and see if he’s willing to make me one…

After saying goodbye to Jolly and his folks, Show and I continued hiking. A few easy miles later we reached Dick’s Creek Gap, where we were planning to catch the free hiker shuttle into our first real town stop: Hiawassee, Georgia. Approaching the gap, we heard someone calling out to us, inaudibly, but angrily. We looked over and saw a young guy, no more than very early twenties, with chin-length blond hair. His large brown pack identified him as another thru-hiker. We gave him an awkward wave, unsure what he said to us or why he sounded mad.

The shuttle showed up shortly and us and the young guy loaded in. He sat across the aisle from us, staring moodily out the window, seemingly ignoring us. Eventually, halfway through the drive, he turned to us.

“Sorry I yelled at you guys earlier, I thought you were someone else.” He went on to explain that he’d run into another couple on trail recently who hadn’t been too nice to him. Evidently he was not happy about it and wanted to let them have it. A case of mistaken identity.

Approaching the town limits, the shuttle driver asked us where we wanted to be dropped off. Show and I told her the local diner (which I cannot remember or find the name of). The young guy implored her to drop him at the Taco Bell, because that’s what he could afford. He further added that he’s broke because he’s only nineteen. Huh, another broke nineteen-year-old on trail this year. The kind shuttle driver, a hiker herself, offered to give him some freeze dried backpacking meals if he wanted them. He says he’ll take all the free food he can get, and will carry as much as he can fit in his pack.

In town, our first order of business, as always, was food. Show and I got a full breakfast spread at the diner, whose atmosphere was warmed by the calming southern drawls of the waitresses. We then resupplied at the Ingles, and headed over to Trailful, the outdoor outfitter, to pick up some backpacking meals. The outfitter even had a small room in the back for hikers to hang out, or store their packs as they ran errands around town. It quickly became our staging area for repackaging our resupply.

As we sat on the carpet transferring all of our processed treats from cardboard boxes into Ziplocs, another hiker entered the room. It was a probably 50-something man who introduced himself as Fraggle, a nickname he’d been dubbed in the Boy Scouts during his youth. I got a decidedly positive vibe from him immediately as the three of us made small talk and swapped stories of past hiking adventures. He seemed not only gleeful to be out hiking the Appalachian Trail, but had an infectious laugh.

We’d heard on FarOut that on Mondays, the town of Hiawassee puts on a so-called “Hiker Feed” where they provide a grilled lunch for the AT thru-hikers. Today just so happened to be a Monday, so we figured we’d check it out. I think it’s hilarious, by the way, that they call it a “feed.” As though we’re livestock or something. I mean, I guess it’s not totally off base to classify us as animals.

A highlight of the hostel was their orange cat, who had the most unique name of all the trail cats we met. Honestly probably the most unique name of any cat or pet I’ve ever met. Ready for it? The cat’s name was… Tuna Fish Cracker. Actually. Supposedly he (or she?) was named by the owners’ neighbor’s child, or maybe the owners’ grandchild, one or the other.

The following morning I was surprised to have woken up well-rested after a relatively snoreless night. Hostel bunkrooms are notorious for snoring, especially when over half of the patrons are men in their 50s and 60s, as was the case on this evening. Although I did wake up at one point in the middle of the night to a clattering and a yelp as First Cathole stumbled getting down from his top bunk (directly across from mine) to use the bathroom. We laughed about it in the morning when he asked if I’d heard the racket.

The hostel provided a homemade breakfast, which was a delicious brunch-style baked casserole of french toast, sausage, eggs and cheese and a side of banana bread. After eating, Show and I got packed and ready to head out on the first shuttle back to Dick’s Creek Gap. Before we hopped in the van, we of course took our obligatory photo with the green dragon wings painted on their barn doors, and signed our names.

Left to right: The Show, Toddler Snacks, Leigh, Fraggle, Sand Bag, Bill. Also, check out the boots and trail runners equal representation in this photo! #bootsarenotdead

Back on trail, Show and I hiked the majority of the day with Leigh. It started out as a misty morning at the Gap, the woods entirely enveloped in white cloud and bare trees dripping all around us. Just under 9 miles into our day we reached our second major milestone of the trail (the first being Neel Gap). We had reached the Georgia/North Carolina borderline and officially completed our first state!

The three of us took some photos with the unassuming weathered wooden sign bolted to a tree on the side of the trail and kept walking. A quarter or so mile down the trail, we heard some hooting and hollering coming from where we’d just been. A mimicked rooster crow, followed by an enthusiastic shouting of “BANGARANG!” Another group of hikers must have reached the border just behind us.

You don’t usually notice anything vastly different when you cross a state line on a thru-hike, particularly on the east coast where the climate is similar enough throughout. Even out west, you’ll see a steady landscape transition, such as the mixing of aspen and sagebrush in southern Colorado and northern New Mexico, long before and after you cross the actual border. I did, however, experience a noticeable difference entering North Carolina from Georgia.

I’ve heard people say that Georgia is one of the harder states on the AT northbound. This is due to it being the very first state needing to be hiked before trail legs arrive. I haven’t confirmed this myself, but it also supposedly has a lot of elevation gain on paper. That may be true, but going off the “real feel” on my body, North Carolina is harder. Particularly those very first couple climbs up Sharp Top and Courthouse Bald. The grade became perceptibly steeper and my lungs were working perceptibly harder to get me up it. Though you don’t peak out on either of those summits, there were some nice views that rewarded our efforts.

By the time we reached Muskrat Creek Shelter, our home for the night, the sun was shining and it had turned into a beautiful afternoon. After inspecting the shelter and deeming it a bit dark and dingy, and knowing rain wasn’t in the forecast that evening, Show and I decided to set up our tent. We selected a large flat spot about 50 feet from the shelter within a patch of rhododendron. As we were setting up, a bunch of hikers began trickling in.

All of them were people we’d at least met once before, including Noah, Alex, Braxton, Bill, and the nineteen-year-old from the Hiawassee shuttle whose name I still didn’t know. A mid-twenties guy named Rewind also showed up; I don’t believe I’ve mentioned him yet because we had only very briefly met him in passing a few days prior. I was excited to have so many others around to hang out and eat dinner with.

Either someone fucked up with the router or this sign was directing us to the privy, not entirely sure.

Once everyone was done setting up their tents or bedding in the shelter, we all gathered round the picnic table to make our dinners. At some point, the topic of conversation turned to how we were going to handle hanging our food. The state of Georgia lets hikers off easy on this front. All (or nearly all) shelter campsites are equipped with bear boxes or cables. Once you get to North Carolina the training wheels come off and you’re on your own. Your options are to hang it in a tree or sleep with it.

Show and I shared that we were going to sleep with our food, as we’ve been doing, without issue, for thousands of miles. As I expected, the others, most of them brand new to thru-hiking, didn’t judge our personal decision but seemed skeptical about doing so themselves. Just give it a few hundred miles and I’ll bet almost everyone at this table will be sleeping with their food too, I thought. I elected not to say this out loud. They’d get lazy and comfortable in their own time without me telling them they would.

Someone asked Leigh what she was going to do. In response, she deadpanned, “I’m just going to take my food bag, and shove it straight up my butt.” We all howled with laughter.

It was the first (of many) sassy things I’d hear her say. Up until this point she’d come across as genuinely warm and open, the kind of person who’s a friend to all and can not only talk to, but make a sincere connection with just about anyone. I hadn’t seen this goofy, slightly unhinged side of her yet, and I liked it.

Show followed her quip by suggesting her trail name be “Safe Pocket,” the meaning of which needs some backstory to explain. At home recently, Show had been playing a game on PlayStation called ARC Raiders. Within the game there is a special inventory know as the “Safe Pocket” where you can store valuable loot, preventing it from disappearing if your character dies. Among his online friends, it had turned into an inside joke to say “I’m shoving this straight up my ass” whenever one of them stored something in their Safe Pocket.

Once Show told this story to the group, everyone loved the name, even Leigh, but she still refused to accept it. “I don’t want to have to tell everyone that asks why that’s my trail name that it’s because I said I’d shove food up my butt!” she exclaimed, laughing. So unfortunately, the first name attempt didn’t stick, but it still deserved an honorable mention on the blog. The term “safe pocket” also became an ongoing tramily in-joke we’d mention periodically throughout the trail to refer to our backsides and/or simply make each other laugh.

As the collective conversation broke apart into multiple one-on-one side chats, my ears perked at the one happening next to me. Rewind, directly to my left at the picnic table, and the nineteen-year-old, sitting right across from him, were talking writing. I’d already heard from Leigh who’d hiked and chatted with him a bit that Rewind was an author who was actually writing a novel while on trail. Now, he was recapping his professional writing career so far for Nineteen and telling him about his current project. Every morning at the shelter he would work on his book for an hour or so before starting to hike for the day.

When it was his turn to share, Nineteen revealed that he writes poetry, and had only started doing so recently after a difficult breakup. They go back and forth discussing being writers while I’m secretly eavesdropping, probably pretending to be engaged in another conversation, but my attention is firmly on this one I’m not even a part of. (Tell me without telling me you have ADHD.)

I consider weaseling my way in. “Guys I’m a writer too!!”

But I stay silent. For some reason it feels utterly wrong to interject. I’m having a “sweetie, don’t interrupt while the adults are talking” moment that I can’t push past.

Writing and I have had a long, hot and cold, on-again-off-again dalliance. We’d flirt and hook up and fight, but I never thought I’d find myself married to the craft. Mostly it was a commitment issue on my end. And a personal insecurity that I wasn’t good enough for them. I still don’t think we’ve had our wedding day; at this point it’s probably more accurate to say we’re engaged. I’ve promised a life with Writing in it, but I’m not quite living it, signed and sealed, yet.

A lot of my struggle is surrounding the label “writer.” For most of my life I’ve effectively been one in mind and spirit, but it felt like too big a word to use to describe myself. Not writing often enough, that it’s a chaotic process for me, the uncertainty of whether I’m even any good at it (or just narcissistically think that I am) have all been excuses of mine not to use the descriptor. So aside from a few pieces published in adolescent writing anthologies and my high school’s lit mag 15+ years ago, it’s mostly been a closeted affair for me. Pages and pages of old writings are boxed up in my basement right now that have never seen the light of day. Effectively, I have nothing to show for it. To prove I am one.

I know this all might sound counterintuitive because here I am writing on the internet for thousands of people to read. But as humanity is a recent blip in Earth’s lengthy geologic history, so too is my being “out” as a writer. Just like being mid-thru-hike, I’ve come a long way, but I’ve still got a ways to go. These days the imposter syndrome has morphed into: you’re just a blogger, that’s not REAL writing. REAL writers are poets, novelists, memoirists, essayists, etc. So anyway, this long tangent is just to explain why I felt so weird blurting out to Rewind and Nineteen, “I’m a writer too, I blog for The Trek!” It sounds a tad trivial in comparison.

“Oh no…” Nineteen sighs. “I think I made way too much cous cous.” I look over and his pot is brimming with the tiny beige pellets. A small pile of rogue ones is littering the picnic table. We all laugh.

I look up at the sky where the sun is low enough to stream through the nearby rhododendrons. “We should go soon if we want to catch the sunset.” Everyone agrees, starting to shuffle around cleaning up from dinner. The shelter we’re at has a side trail to a rock outcropping called Raven Rock, where I remembered catching a dope sunset last time in 2020.

“Ugh, I still haven’t finished all my cous cous,” complains Nineteen, “I guess I’ll just have to bring it with me.”

Little by little, in groups of two or three, we make our way out to Raven Rock. I recalled it being fairly close, but after a quarter mile following the blue blazed trail through the woods I’m getting concerned. Most of us are wearing our camp shoes, which are not ideal for traveling long distances. It’s getting darker by the minute. At this rate we might miss the sunset. I think about giving up and heading back, but something tells me to just keep going a little further.

Eventually, after another quarter mile or so I pop out of a rhodo tunnel to a jumble of large gray rocks and open sky. “I think this is it!” I call back to Show and Noah who are a little behind me. I scramble up the rocks (conveniently didn’t remember any scrambling last time either) and the sunset comes into vision. “Guys it’s SO sick, it’s worth it!” I hear their pace quicken in the rustling leaves and the guys join me on the cliffside. An expanse of dusty blue mountains sits before me, each layer lightening a shade as they go further back. Above them is an ombre sky fading seamlessly from golden yellow to firey orange. The whole crew gathers on the rocks to take in the views.

“Oh no, I spilled some of my cous cous!” Nineteen exclaims. I look over my shoulder and see that he’s scrambling over a large boulder one handed, pot and spork in the other.

I laugh, “I can’t believe you actually brought that all the way out here!”

“Well I have to finish it!” he says anxiously. He finds a comfortable sitting spot, feet dangling off the cliffside and continues eating.

I can’t totally remember, but I want to say it was me that said it: “I think we should call you Cous Cous Sunset.” It gets a laugh and some vocalizations in the affirmative from the others.

“Noooooo!” Cous Cous Sunset groans dramatically. “I mean, I’m okay with the ‘cous cous’ part but not the ‘sunset’ part.”

“Why not?” I push back, “the whole point of the name is because you brought your cous cous all the way out here to see the sunset!” I loved it for him. And I decided right then and there that I was going to keep calling him that until it got however reluctantly accepted.

I’m happy to say the name did stick! He did mostly go by simply “Cous Cous” throughout his time on trail. But it was a real proud moment for me a few days later when I heard him introduce himself to another hiker with his full name: Cous Cous Sunset.

Left to right: Leigh, Show, Noah, Cous Cous Sunset

Later that night I wake up out of the black. The night is quiet aside from a muffled snore coming from the shelter. I look at my watch. It’s a little after 3AM. My brain’s inner monologue flips on without my consent. It starts writing, automatically.

Another thing I should mention on the subject of writing is that prose isn’t historically my primary medium. It’s poetry. Though only my eyes have seen 90% of them, I’ve written hundreds of poems starting in middle school on through to the present. What I love about poetry is that it allows you to speak in metaphoric code and experiment with wordplay and line breaks. I’ve always found it fascinating.

I don’t always feel I have a choice in the matter. I tend to write it against my will, often in this situation, lying awake in the middle of the night. My body will be tired but the gears will be turning in my mind, spitting out lines. A lot of the time I’ll let it play out without forcing myself to get up and write it down. This is a good one, no way I’ll forget it. I’ll just write it down in the morning, I’ll tell myself. Then it dissolves into sleep, gone forever. It’s wild to think how many fetal poems I’ve birthed in my mind and miscarried. No one will ever know them, not even me.

This time, inspired by Rewind and Cous Cous’ dinner conversation, I make myself write it. I feel around for my phone next to my sleeping pad, find it, and open the notes app. Without thinking, I type:

We leave our clothes at the city limits where the moss grows in a pavement crack vines cover the concrete jungle and ferns curtain the path

We strip to ourselves and enter at the wilderness boundary that isn’t one at all because it frees all the wild things

We’re all skin, bone, naked to society vulnerable to nothing unclothed and unhinged

Laughter in the woods is a wildfire spreading warmth, burning down everything we hold

I put my phone back down beside me and snuggle into my quilt, pleased with myself. There we go, I’m writing on trail too! I close my eyes in sleepy satisfaction and drift away.
