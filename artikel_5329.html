Money habits that show how LeBron James guards small sums and spends big

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

LeBron James skips data roaming and paid apps, yet his $1.4 billion fortune rests on investing in his body and his businesses.

Most everyday money advice tells people to cut the small charges they pay again and again, such as roaming fees, app subscriptions and premium tiers of services they already use. A person who pays many of them each month can spend a large sum over a year without noticing. That advice is written for households with thin margins, where a few dollars can decide whether a bill gets paid, so it is fair to ask whether it means anything for someone who will never have to worry about money. LeBron James, one of the richest athletes alive, is the sharpest test of that question, because he keeps refusing exactly these charges. He could pay all of them without noticing and still declines. That raises a real question about his fortune: Did the habit help make him rich, or is it simply something he does while being rich?

James has never kept his thrift a secret. He appeared in the 2015 film "Trainwreck," which played his cheapness for laughs, according to CNBC. He chose to make the trait part of his public image, and that makes it read more as a statement of who he is than as a private budgeting system. That matters for how to judge it. A trait someone displays in public can be entirely sincere and still explain very little about where the money came from.

Yahoo Sports set out to show how James's frugal habits fit his status as a billionaire, from listening to Pandora with the commercials to skipping data roaming on trips to Toronto. Those habits are real, and he has kept them for years, so they deserve to be taken seriously as a principle he lives by. The harder question is whether a principle about a few dollars has anything to do with a fortune counted in billions. Answering it means weighing the people who credit his thrift for his wealth against the evidence of where James actually spent his money.

James's teammates noticed his thrift in a daily refusal to pay for something most travelers buy without a second thought. On trips into Canada, he kept his phone off cellular data until he reached Wi-Fi at the team hotel or the arena. He chose to stay offline in a foreign city rather than pay extra to use his phone there. His teammates saw the routine most often in Toronto. Kevin Love, James's former Cavaliers teammate, says he watched it happen there more times than he could count. That repetition turns a skipped charge into a rule. A traveler who forgets to switch on roaming once is careless, but a man who waits for Wi-Fi on every trip has made a decision.

The money at stake was trivial, a roaming fee that former NBA player Tristan Thompson estimated at $40, according to Yahoo Sports. To his teammates, the small size of the charge was the whole point. Love links the habit to an old piece of financial wisdom: losses left unchecked can ruin even the largest fortune, however small each one is. In that view, cutting off minor costs is simply what a careful rich person does. Thompson went further and tied the habit directly to James's wealth. "I guess that's what makes him a billionaire," Thompson said.

That is a claim about cause. It comes from people who watched James on the road but never saw his accounts.

The Toronto routine shows how James treats a small charge. Whether that habit explains his fortune is still an open question.

LeBron James has claimed his thrift as his own policy, and that puts it on firmer ground than stories told by his teammates. He confirmed the habit himself in a 2017 ESPN interview, Yahoo Sports reports, and he named a short list of small charges he refuses to pay. "No, I'm not doing that. I'm not turning on data roaming, I'm not buying no apps, I still got Pandora with commercials," James said. He could pay for any premium tier and chose the version with ads instead. The refusal is a choice, not a limit on what he can afford.

The rule outlasted the interview by years and carried over to a different platform. In March 2023, James publicly refused to pay for Twitter $TWTR Blue, according to CNBC, and he said he expected to lose his blue check mark because he would not pay. Tristan Thompson, a former teammate from James's years in Cleveland, says James also sticks with the free version of Spotify $SPOT. James keeps the rule from music to social media, even when it costs him a public mark of status. That makes it a principle he holds, not a running joke.

The sums James turns down are too small to matter to someone with his income. His 2023 tweet put the Twitter Blue fee at $5, though the service cost $8 a month at the time, according to CNBC. Next to his Lakers pay of $52,627,153 for the 2025-26 season, either amount is far too small to change his finances in any way. The refusal saves him no money that counts. Its value is symbolic: it shows he will not pay a few dollars he considers wasted.

If the habit cannot explain the fortune, the answer lies in where James has been willing to spend heavily.

James refuses small charges, but he spends freely wherever the money can come back to him. His teammates credit the refused fees for his wealth, yet his fortune, an estimated $1.4 billion according to Forbes figures reported by Yahoo Sports, rests on heavy investment in his body and his businesses. One early deal shows how that kind of investment can pay off. James reportedly took home around $30 million from his share of Beats by Dre when Apple $AAPL bought the company in 2014, according to TIME. That is a different discipline from thrift. It is a rule about where his money goes rather than a rule against spending it.

His spending on his own body, reportedly more than $1 million on health and fitness, looks like a star's indulgence, but it serves the body that earns his salary. James has stretched his career into a 23rd NBA season in 2025-26, according to TIME, and he collects another salary for every year he stays on the court. When James pays for his own upkeep, he is protecting the income his playing career still brings in.

James has made the same choice again and again, taking a share of a business over a one-time payment. With Beats, he took a small equity stake instead of a fee, according to TIME. In 2021 he gave up his small piece of Liverpool in return for a wider stake in Fenway Sports Group, the company that owns the club along with the Red Sox and the Penguins. His holdings also include SpringHill. Set against that pattern, the refused data fees and app upgrades are a real and lasting principle, but they are not where the billions came from.

He guards a few dollars out of conviction, but he got rich by taking a piece of the business.
