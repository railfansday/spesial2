Making six figures? You might still be using buy now, pay later, according to new research

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

We are a nation united in our desire to pay tomorrow for what we’re buying today.

In the past decade, various deferred payment options have grown in popularity and new data from Block might buck the notion of who the “typical” customer is. Once favored by younger shoppers and those consumers who had limited access to credit, households earning more than $100,000 per year are actually more likely to opt for some of these deferred payment options now, according to the Oakland, California-based fintech company.

Buy now, pay later (BNPL) or checkout financing is the most popular among the 66% of American adults between the ages of 18 and 59 surveyed by Block who have used a deferred-payment option in the past year. And honing in on those households earning more than $100,000 per year, 69% had used BNPL or checkout financing in the past 12 months compared with 65% of households earning between $50,000 and $100,000 and 61% of those earning less than $50,000, the study found.

But it’s not just BNPL that’s increasingly popular among the highest earning Americans. Block’s study found that four of seven different deferred-payment options were more commonly used by the highest earners, including the option to split a purchase into payments after making it or using an installment plan from a store, provider or lender.

Though Block has a dog in the race—it owns Afterpay and Cash App, both of which offer deferred-payment options—the company contends that their growing popularity isn’t necessarily a sign that people are spending beyond their means. Rather, the company found that 72% of users financed at least one expense in the past year that they technically had the money for, but paying upfront would have felt like a strain, while 47% opted to spread out payments to leave money set aside for savings, investments or other priorities.

The report shows how people weigh the tradeoffs of the cost of financing versus keeping cash in their accounts. 

“People don’t look at their checking balance and think of it all as money they ⁠can spend,” Juan Hernandez, head of credit and underwriting at Block told Reuters. “​Some of it is already set aside ​for rent, some is for the next bill, and some they want to keep in case something ​goes wrong.”

FINANCING SHOWS STRAIN

Even so, some of the Block data underscores the strain that many Americans are feeling amid the current affordability crisis that’s seen people struggle to pay for everyday expenses. That’s important as consumer spending accounts for roughly two-thirds of gross domestic product and while Americans have largely weathered the current affordability crisis, one measure of consumer confidence recently fell to the lowest level since 2014. 

The Block study found that people are using deferred-payment methods to finance everyday purchases, like groceries, filling up their tank at the gas station or to pay their utility bills. To cover groceries and other bills, cash advances, earned wage access and/or short-term loans accounted for 58% of these financed transactions. 

And even though a vast majority of consumers—82% of those financing basics and 73% who are financing bills—reported repayment horizons for these expenses within six weeks, that still shows that many people are struggling to keep pace with their day-to-day expenses.

Such data underscores the findings of a recent survey by Pew Research Center. Among American adults surveyed in August, 27% said they can’t pay some of their monthly bills—or can only afford to make partial payments.

The so-called blue tsunami that saw Democrats take control of both the House and Senate in the midterm elections also gave these politicians a mandate to address affordability. And Hakeem Jeffries, the current Speaker-Designate of the House, indicated last week that will be among the priorities for the new Congress come January.

“We’re going to relentlessly fight for an affordable America,” Jeffries said in a press conference. “We’re going to work hard to drive down the high cost of groceries and gas and housing.”
