Russell Crowe’s Classic Thriller Is Still His Greatest Movie 27 Years Later

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Between 1997 and 1999, Russell Crowe became an international superstar. His measured intensity and knack for disappearing into a role put him on the radar of critics, especially when played several uber-slick, violent characters in Virtuosity, The Quick and the Dead, and LA Confidential. Then, shortly before he nailed the role of a sword-and-sandals picture action hero in perennial dad-cinema favorite Gladiator, Crowe truly disappeared into his greatest performance: Big tobacco whistleblower Jeffrey Wigand. In Michael Mann's obsessively detailed true corruption story The Insider, Crowe showed a new side of himself in a character completely at the mercy of the powers that be. His portrayal of Wigand is schlubby, pasty, and paranoid — everything his prior high-profile roles were not.

Crowe, Mann, and Al Pacino as CBS producer Lowell Bergman made a piece about journalism and corporate crime that thrums with the urgency of the very best thrillers. Mann has always had a gift for bringing stories about processes and men who dedicate themselves obsessively to their professions to vivid life (see Heat and Manhunter), and he proved the ideal director to deliver the true story of Wigand and Bergman vs. CBS and big tobacco. The film also earned Crowe his first Oscar nomination, and he remains the beating heart of The Insider.

The Insider is based (and, to be sure, it has been dramatized) on a 1996 Vanity Fair article about the tribulations of Dr. Jeffrey Wigand when he blew the whistle on cigarette company Brown & Williamson's practices and brought his story to CBS News' 60 Minutes. Over the course of two and a half hours, Mann depicts the personal toll that financial and physical threats took on Wigand, and the battle Bergman waged against the higher-ups at CBS to get the whole story on the air.

And in 2026, with widespread public fury over the fusion of corporate and political interests, as well as leaked reports of battles within CBS over what airs or does not air on today's 60 Minutes, the film hasn't lost a drop of its immediacy. That doesn't just come from the subject matter, though. Mann delivers on his trademark enthralling filmmaking and perfect blocking while stacking his cast with some of our finest character actors — now including Crowe. We're treated to the inimitable Michael Gambon as the demonic Brown & Williamson CEO in an early scene threatening Wigand's family in the most genial, corporate way possible. Christopher Plummer and Phillip Baker Hall also deliver as legendary CBS journalist Mike Wallace and producer Don Hewitt, respectively.

After a couple of years as a slick — albeit often violent — leading man, Crowe showed a genuine willingness to disappear into a character facing circumstances beyond his control. Although Crowe is a handsome man, his Wigand is almost aggressively plain; the cinematic picture of a pencil-pusher. And as his righteous fury at the company where he used to work rises, Mann makes sure you can see every twitch, blink, and bit of tension in his bespectacled face. It's the mirror image of Crowe's almost entirely physical performance as Maximus in Gladiator.

Crowe met the real Jeffrey Wigand three times in order to absorb as much as he could about his story without becoming "imitative," Mann said in a later interview. "The trap both Russell and I wanted to avoid was falling into a Xerox of the actual guy," he said. "What results therefore is Russell’s evocation of Wigand, the essential, authentic Wigand that’s based on his origins and what he’s confronting. The beauty of Wigand is his awkwardness. He was definitely a hero, warts and all. He’s a scientist who went to work for a tobacco company—for the money. That’s what makes his obstinacy so heroic."

Today, The Insider is as enthralling as the day it was released to unremarkable box office but unanimous critical acclaim. Mann has never made a boring film, no matter his subject, and he gives the story of a morally-conflicted corporate scientist and an aggressive TV producer the same kind of energy he brought with crime masterpieces like Miami Vice and Public Enemies.
