Monday briefing: What could Andy Burnham’s reset mean for the UK?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

In today’s newsletter: The prime minister has reopened the debate over a future relationship with a changed EU – as the bloc faces the rise of the far right, a protectionist economic agenda and growing defence ambitions

Good morning. Nobody really wants to think about Brexit any more, save for the original Brexiters, who seem delighted that Andy Burnham’s “reset” with the EU has given them an excuse to get the old gang back together. But arguments about whether the UK should rejoin the EU will reignite in parliament this week, as Burnham briefs MPs on the deal he agreed on Friday.

That deal wasn’t so much a victory as the culmination of Keir Starmer’s incrementalist approach to UK-EU relations. Its core agreements haven’t been ratified by member states yet, and the EU won’t sign them off until Britain resolves a dispute over tuition fees for European students. Burnham is apparently “determined” to take the UK back into the EU, but don’t expect any progress on that before the next general election.

Putting aside the question of whether a second referendum would merely reopen old wounds, Europe has changed dramatically since 2016, creating new challenges for any government seeking a closer relationship with the EU. For this morning’s newsletter, I discussed these challenges and what they mean for rejoining with Mujtaba Rahman, managing director for Europe at the Eurasia Group and a frequent contributor to the Guardian’s Opinion section. That’s after the headlines.

Three separate deals were announced at Friday’s summit in Brussels: one on food and drink, another on emissions trading, and a third on youth mobility, which will allow British students to work, live, study and travel in the EU and give reciprocal access to EU students in Britain (for a full recap, read Jennifer Rankin and Alexandra Topping’s explainer). Burnham wants to go further, and both he and his foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, have used the near-identical phrase that all options are “on the table” for Britain’s future relationship with the EU.

You’d be forgiven for feeling weary about revisiting the last decade in British politics. As one Labour MP recently told the Financial Times (£), “nobody is asking for this.” Burnham seems to be testing the ground ahead of a general election, hoping that a campaign to rejoin, or at least move closer to, the EU could plausibly win over progressive defectors.

Still, Friday’s agreement was a reminder of how difficult it is to negotiate with a club of 27. Member states, including France and Italy, issued a statement making it clear that the deal wouldn’t be implemented unless Burnham offered a deal on tuition fees for EU students at British universities. “There’s a fairly strong sense that we’ve reached the end of the incremental style of negotiating,” Mujtaba Rahman tells me. “There isn’t much more for the UK to do before it starts to resemble a cherrypicking state.” And there are other developments that will be on the minds of negotiators and ministers contemplating the UK’s future relationship with Europe.

Has the far right made the idea of European Union membership less attractive?

This is one of the major developments: Europe has become decidedly more right-wing since 2016. Though the European far right lost one of its key figureheads when Viktor Orbán was defeated in the Hungarian elections earlier this year, far-right parties govern in the Czech Republic and Italy, and support for them is rising in Austria and Germany. The proportion of voters in Europe who cast their ballots for far-right parties has grown more than five times since the 1990s, and has increased particularly sharply over the last three years.

Whether this will make rejoining less appealing depends largely on what happens next year in the French elections. Marine Le Pen, who is now favoured to win, wants to slash France’s contribution to the EU budget and drastically downsize the union. It’s possible that a Le Pen presidency could embolden other EU member states to seek closer ties with a pro-European British government, but it’s also possible that Le Pen’s domestic policies, such as reducing the retirement age to 60, could trigger a domestic economic crisis and inflame a conflict between the EU and France, one of its founding member states. “If Marine Le Pen wins, and France tries to enforce a domestic programme that runs in opposition to the EU, then I think a lot of people in the UK will look at the resulting crisis and think, ‘Thank God we’re out of that’,” Mujtaba says.

Is the EU’s new protectionism a reason to rejoin?

The EU’s Made in Europe strategy would have been almost unimaginable a decade ago, when progressives criticised the union for enforcing free-market globalisation on its members and forbidding state aid. The strategy, which uses public money to support goods made by companies in the single market, is a sign of both how isolated the UK has become and how much the world has changed. Burnham is now trying to make “reindustrialisation” part of his agenda at home, but the EU’s initiative is designed to disadvantage countries outside the EU. It risks scuppering his efforts by deindustrialising the UK car industry, which relies on European buyers.

“It’s very hard for the government to influence this because the Made in Europe negotiations are internal to the EU. And it’s very hard for member states to provide any guarantees because there’s no UK guardian at the table,” Mujtaba says. The EU hasn’t included the UK in the scheme, though Friday’s summit promised closer cooperation on steel and electric vehicle supply chains. But that may require the UK to follow EU policies without having any ability to shape them, making the idea of full membership much more appealing.

Would rejoining mean taking a different approach to defence?

This is perhaps the strongest reason of all for rejoining, but it’s also another area where the EU’s approach has evolved significantly over the last decade. “The geopolitical environment is forcing the EU to do a bunch of things the UK has historically taken issue with,” Mujtaba says. “It’s intervening in areas of policy that would have traditionally been the purview of nation states.” In particular, the EU’s Security Action for Europe facility (Safe), a new EU defence fund offering up to €150bn in loans to member states to fund defence investments, shifts defence from a national concern to one of wider European governance.

Britain refused to pay the financial contribution that Brussels wanted for it to participate in the Safe fund, though there are signs that the two sides may still reach a deal. But any campaign to rejoin would need to critically evaluate the deep ties and so-called “special relationship” between the UK and US in light of an EU that has grown to regard the US as an unreliable partner on security.

“The UK isn’t quite as far-reaching in its conclusions about the US,” Mujtaba says. “You don’t hear UK officials talking about the Americans in the same way that European officials are talking about the Americans.”

A bit of good news to remind you that the world’s not all bad

David Parr’s days were spent decorating wealthy homes with William Morris’s much-loved textile designs. But by candlelight at night, Parr, a Victorian working-class labourer, would turn to the interiors of his own home, decorating his walls with his own beautiful designs, work that went largely unnoticed during his life.

After his death in 1927, those extraordinary walls in his home on Gwydir Street in Cambridge were thankfully preserved by his granddaughter, Elsie. Today, The David Parr House, as it is now known, is set to be honoured with a blue plaque, which is thought to be the first ever dedicated to a working-class Victorian art-workman.
