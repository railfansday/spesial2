A Rocket Failure Just Cost Amazon 40 Internet Satellites. It's Good News for SpaceX, but I'd Still Want a Lower Stock Price.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

How much can a rival's failed launch really help a company worth around $1.85 trillion?

Amazon (AMZN +0.05%) just lost a full rocket's worth of internet satellites. On Sunday, Nov. 15, a Vulcan rocket from United Launch Alliance (ULA) carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites hit trouble around 10 minutes after liftoff and never reached orbit.

It's a big loss for a network that had launched almost 400 satellites on 14 missions before Sunday. Its license requires 3,232.

And I think it's good news for SpaceX (SPCX +3.16%), which runs Starlink, the network Leo was built to take on. Starlink already has over 11,100 satellites in orbit. Leo is arguably its best-funded rival, and that rival just slipped further behind.

But how much does that help the stock? SpaceX shares trade near $136 as I write, which values the company at about $1.85 trillion.

Leo was relying on Vulcan more than any other rocket. Sunday's flight was the first of 38 Vulcan launches Amazon has bought, and Amazon has said the missions would carry over 1,500 first-generation satellites, almost half the constellation.

ULA hasn't given a cause yet (the problem came when its Centaur upper stage was firing), and the rocket might be grounded for a while as engineers investigate.

Leo's other big booking has the same issue. Amazon holds 24 firm launches on Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket, which hasn't flown since a blast on its launch pad in May.

That mainly leaves Europe's Ariane 6, due to carry 36 more Leo satellites no earlier than Nov. 23, and SpaceX itself. Amazon bought three launches on SpaceX's Falcon 9 rocket in December 2023 and used them in 2025. It then added 10 more in late 2025.

Still, the rate was already slowing before Sunday. Leo sent 155 satellites into orbit in the second quarter, yet just 29 in the third quarter, and none have reached orbit so far in the fourth. In the last 12 months, it added around 240 satellites, or about 20 a month. Its Federal Communications Commission (FCC) license gives it until July 30, 2029, to have all 3,232 in orbit and operating. To do that, Leo would need to average almost 90 a month from here, over four times its recent pace.

Leo has also missed its own goals before. In January, Amazon told the FCC it expected around 700 satellites in orbit by July 30, 2026. It hit about 400. The FCC had already waived the normal penalties for missing the halfway deadline, with conditions, in June.

Starlink, meanwhile, keeps adding satellites faster than Leo can launch them. SpaceX's quarterly filings showed over 10,200 Starlink satellites on Aug. 4 and more than 11,100 on Oct. 22. In under three months, Starlink added more satellites than Leo has launched so far.

After all, Starlink is where SpaceX makes its money. The connectivity segment earned around $2.4 billion in third-quarter operating income, most of the $2.8 billion its three segments made in all.

Amazon could depend more on SpaceX's rockets while Vulcan and New Glenn are idle. But this likely wouldn't move SpaceX's numbers much. Its launch-services business pulled in around $1.1 billion in the third quarter, below 8% of total revenue, and the space segment had a $7 million operating loss.

Put simply, the bigger win is the added time Starlink gets, not more rocket sales.

The catch is that the stock already pays for a lot of that. At around $1.85 trillion, SpaceX's valuation is about 32 times sales, using its annualized third-quarter revenue.

At that price, investors are already betting on Starlink staying well ahead, and on SpaceX's artificial intelligence (AI) spending paying off. Leo's problems don't help with the second part.

In the end, Sunday's failure is good news for SpaceX's business. It gives Starlink more room in a market where its best-funded challenger was already running late. But a rival's stumble doesn't make the stock any cheaper, and I'd still want a lower price before buying.
