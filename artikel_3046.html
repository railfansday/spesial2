Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Lucid's EsoGuard Medicare win comes alongside a senior note covenant miss.

Need a quote from a Motley Fool analyst? Email [email protected]

PAVmed Inc. (PAVM +11.31%) reported that its primary subsidiary, Lucid Diagnostics, secured nationwide Medicare reimbursement for its EsoGuard test, a development management described as a transformational milestone. The company is currently focused on four key technology platforms: Lucid's esophageal diagnostics, Veris Health's digital oncology platform, PortIO vascular access, and Octeris imaging. Management indicated that the company is leveraging its engagement with the Ohio State University (OSU) to optimize workflow and adoption for the Veris platform ahead of a planned FDA submission for its implantable monitor in early 2027. Despite a GAAP net loss influenced by non-cash mark-to-market adjustments of its Lucid holdings, PAVmed maintained a non-GAAP adjusted loss of $1.2 million for the quarter while advancing regulatory pathways for its earlier-stage medical devices.

Lishan Aklog: Yes. Look, we can get a little bit in the weeds on that for those who are students of 510(k) versus De Novo. Just as a reminder, the 510(k) pathway is the lowest hurdle for a medical device to go through FDA approval -- FDA clearance, and it just requires a substantial equivalence to an existing predicate device. The next stage is the De Novo, which is -- has a higher -- it is between the 510(k) and the PMA and has somewhat higher requirements with regard to the clinical study design, the size and so forth.

When PortIO was actively being pursued before we paused the project several years ago, our engagements with FDA had led to a determination at that point that this was likely -- that this needed to go down the De Novo pathway. That's how we were proceeding before we paused the project. What's happened since that engagement with the FDA are 2 things. One, we had our first-in-human study, a successful human study in Colombia, South America that demonstrated the safety of the device in humans. That wasn't available at the time of the original engagement with the FDA.

Also, on further analysis with our team as well as with external regulatory consultants, some of whom were actually -- were aware of the previous application, it's become clear to us that the major consideration with regard to moving this down the De Novo pathway had to do with the predicate device was used for urgent and emergency situations and PortIO is not really intended for that. The purpose of PortIO is to be a long-term vascular access device comparable to existing ports and existing long-term catheters.

We have a possibility, and I think a reasonable possibility that we -- this time around, we can proceed on a 510(k) basis by simply saying that we're not -- that the intended use does not include emergency and urgent insertion, which is consistent with sort of the commercial strategy anyway. We had an initial call with the FDA, and we have a formal pre-sub meeting in January, and that will determine -- that will give us a strong sense as to whether each direction. Both of them will be fine. We're confident based on the previous data and based on the previous conversations that we have a clear path to completing a study and getting this cleared.

But obviously, our strong preference would be to be able to do so through the 510(k) pathway, which would be shorter and less capital -- less capital requirements and potentially might not even need additional human data, but that's still to be determined.

Jeremy Pearlman: Okay. Great. And then just lastly, moving to Octeris. Maybe help investors understand what is it -- what type of -- what problem is it trying to solve? Lishan Aklog: Yes. Excellent. That's the paradigm under which these patients are being managed. You can consider this basically the Octeris to fit in, in the post- EsoGuard step. EsoGuard identifies these patients with precancer, they go for an endoscopy. Then at the time of endoscopy, what are the various things that can be done to enhance that.

The direct answer to your question is that Octeris does compete with some existing technologies. Most of them are molecular tests that seek to risk stratify patients or to determine at the time of confirmatory endoscopy, whether the patient needs more aggressive surveillance, whether there's a likelihood that they have the later-stage precancer and maybe should be directed more towards an urgent -- more immediate treatment with ablation. There's a variety of technologies that are out there and molecular tests that are out there that seek to be supplements to the confirmatory endoscopy.

Octeris has the opportunity to make essentially most of those obsolete because what it does, instead of using a molecular test, it uses imaging, a combination of optical coherence tomography as well as an additional more proprietary optical method to assess the areas of abnormality at the time of endoscopy. So we can look at those cells and it's able to measure the size of the nucleus, which correlates very closely with the likelihood that they have late-stage precancer, which would need to be treated more directly.

That's a bit in the weeds, but the bottom line is that when Octeris is finalized and commercialized, it will provide the opportunity to take a patient who has suspected precancer, suspected Barrett's esophagus, hopefully, from having gotten an EsoGuard test and during the endoscopy to determine whether that patient has a late-stage precancer and whether those areas should be biopsied more aggressively. Then if it does come back as dysplasia the later-stage precancer, whether that patient should go straight to the definitive treatment. Over the long-term, later versions of this device, the goal would be to have it be a onetime procedure.

You go in for your confirmatory endoscopy and the endoscopist assesses the area and using the supplemental imaging from Octeris, they can decide to ablate and destroy those abnormal cells on the spot, eliminating the need for multiple other interventional procedures. That's the paradigm that we're operating within. If it is successful, it really will shake up the way these patients are managed after they are identified as having precancer.
