Quentin Tarantino's Near-Perfect New Netflix Thriller Could Be the Next 'Knives Out'

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The uncanny mix of Quentin Tarantino and David Fincher's unique styles combines to create something new, imperfect, and extremely exciting

Legendary filmmaker Stanley Kubrick died unexpectedly in his sleep in March 1999 at the age of 70, leaving behind a handful of unfinished projects that would never see the light of day. One of them, however, was eventually completed after Kubrick's close friend Steven Spielberg stepped in to direct A.I. Artificial Intelligence. The result is an oddly hypnotic melding of the two directors' distinct styles, making it one of the most unique films ever released. Artificial Intelligence isn't a perfect movie (it's nowhere near the top of either filmmaker's greatest hits), but there's something fascinating about seeing how their sensibilities overlap to create something totally new.

The story behind The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth isn't quite as dramatic, but the ultimate effect is similar. Nobody died. Quentin Tarantino just made the bizarre decision that his 10th film should be his last and that a sequel to Once Upon a Time in Hollywood wasn't going to cut it. So instead of trashing the film like he did with his last failed effort, Tarantino handed the script off to David Fincher. The result is another unusual melding of directorial styles that doesn't always work (it's definitely not the best either Fincher or Tarantino has to offer), but still manages to provide a uniquely fun time at the movies — and also on Netflix later this year.

The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth picks up eight years after Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood. Rick Dalton (Leonardo DiCaprio) is off in Australia starring in a popular new series, while his former stuntman Cliff (Brad Pitt) works a series of odd jobs in Los Angeles. One night he's serving drinks at a tiki bar with a mud wrestling ring​​​​​​​; another he manages the New Beverly Cinema (a one-screen theater owned by Tarantino himself IRL).

But Cliff's real profession has seemingly become professional fixer to the stars. In the film's opening scene, he helps retrieve a stolen Oscar for a director he's friends with, casually murdering one of the criminals and sending the other one back home crying to his mommy on a Greyhound bus. Throughout it all, Pitt remains cool and collected. He might just be the smoothest character Tarantino ever came up with, and that's saying a lot when the competition includes the Bible-quoting hitman Jules Winnfield (Samuel L. Jackson), the unflappable flight attendant Jackie Brown (Pam Grier), and pretty much every role played by Michael Madsen.

After that quick, scene-setting introduction, Tarantino's script gets to the meat of the story. Cliff receives an invitation to meet with TV star Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), which is odd because Charlie Peters famously does his own stunts. Turns out, the actor is in a serious jam with some Filipino gangsters who are threatening to murder his wife (Carla Gugino). Cliff reluctantly agrees to help, mostly because he's developed a crush on Charlie's beautiful assistant, Karen Crosby (Elizabeth Debicki). Cliff Booth unfolds somewhat predictably from there. The gangsters pursue Charlie, Cliff pursues both the gangsters and Karen, and a series of misadventures and sidequests rise up along the way.

Tarantino also fills his script with the kind of meta-culture commentary we've come to expect, this time aimed squarely at the movie industry itself. The story overflows with films within films and made-up anecdotes from Hollywood history. At one point, the four main characters sit at a table and debate whether cinema even qualifies as an art form​​​​​​​, with Debicki delivering a scalding takedown​​​​​​​ of the entire industry that compares the choice between Star Wars and The Godfather to a Big Mac or a Whopper. Tarantino is playing the hits here, and I gleefully ate it up.

So what does Fincher bring to this unexpected fusion of styles? That's a tougher question to answer. Cliff Booth swaps the colorful comic-book pop of Tarantino's previous work for the same subtle gray filter that defines everything from Seven to The Social Network. Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross also continue their collaboration with Fincher, and their moody, noir-ish score further emphasizes that washed-out aesthetic​​​​​​​. The soundtrack is also full of '70s pop music, but if you're expecting a collection of Tarantino-level needledrops, think again​​​​​​​. Whoever picked the songs for this movie seriously phoned it in, with the most ambitious choice somehow being the AC/DC album title track "Dirty Deeds Done Dirt Cheap" playing over a demolition derby sequence. Another scene is punctuated with "It's a Shame" by The Spinners. It's almost as if someone typed in "1970s cool songs" on Spotify and let the algorithm do the work for them.

Gripes about the music aside, the world of 1970s Los Angeles is stunningly realized. The cars, the clothing, the street signs. It's all beautiful to behold, and Fincher's camera luxuriates in that aesthetic even more than Tarantino. Heck, even the vintage cans of beer and soda that Cliff guzzles down are fun to look at. At 2 hours and 20 minutes, Cliff Booth is by no means a breezy movie, but I could have spent even longer soaking in the world that Fincher and his team bring to life.

Cliff Booth doesn't exactly set up a sequel (the film's abrupt ending is arguably open to interpretation thanks to a bizarre mid-credits tag), but it's easy to imagine a world where we get to keep hanging out with Cliff for even longer. Like Benoit Blanc returning to solve each new Knives Out mystery, Brad Pitt's fixer could easily resurface a few years from now for another misadventure, ideally with Tarantino still writing the script. Fincher could return, too, but I'd be just as happy with another director taking the helm. What would a mashup between Tarantino and Richard Linklater or Greta Gerwig look like? I'm dying to find out. (Those are both huge longshots, but you get the idea.)

Even if we never see Cliff Booth again, I'm glad we have this weird little movie. It might not be perfect, but getting to spend a couple more hours in the world Tarantino created​​​​​​​ feels like a gift — especially when the director seems so hesitant to actually deliver his 10th and final film.

The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth will play in IMAX theaters only starting on Nov. 25, and releases on Netflix a month later on Dec. 23.
