Gay People (Mostly) Explain Gay Halloween

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

When you see a post that begins with “I hate gay Halloween, what do you mean you’re dressed as…” you know you’re in for a good laugh, or perhaps the feeling that you’re completely out of the loop. Trick-or-treating isn’t until this weekend, but in the age of social media the day itself has taken the back seat—it’s online where the battle for the best costumes takes place, and it’s happening earlier every year.

The trend began taking shape in the late 2010s and gained strength during the pandemic: celebrities—most notably the Kardashian-Jenner clan—began re-creating memorable moments from pop culture, mostly old film posters or iconic music album covers, and staging elaborate costume shoots for their Instagrams. The closer the re-creation would get to the original the better—and worthier of likes.

The idea of gay Halloween exists somewhere in this realm, though it’s inevitably more lo-fi, and the most successful looks aim for laughs rather than mere online clout. Plainly, it’s when people—mostly gay men—pick a niche pop culture moment or meme and re-create it in a costume. Forget witches and Scream masks. In 2004, Mean Girls declared, “In girl world, Halloween is the one night of the year when a girl can dress like a total slut and no other girls can say anything about it.” The 2026 equivalent is less about horny devils. Halloween is now the one night of the year when you can let out your inner freak or geek: Now you can be the breakup Post-it from a 2003 episode of Sex and the City, or Marc Jacobs when he wore a neck brace. Two years ago, a friend dressed as Antonio, a character played by Ariana Grande on Saturday Night Live. Another at the same party went as Rihanna as a nun for an Interview magazine cover.

Owen Thiele and Benito Skinner dressed as PinkPantheress and Zara Larsson for Halloween.

Once upon a time, Halloween was about being as frightening as possible. Sure, there is something indeed terrifying about being broken up with via Post-it, and some less-online people are still attached to the classics—a zombie, a devil, et cetera—but Halloween has been co-opted by the internet, and by celebrity culture, in a way that has made it entirely self-indulgent.

Consider Heidi Klum’s famous annual Halloween bash, which she started in 2000. Early on, her costumes evolved from classic (a dominatrix the first year, a vampire in 2005) to more complex (a skinless person in 2011) to complete cosplay. In 2015, Klum appeared as Jessica Rabbit; in 2018, as the ogre version of Fiona from Shrek. Last year, she was a Medusa, and her husband, Tom Kaulitz, a hoplite turned into stone. There are Hollywood-level prosthetics, and, largely, it appears as if her costumes are made more for an image than for a party. Klum, in this space, is both the trendsetter and the shining example of the way our collective take on the holiday has evolved to adjust to our online-first lifestyles.

“Someone I just started working with recently said, ‘We live in a reference economy,’” says stylist Ron Hartleben, who works with Camila Cabello and Tyla, among others. “That really struck me,” he says. “Everything has been done. Everyone has access to all the same images now.” So much so that people in the queer community “have gone about seeking the most niche, obscure reference from a show or pop culture moment and making that their Halloween costume” so that a mere mortal, in this case a cis, heterosexual person, may not understand it. “Gay Halloween is exploiting that obscure moment in pop culture and running with it to create one of the most stupid and entertaining outfits imaginable,” Hartleben says. “As long as you think it’s funny and you get it, you’re doing gay Halloween right.” Consider Benito Skinner and Mary Beth Barone’s costume this year as a shining example of gay Halloween: they dressed as Dakota Johnson and Ellen DeGeneres and even recreated their infamous, viral interview about the actor’s birthday party.

But gay Halloween has now broken past its own internet bubble. Celebrities and straight normies alike have adopted the reference-first language popular in queer and adjacent subcultures—from drag to ballroom and beyond—and made it mainstream to come off as more in-the-know.

Last year, Mandy Lee, a trend forecaster known as @oldloserinbrooklyn on TikTok and Instagram, dressed as Jonathan Anderson smoking a cigarette by the Seine, a video she reposted this year next to the original clip of Anderson to much online buzz (hers was taken by the Gowanus Canal in Brooklyn). “It’s a format that works particularly well online versus IRL because you’re able to [have] text onscreen or even throw up reference images to make it click,” Lee says. She categorizes her costumes as “fashion Halloween,” which she says lives under the same umbrella as gay Halloween. She’s dressed as Lynn Yaeger and Miuccia Prada before, and also the “tabi swiper.” The trick is that they are, as she says, “low effort.” They’re costumes that rely on what’s already in Lee’s closet—no production value, just a sense of humor. “It’s just fun to get hyper specific and granular,” Lee says. “[The costumes] are also not for everyone; they are for fashion people, and that’s what makes it fun.”

Earlier this week, stylist and creative director Chris Horan, who works with the likes of Charli xcx, Demi Lovato, and Christina Aguilera, reposted clips he’d been tagged in on Instagram of fans dressed as Adéla, for whom he is both stylist and creative director. “I obviously love when people dress up or reference any of the looks we’ve worked on,” Horan says. “It takes a lot of effort to make any sort of look on our part or [the fans’], so it always makes me happy to see it.”

Last year Horan worked with Lovato on her costume, for which she dressed as Poot Lovato, her fictional twin sister that originated from a Tumblr post in 2015. Poot had long been a gay Halloween staple, but her fans would have never imagined that Lovato herself would bring her back to the forefront in such a way. Horan thinks these costumes also speak to something bigger: “I don’t think you’re really hitting as a pop star if you don’t have an immediately identifiable look.”

The internet has changed the way we do most things, particularly getting dressed, so it’s no surprise that it would affect how we get dressed up. We do things not just for ourselves, or even the people who will see the costume IRL, but for our circles, or “followers,” to congratulate us. Social media may not have ruined Halloween—it’s made it funnier and perhaps less spooky—but it’s altered its spirit.
