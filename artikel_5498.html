The 10 states best positioned for the AI data center boom

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Virginia scores 100 and Tennessee's AI sites use 9.9% of state power in a look at 10 of the 24 states TRG Datacenters ranked.

On the edge of a small town, a local planning board weighs whether a developer can build a campus packed with servers. The vote will shape what nearby households pay for energy, how much pressure falls on local resources, and how many jobs the town gains. Big money rides on decisions of this kind. Research cited by GovTech projects that more than $600 billion will flow into AI infrastructure in 2026, and the spending is heading toward places few observers would have predicted. Established tech hubs make up one part of that picture.

Each new facility hands its host community a two-sided bargain. Jobs and tax revenue arrive, and so does fresh demand on power systems, on watersheds, and on the rate structures behind every household's monthly utility bill. Loads of this size surface in utility commission filings ahead of any headlines. An upbeat press release leaves the tradeoff intact. TRG Datacenters frames the issue around location, arguing that the spot where a facility lands decides what it does to its surroundings. A state's capacity to absorb new sites depends on more than the number it hosts today. A crowded map shows that a state arrived early, and a head start does not settle which state is best prepared.

Gadget Review examined the question through a TRG Datacenters ranking that scored 24 states on AI readiness and set the top performer at 100. The article ties readiness to clean grids, open land, and water security. Its counts cover AI data centers alone. The source does not publish the formula or weighting behind the scores, and it gives no reporting period for the counts or the consumption figures. Three states come with figures for the share of statewide electricity AI operations consume, a gap that limits any comparison of AI's burden across the full group. What follows covers the 10 highest-scoring states among the 24, in descending order.

Virginia hits a score of 100 with seven AI data centers, a count that trails Texas by six facilities. No state but Texas runs a larger AI fleet in this group of 10, and each of the remaining eight operates five or fewer. Virginia's seven sites match the combined count of Georgia, South Carolina, Mississippi, New York, and Oregon. Virginia accounts for 18.4% of the 38 AI data centers across these 10 states. Texas holds close to twice Virginia's total yet scores 19.2 points lower. Facility volume cannot explain the top mark.

Ohio, the runner-up, sits 11.8 points behind Virginia with five AI facilities. No other pair of consecutive entries on the list shows a gap that wide. Ohio, in turn, clears Mississippi in seventh by 11.5 points. The fall from first to second outstrips the full spread across the next six states. Virginia's lead over Ohio makes up 43% of the 27.6-point distance between the first and 10th states in the selection.

Loudoun County, Virginia, goes by the nickname data center alley, and it earns the label. CNBC reports that the county holds the largest concentration of data centers anywhere in the world.

The Energy Information Administration finds that the Dominion $D zone, the section of the PJM grid that includes Virginia, serves more data centers in one location than anywhere else in the world. The agency attributes most of that concentration to fiber optic connections, available land, and power infrastructure. Access to a compact fiber backbone and four undersea fiber cables helped lift Virginia into a leading position for data centers, according to a separate Energy Information Administration analysis.

Open land appears both in the agency's explanation and among the conditions this ranking rewards. Any state chasing Virginia's standing will need assets of that kind, since the Texas result shows that piling on facilities falls short.

Ohio carries an 88.2 readiness score on five AI data centers, a mark no state besides Virginia exceeds. Texas hosts more than twice as many facilities yet trails by 7.4 points. The gap suggests Ohio's appeal lies in its ability to absorb new campuses, a quality its modest current footprint does not reveal.

Ohio beats New York, a coastal tech hub, by 11.6 points, 88.2 against 76.6. Indiana, the other Midwestern entry in this group, sits 15.8 points lower at 72.4. Geography alone cannot account for Ohio's position, because the Midwest produces both the second-highest and the lowest marks among these 10 states. Ohio's edge must come from conditions Indiana does not match.

AEP Ohio has written a safeguard into its rules for data center customers. A campus that cancels its project or misses its contract terms owes the utility an exit fee. The provision keeps the cost of an abandoned commitment with the developer. It speaks to the rate-structure worry that follows these projects into every host community.

The U.S. Department of Energy and the Department of Commerce announced a partnership with SoftBank and AEP Ohio to redevelop federal land, upgrade energy infrastructure, and build advanced computing capacity in southern Ohio. The plan targets land and power, two conditions the ranking ties to preparedness. AEP Ohio plays two roles in the state's story, setting terms for data center customers while joining a federal push to expand computing.

Jerome Township has imposed a moratorium on new data centers, citing utility and safety concerns. The pause shows one community drawing a line as developers and federal partners pursue projects elsewhere in Ohio. Alongside the exit fee, the moratorium reveals a state whose appeal comes with guardrails at both the utility and township levels. A high statewide rating offers no guarantee that every town will welcome the next proposal.

Tennessee's AI data centers consume 9.9% of all the electricity the state uses, the steepest share of any state with available figures. Three facilities generate that demand. Split three ways, each would claim about 3.3% of statewide consumption, a heavy burden for a single site. A load of that size leaves no room to treat any one of those campuses as a minor customer.

Tennessee's figure runs 1.8 percentage points above Indiana's, even though both states host three AI data centers. It also exceeds Mississippi's by 2 percentage points, and Mississippi operates one fewer facility. Matching facility counts in Tennessee and Indiana translate into different demands on each state's power supply. The share hinges on how much power each campus pulls and how much the state consumes overall, and a site tally captures neither.

A readiness score of 83.7, third-best in this group of 10, accompanies Tennessee's hefty electricity draw. The state earned that mark with fewer AI sites than either of the two states ahead of it. Its score also beats Indiana's by 11.3 points, despite the identical facility count and Tennessee's larger slice of the grid. Texas operates 10 more AI data centers and still finishes 2.9 points behind. Heavy AI demand and strong preparedness coexist inside one state's borders. A high mark leaves open the question of whether the grid has slack to spare.

An xAI facility stands in an industrial park on the south side of Memphis, near factories and a gas-fired power plant the Tennessee Valley Authority runs. Some community members questioned whether the city's utilities could supply so much power to a customer such as xAI. Earlier ice storms and other extreme weather had pushed the Memphis grid into rolling blackouts, and those episodes fed the doubts. The Memphis debate shows residents asking, at the scale of one city, the question Tennessee's statewide load raises for the whole grid.

Georgia earns an 81.8 readiness mark from a base of two AI data centers, the fourth-highest result in the ranking. It outscored 20 of the 24 states TRG Datacenters evaluated. South Carolina, New York, and Oregon are the lone states in this group with fewer sites, each running one. Georgia sits in the upper half of the table on one of the leanest footprints among the 10.

Every state above Georgia runs more AI data centers than it does, from Virginia's seven to Ohio's five and Tennessee's three. Across the first four rows, scores and site counts descend together. Georgia's two facilities fit that line, and the order holds until the next row.

Texas sits one point behind Georgia while running 13 facilities, six and a half times as many. The biggest buildout among these states could not lift Texas past a rival with a sliver of its hardware. Georgia's standing suggests the traits behind a strong score can take hold before a large wave of construction arrives.

Mississippi matches Georgia's two AI data centers yet finishes 5.1 points lower, at 76.7. Identical counts produced a wide gap between the two Southern states. The number of buildings explains little about where either state lands. Whatever separates the two lies beyond anything a site tally captures.

The source reports no share of statewide electricity for Georgia's AI sites. The strain its two facilities place on the state grid stays unmeasured, unlike the loads documented in Tennessee, Mississippi, and Indiana.

Georgia's 81.8 falls inside a 3.2-point band holding four states, from Tennessee's 83.7 down to South Carolina's 80.5. Georgia trails Tennessee by 1.9 points and stays 1.3 points clear of South Carolina, with Texas wedged between Georgia and South Carolina. Margins this thin mean a modest shift in any one state's conditions could reshuffle the middle of the order.

Texas counts 13 AI data centers, the largest total among these 10 states, yet its score of 80.8 lands it in fifth place. Virginia finishes 19.2 points higher with seven facilities. Ohio, Tennessee, and Georgia each post a stronger result while operating five, three, and two sites. The four states above Texas run 17 AI data centers combined, four more than Texas by itself.

The Texas buildout dwarfs every other state on the list. Its 13 sites make up 34.2% of the 38 AI data centers spread across the 10 states. Virginia and Ohio, the two strongest entries, have 12 between them, one short of the Texas tally. Tennessee, Georgia, South Carolina, Mississippi, New York, Oregon, and Indiana hold 13 facilities among them, matching Texas on its own.

Sheer volume buys Texas a thin margin over states with a fraction of its presence. South Carolina trails by 0.3 point with a single facility. Georgia edges ahead by a full point while running 11 fewer sites. Mississippi sits 4.1 points back with two AI data centers. Texas stands closer to a one-site state than to Virginia, and among the top five, the state with the most facilities carries the weakest rating.

Site counts and outcomes drift apart across the group. Indiana, with three AI data centers, falls below New York and Oregon, which host one apiece. South Carolina, with one site, outscores Mississippi and its two. If site totals drove the order, Texas would hold Virginia's spot and the one-facility states would cluster at the bottom. The table scatters them across sixth, eighth, and ninth.

The source supplies no electricity share for Texas. The load Texas' sites place on the state grid goes unmeasured in these figures. On this evidence, more Texas construction would enlarge its slice of the group's facilities with no sign it would lift the state's standing.

South Carolina registers an 80.5 AI readiness score from one AI data center, the slimmest footprint among the six highest-scoring states. A single site keeps the state in tight company with rivals that run many more.

Texas edges South Carolina by 0.3 points despite running 12 more AI data centers. Georgia, with two sites, finishes 1.3 points higher. Tennessee's 83.7 and South Carolina's 80.5 bracket a four-state band 3.2 points wide, and facility totals inside it swing from one to 13. Within this cluster, extra buildings buy little on the scoreboard.

The distance to the top of the table dwarfs that band. Virginia's perfect 100 on seven sites puts it 19.5 points clear of South Carolina. Ohio's 88.2 on five facilities opens a 7.7-point cushion over the sixth-place state. The climb from sixth into the top two covers more ground than the entire cluster South Carolina occupies.

South Carolina marks the lower edge of the selection's crowded middle. Below it, scores fall away before Mississippi appears, a drop wider than the full band above. The divide leaves South Carolina's lone site on the upper side, beside states with larger footprints.

Indiana divides its 8.1% among three as well, which works out to 2.7% each. Mississippi operates one fewer site than either state, yet each of its facilities carries a larger slice of the grid.

Water security, one of the conditions the ranking rewards, carries heavy stakes in Morrow County, Oregon. Syndicated columnist Ben Jealous, writing in the Chicago Sun-Times, describes families there as living through a crisis, with concerns centered on a nearby Amazon $AMZN data center. He points to a Rolling Stone investigation that links the area to miscarriages among mothers and rare cancers among neighbors, claims Amazon denies. Jealous stops short of naming a proven cause. He argues that communities hosting data centers that consume enormous amounts of water face genuine risks either way.

A 2024 Associated Press photo published with the column shows Mike Gorman, the Morrow County assessor and tax collector, reviewing an Oregon Enterprise Zone Exemption Claim form for Amazon Data Services, Inc., at the county courthouse in Heppner.
