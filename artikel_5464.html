9 tornado basics that look different as the threat moves east and into winter

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Nine basic tornado questions, from Tornado Alley to watches, warnings and EF ratings, and what each familiar answer leaves out.

Ask most Americans to picture a tornado and they will describe a dark funnel over flat farmland in late spring, a danger that belongs to the Great Plains. That picture may be falling out of date. Twisters are decreasing on the Great Plains, the Chicago Sun-Times reports, and tornado activity in the eastern U.S. has risen 12%, according to the U.S. Census Bureau. The calendar has changed along with the map. From 2010 to 2025, tornado counts ran higher from January through April and rose notably in October and December. Tornado season now reaches into the winter months, when few people expect a storm worth watching. A household that prepares only for spring storms over Kansas may be guarding against the wrong place at the wrong time of year.

Getting the picture wrong carries a real cost. Of the U.S. weather disasters that have each cost $1 billion or more since 1980, nearly half were tornadoes, according to the U.S. Census Bureau. The change is in where and when tornadoes strike, not in how many there are. National tornado numbers have held stable or declined since 1995, according to a US Forest Service study. The Chicago Sun-Times reports that Shel Winkley, chief meteorologist at Climate Central, sees no evidence in current research that climate change is producing more tornadoes. The same danger is spread across a different map and a longer calendar. Even the move east is still under study. The Forest Service researchers describe the shift from the Midwest toward the Southeast as a potential one that appears in damage trends, not a confirmed change in where tornadoes form. Readers should hold the new map loosely. The sensible response is not alarm but a fresh look at the basics.

The NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory set out to answer the common, basic questions the general public asks about tornadoes, from how a storm's strength is rated to where Tornado Alley lies and what separates a watch from a warning. These are the questions ordinary people ask, not the problems specialists study. The answers cover what a tornado is, where and when tornadoes form, and how forecasters count them, warn about them, and rate them. Those answers are a sound foundation, but several of them read differently now that tornado activity has risen in the East and stretched into the colder months. Each slide takes one basic question and looks at what the familiar answer leaves out for someone deciding when to take cover.

A tornado is defined by its contact with the ground, not by whether anyone can see it. Many people decide whether to take cover by looking for a funnel, and that test can fail. A tornado's wind is invisible, and it becomes visible only if a condensation funnel forms, according to the NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory. A tornado can therefore be on the ground and doing damage while the sky shows no funnel at all. The funnel that people do see may not be the danger yet, either. A funnel that has not reached the ground is a funnel cloud, not a tornado, and the danger begins at the moment of ground contact, which is hard to judge from a distance. A person who plans to take cover once they see a tornado may never get that signal. The cue to move has to be a warning, not a sighting.

The harm a tornado does depends on what stands where it touches down as much as on the strength of its wind. Even a tornado at the bottom of the Enhanced Fujita scale can destroy a manufactured home, according to the U.S. Census Bureau. A low rating may sound reassuring on the news, but it offers no safety to people who live in manufactured housing. Some communities carry that risk across much of their housing. In St. Helena Parish, La., 45% of homes are mobile homes, so the mildest tornado there is could wreck a large share of the housing in the parish.

For people in manufactured homes, every tornado warning calls for the same urgency, whatever strength forecasters expect, and the time to act comes before anything is visible.

Tornadoes are not an American peculiarity, even though many Americans picture them as part of the Plains landscape. Tornadoes occur in Australia, Europe, Africa, Asia, and South America, according to the NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory. Argentina and Bangladesh both see high concentrations of tornadoes, so some of the heaviest tornado activity happens on other continents, far from Kansas and Oklahoma. Tornadoes also reach New Zealand. It is not a hotspot, but few people would think of it as tornado country at all. A storm that can produce a tornado is dangerous in any country. A traveler or an American living abroad should take a tornado warning there as seriously as one at home.

Within the U.S., tornadoes strike wooded land as well as open, flat country, and they have damaged more forest in recent decades. Tornado damage to forest land grew 36% across the latest two decades, according to a US Forest Service study led by Holly L. Munro of the agency's Southern Research Station. Tornadoes are tearing through more wooded ground, not only the open prairie of the popular image. A tornado that crosses timber country destroys forest as well as buildings. That damage harms the land and the people who earn a living from it, not only anyone standing in the storm's path. The honest answer to where tornadoes occur covers far more ground than the familiar picture does, including the wooded places many people may have assumed lay outside tornado country.

Americans face about 1,200 tornadoes in a typical year, according to the NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory. That makes tornadoes a regular part of life in this country, and every household in a tornado-prone region needs a plan. The normal behind that number is younger than most people assume, though. Official tornado records exist only from 1950 on, so the whole average fits within living memory, and the true long-run average remains unknown. Methods for finding and logging tornadoes shifted over the years. The average therefore mixes early years with recent ones that were not tallied on equal terms. The number is right about the size of the threat, but its exact digits are softer than they look.

A normal year looks larger once better counting is taken into account. Harold Brooks of the National Severe Storms Laboratory and Greg Carbin of the Storm Prediction Center put the adjusted average at 1,283.3 tornadoes a year, according to the NOAA Storm Prediction Center. They built that estimate as a statistical average standardized to 2007 reporting levels, not as a raw count of tornadoes people observed. The two estimates describe different things. One describes what people recorded, and the other describes what a year might look like if every year had been counted the way tornadoes were counted in 2007. Even the adjusted figure leaves considerable uncertainty about what an average year looks like. When forecasters call a season above or below average, the yardstick itself is approximate.

Tornado counts are least settled while a season is still under way. Officials build the current year's running total from preliminary local storm reports and adjust it to reduce duplicate reports, according to the NOAA Storm Prediction Center. A mid-season tally in the news is a preliminary figure, not a final one. A national running total is a count of reports across the country, not a measure of how exposed any one town is that week.

A high or low count is no reason to relax or to panic, because the preparation stays the same every year.

Many people hear the name Tornado Alley as a boundary, and they draw a comforting conclusion from it: people who live outside the alley can treat tornadoes as someone else's problem. "However, the idea of a “tornado alley” can be misleading," the NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory said in its own answer to the question. Every one of the fifty states has a reported tornado on record. Tornado Alley marks the region where tornadoes cluster most often, not the only region at risk. The most violent tornadoes also strike outside the alley every year, and in 2026 the heaviest tornado activity in the country was centered well east of the Plains. By mid-August, central Illinois had the highest reported tornado density in the nation, according to the Chicago Sun-Times.

Illinois is a clear example of how far real tornado activity can stray from the Great Plains picture. By late August, the state had recorded 207 tornadoes and counting, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. That count far exceeds a typical Illinois year, which brings closer to 50 tornadoes and already made the state tornado country before 2026. The surge came while national tornado activity ran below average in 2026. The danger that year was concentrated in one state rather than spread across a busy season nationwide, and that state lies outside the region most Americans associate with tornadoes.

For a reader, the useful question is not whether their county falls inside Tornado Alley but whether they would respond to a warning as quickly as someone who lives there.

Americans tend to talk about "tornado season" as one stretch of the year that the whole country shares, but the timing of peak tornado activity differs from one region to the next. The NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory treats the Southern Plains of Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas as one region with its own peak. Minnesota, Iowa, Nebraska and both Dakotas make up another region, spanning the upper Midwest and northern Plains, with a peak of its own. Even the Southern Plains peak describes when tornadoes have tended to cluster there, not a fixed schedule. A family in Minnesota that sets its guard by the Oklahoma pattern would be watching for the wrong weeks. A family in Oklahoma that relaxes once the usual peak has passed would be treating an average as a promise. The useful question is not when tornado season is, but when it is in the region where you live.

A regional peak is the stretch when the odds of a tornado are highest, which is different from the only time danger exists. Federal storm scientists are clear that no month is safe. "But, remember, tornadoes can happen at any time of year," the NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory said. Knowing your own region's peak is still worth the effort. It tells you when to check forecasts most closely and when to make sure everyone in the house knows where to shelter. A tornado warning in an off month deserves the same immediate response as one at the height of the local season.

Most people hear "watch" and "warning" as a milder and a stronger version of the same worry. In fact, the two words give different instructions: a watch means get ready, and a warning means move. "A tornado warning indicates that you should ACT NOW to find safe shelter!" the NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory said. That order does not depend on anyone spotting a tornado. Forecasters can issue a warning when radar indicates a tornado, without any report from a spotter. A person who waits to see a funnel before heading for shelter is waiting for a sight that the warning does not require. The warning itself is the signal to go.

An order to act at once leaves no time to work out where to go, so the decision has to come earlier, during the watch. Emergency planners treat the choice of shelter as preparation, not as a reaction to the alarm. The NIH Office of Research Services advises employees to locate their two closest shelter areas ahead of time. Picking the right room matters as much as moving quickly, and a fast reaction does little good if it ends in the wrong place. Top floors, areas with glass, and rooms with wide-span ceilings are the places to avoid. Taking shelter means leaving those spaces for a safer room, not just stopping wherever you happen to be standing when the warning sounds.

A watch is the time to pick the room, and a warning is the time to be in it.

A tornado's rating is an estimate worked out from what the storm broke, not a wind speed that an instrument recorded. The NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory reports that the National Weather Service rates tornadoes on the Enhanced Fujita Scale, which has 28 damage indicators covering the kinds of things a storm can wreck. Surveyors then judge each indicator across eight degrees of damage. A rating is a graded reading of a damage checklist rather than a measured gust, so it depends on what stood in the tornado's path and on which parts of that path surveyors could reach.

Even a storm severe enough to rate near the top of the scale can leave damage that the scale doesn't count. The National Weather Service rated the Enid, Okla., tornado an EF4, and federal researchers surveying its path documented damage to items that the scale's indicators don't include, according to NSSL. Damage with no place on the checklist can't raise a rating, so the official number can understate or miss part of what a storm did. Anyone judging a storm afterward should read a modest rating as the least it did, not the most.

Ground crews can't safely walk every stretch of a damage path, and a rating can reflect only the stretches they reach. To make sense of the damage the scale doesn't list, researchers compared it with standard indicators nearby, according to NSSL. As long as that kind of damage has no place on the checklist, a rating remains a best judgment based on what crews could reach and what the scale includes.

The way forecasters rate tornado strength changed in 2007, when the National Weather Service replaced the original Fujita Scale with the Enhanced Fujita Scale. Old ratings were never revised to fit the new scale, and a storm that earned an F5 before the switch keeps that rating. A correlation between the two scales lets researchers set older and newer storms side by side.

Anyone who calls a new storm as strong as a legendary one is relying on that link between two damage-based scales, not on a single unbroken way of measuring.

Scientists still can't fully explain how tornadoes form, even though the question sounds basic. What they do know points to the storms that deserve the most fear. NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory describes the supercell as a rotating thunderstorm built around a mesocyclone. The most destructive tornadoes come from supercells, according to Smithsonian Magazine. The explanation fails at the step from a rotating storm to a tornado on the ground. That step is not fully understood, and researchers are weighing at least three competing ideas for where the spin near the ground comes from. For anyone watching the sky, a rotating storm is a warning sign, not a sure sign that a tornado will follow.

The best field evidence narrowed the search without solving it. Results from the VORTEX2 project link tornado development to temperature differences at the edge of a storm's downdraft once a mesocyclone exists, but that link remains a theory, not settled science. Modeling studies have also produced tornadoes without that temperature pattern, so a storm that lacks it can't be written off. "The big takeaway that we got from VORTEX-2 was that you can’t really tell whether a storm is going to be tornadic or non-tornadic just by how it looks on radar or what the weather balloons in its proximity show you," Robin Tanamachi, a research meteorologist at Purdue University, told Smithsonian Magazine. No one watching radar can promise which rotating storm will produce a tornado, and anyone who waits for that certainty before taking cover has waited too long.

The standard supercell explanation fits Southeast tornadoes worst. Many tornadoes there form from squall lines, which are long lines of storms, rather than from single rotating supercells, according to Smithsonian Magazine. For those tornadoes, the gap in knowledge covers the whole process rather than only its final step, and Texas Tech University atmospheric scientist Johannes Dahl says researchers do not know how they work. That gap matters in a region where tornadoes in 2011 took more than 300 lives.

Research into how tornadoes form continues, and much of it is unfinished. Scientists at the National Severe Storms Laboratory simulate tornadic storms in computer models, and the lab acknowledges that much work remains. The tornado itself is a small feature, closer to 65 feet across in a typical case, according to Smithsonian Magazine. Penn State University atmospheric scientist Paul Markowski says tornado prediction may be reaching limits set by chaos. Forecasters can expect to issue better warnings but not certain ones.

The final judgment will stay with the person watching the sky.

Most people picture a dark funnel hanging from a storm cloud, and they treat the sight of one as the moment of danger. Trained spotters draw the line somewhere else. The NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory says that a funnel that has not reached the ground is only a funnel cloud, and that it becomes a tornado once it touches the ground. The question that matters is where the bottom of the cloud ends, not how menacing its shape looks. Spotters confirm a tornado by finding dust and debris beneath the funnel. Anyone watching a storm should keep their eyes on the ground under the cloud as well as on the sky above it. The proof that a tornado has formed shows up down there.

The more dangerous mistake runs the other way, when people assume that no funnel means no tornado. "Be careful! Tornadoes can form and be in contact with the ground without a fully condensed funnel!" the lab said. A person waiting for the classic shape may be waiting for something that never fully appears, even while a tornado is already on the ground nearby. The storm's other signals deserve action in their own right. The CDC lists large hail as a sign of tornado weather. Hail of that size is a reason to move to shelter even when no funnel is in sight.

These cues are not reserved for trained spotters, and anyone who sees a storm can use them as a backup to official warnings. Any one of them, whether debris at ground level, a funnel touching the ground or large hail, calls for taking cover right away. Waiting for a second sign to confirm the first only uses up time.
