Watch Buffalo Springfield Come Together for the First Time in Many Years

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

When Richie Furay was first approached with the idea of creating a documentary about his life, the founding member of Buffalo Springfield and Poco was very skeptical. It was nearly a decade ago that his late manager, David Stone, proposed the idea. “I was like, ‘Really?’” the 82-year-old musician remembers over a recent Zoom call from his home in Boulder, Colorado. “‘[But] I’m just the guy in the middle.’”

This is exactly what makes Through It All: The Life and Influence of Richie Furay so compelling. Furay was a country-rock pioneer who played in two groundbreaking bands (as well as the supergroup Souther-Hillman-Furay Band), but he never became a household name like many of his peers. In the mid-1970s, he converted to Christianity, and devoted his life to faith and his family. “I have to stop and say, ‘Look, it’s not about my name being written in lights at Carnegie Hall,’” Furay says. “For me, it’s my name being written in the Lamb’s Book of Life that’s really important.”

Through It All, directed by Denny Klein, has been completed, and will be submitted to festivals in the near future. The film features narration by Cameron Crowe, and includes interviews with David Geffen, Clive Davis, J.D. Souther, and more. You can also watch Furay meeting up with his Buffalo Springfield bandmates Neil Young and Stephen Stills, in a clip shared exclusively with Rolling Stone.

Below, Furay looks back on his career and discusses what it’s like to watch his life on film, and the legacy he wants to leave behind. “What can I say? It’s been an interesting journey,” he says. “When I left Yellow Springs, Ohio, to be a folk singer in New York, who knew what it was going to lead to all these years later, to us talking today?”

I imagine it’s surreal to watch a film about yourself. It must be like watching your life flash before your eyes.I know. I was hoping I’d still be around when it came out, so at least I got to see it now.

Nine years in the making is a long time. At any point, did you worry It might not get released?Yeah. With Covid and David passing away, and other things that they have to do to get everything working right… It’s taken a long time. But Denny’s been persistent, and when David was here, he was persistent, too. I knew they were going to go to the finish line as far as they could go. And Denny has certainly picked up the ball and he ran with it. Have you seen the film yet?I did. I saw it over the weekend, on Friday night. That’s the first I’ve seen it. I’ve been the subject, but it’s been their project. So it was kind of all new to me, too. Although we did hours and hours of interviews. And then to break all of that down is like, oh, my gosh. How in the world do you do that?

I’m curious what it’s like to work with Cameron, because this is someone who interviewed you several decades ago.Well, I was Cameron’s first interview for Rolling Stone [in 1973]. And that was years and years ago. He was a kid, and I was a kid. And I had not seen or talked to Cameron for the longest time. And somehow on my social media [in 2017], a Cameron Crowe appeared, and I wrote behind the scenes and said, “If this is really you, I want to talk to you. Because I want to invite you to a show that I’m doing at the Saban Theatre in Los Angeles with my friend Timothy B. Schmit, and I want you to come.” And he wrote back and said, “It’s me, I’ll be there.” And that’s how we reconnected. And then to have Cameron actually be a part of what’s going on here is just more than I could have ever thought of or asked. It’s been really wonderful. He’s such a great guy. I love him.

In the film, he expresses a lot of guilt over his Rolling Stone headline, “Poor Poco: They Were ‘The Next Big Thing’ Four Years Ago.” He didn’t write that headline, but I have no idea who did.I have no idea, either. Have you seen Almost Famous?A long time ago. It reminded me of Poco to a certain degree, but Poco didn’t really ever attain the success when I was in the band that the movie had the group making at the time. But that’s part of movie-making, and that’s why he is a great filmmaker.

This documentary is emotional at times to watch, because it has interviews with people who are no longer with us: David, Clive Davis, and the great J.D. Souther.As I was watching this, it certainly put a bump in my stomach as well. I mean, from Clive to J.D. to Steve Cropper that I worked with and others. Wow, it just goes to show you how short life is, and you never know how long you’re going to be here.

When I was recording [2022’s] In the Country, J.D. came to the studio to do the interview. We hadn’t seen each other for a while. And people try to make things up. I can’t believe how much people try to talk about, “Oh man, these guys couldn’t get together. They didn’t like each other.” It was all nonsense.

They don’t know what’s going on in the background of people’s lives. J.D. and I never had any difficulties at all. I will say that when David Geffen put the band together, it looked good on paper, but not always what looks good on paper translates into reality. Because David’s thing was, “Hey, we’ll just put together another Crosby, Stills, and Nash.” It’s like, “Really? It’s that easy, David? I’ve been at this for six years now.” And yeah, great songwriters, great singers, great band, great musicians. But like I said, what looks good on paper sometimes doesn’t translate into reality.

And then, who knows what’s going on in the background? I was in the middle of a personal and family crisis when that whole band got together. That took its toll, but it had nothing to do with how we got together. When J.D. came and did the interview, it was just so good to see him. I mean, the hugs that we had and the pictures that we took. It was just wonderful.

When was the last time you had seen him prior to that?I guess I went to one of his concerts, and I think it was in Cleveland, and I can’t even remember the year. It was one of his solo concerts, and I was back there with a friend, and we went to see the concert. When J.D. played here in Denver, I would see him, and if I happened to be in the town where he was playing, we’d make the time and go see him.

Do you remember your last conversation with him?It was when we were doing In the Country. There was so much going on there, sometimes it just comes and goes, and I can’t remember. But I remember the feeling that I had after he had left. It was like, “Wow, man, I’m really glad I got a chance to see and talk to you.”

Let’s talk about this Buffalo Springfield conversation that takes place in the film. When did that happen?About two years ago. So it was very recent. We were actually going to get together before we got together in California, but there were some worldly circumstances that got involved, and Neil said, “I can’t do it now.” It was interesting though, because Stephen and I had a relationship before Stephen and Neil or Stephen and Neil and I had a relationship. So Stephen and I went on to do an interview together, which was our part of the being together. And then we had the three of us together, which is a whole other dynamic.

I love that no matter how many years pass, you guys are still laughing and enjoying each other’s company.Isn’t that cool?

When did you last see Neil before that?Oh, before that, I guess it would’ve been when we did the [Buffalo Springfield] concerts [in 2011]. That would’ve been the last time that I actually saw Neil.

You seem to not have any hard feelings about that reunion falling apart.No. It all worked out typically, as it usually did. I think we did seven shows, and then Bonnaroo was the very last one. And after every show, we’d always get together and we’d decompress and go, and then everybody would go their [separate ways]. After Bonnaroo, I never saw anybody. It was crazy. It was like, “Well, this must be over now.”

Would you ever play with them again? I’ve seen Stephen’s Light Up the Blues benefit, and he and Neil have both played Buffalo Springfield songs. I’d love to see you on the stage with them.Well, David offered my services for the one about, I don’t know, maybe two years ago, and we never heard back. And I understand. The question hasn’t come up, so I haven’t had to deal with whether I would or not. I love those guys, and a lot’s just passed. We’re getting older and older as we go. I’m getting ready to do my last two concerts starting on Friday and Saturday.

The last ever?Well, unless there’s something that comes of the documentary. I’ve made a commitment to go out if there is something. Sundance is coming to Boulder, and if Sundance picks it up for whatever reason, there’s a possibility that I could do a short concert here for that as well.

Other than that, it’s just time. It’s just getting harder and harder to get out on the road. I’m not working at the Sphere every weekend, [like the Eagles]. I love those guys, man. I’ve been out there a couple times now, and I love what they do. I’m so happy for them, but it’s harder and harder for me just to get out and travel around.

What did you think of the Eagles at the Sphere? It’s such an amazing show.Oh, I loved it. My wife Nancy and I saw it, and then I came back and I said, “My daughter has to see this.” And it took us another, I don’t know, probably eight or nine months to get her out there. But I just had to go, because it was so dynamic. I loved it. I loved every bit of it.

I mean, Timothy and I have been very good friends for a long time, and we walked backstage and I’m seeing the whole setup that they have out there, and I just love what they’re doing. I think for whatever impact I had on that band or Poco had on the band … well, they had two of my bass players in Poco. So, we must have had some influence on them. But yeah, I love them. The music, you just sit back and you just smile. And plus, the presentation at the Sphere is just absolutely overwhelming.

What was that like for you when you started hearing songs like ”Take It Easy” on the radio?When I first heard “Take It Easy” it was a heartbreaker, because we had just released “A Good Feelin’ to Know.” We were in the New York area. I’ve played every SUNY there is, every gymnasium. And we had just released it and we’re driving into one of the other state universities in New York, and all of a sudden it was, “Well, I’m traveling down the road trying to loosen my load.” And it’s like, “Oh, boy.” That’s when I got a hold of David Geffen and he convinced me [to form Hillman-Souther-Furay].

I love that David is in the film. He doesn’t do that for a lot of people.No, I know he doesn’t. And David, I feel that both of us have respect for one another. He stayed in my house before. My car broke down and I was bringing him home. That was probably, oh golly, 30 years ago. It was during the Poco time.

I haven’t seen David in a long time, but at one point in time I wrote him a letter. This was probably about 20 years ago, if not more. And I wrote him a letter just appreciating him and our relationship. And you know what? Within a week, I had another letter written back. So we haven’t really seen each other, but I’m very thankful for the relationship that we had over the years.

Your vocals are remarkably preserved. You sound incredible.Well, it’s been by God’s grace, there’s no doubt about it. Because now I feel like I’m one and done, maybe two [shows]. If I try to do three, man, it’s kind of really difficult. I did three in July. We went from Kent, Ohio, down to Cincinnati and then down to Nashville. And by the time we got to Nashville, it was like, “Oh my goodness, this is not the place I don’t want my best voice.” It holds up, and I’m very thankful for that. I’m just hoping it holds up for two more shows.

How did you feel about your later life being portrayed? It’s not something many people know about. You have a very special story.Right. And it was presented in that way. Denny was insistent, and that was the only way I think that this was going to be anything different than any other documentary. Here’s where this guy’s life has led him, and I think they certainly covered that and portrayed that well.

Do you ever think about what your life would’ve been like had Al Perkins not joined the band, and you hadn’t become a Christian? Or if Souther-Hillman-Furay had taken off and you became stars?Yeah, it probably would’ve been different. I don’t know that Nancy and I would’ve made 60 years. So I wouldn’t go back and trade my life today for any of the success that music might have brought me for what I have in almost 60 years of marriage and four daughters and 13 grandkids. There’s not even any way to compare that.

I’ve been very thankful that the journey has allowed me, even doing 35 years of pastoring a church, to continue to be able to play music. I mean, the two shows that I’m doing in California, they’re not huge venues, but they’re sold out. And so it makes you feel good that my whole life still is having an impact in touching people’s lives.

You obviously have very different political views from Neil and Stephen. Does that ever get in the way of your conversations?No, because we’re adults. And that’s how it has with some, and I’ve reconciled with some. For others, they’re respectful of me and I’m respectful of them, and that’s where it stands. And that’s how my relationship with Stephen and Neil has always been.

Yeah, we’ve been different. We’ve been as far apart, probably, as you said, politically as one can be. And it doesn’t matter when we get together. When we hug each other, you can tell that it’s real, that our friendships that we established years and years ago are meaningful. They weren’t just fleeting. They were real, and that’s how it should be.

You can have your opinions about things, and I can have my opinions about things, and you know what? Then let’s move on. When we did a band, it was like a marriage. And if that marriage breaks up, it can be a crisis, but it also can be a workable situation, and we’ve decided to make it a workable situation.

In the film, Neil jokes that he should have gone to Monterey. What are your memories of the festival?I was actually struggling with tonsillitis. I’d just gone through a tonsillectomy or whatever, and it was difficult. But not having Neil there was certainly very difficult. I mean, I loved David [Crosby], and he was a sweet guy. But he did not fill the bill. And even Buffalo Springfield had nine people in and out of the band in two years. It was a Canadian band, and those Canadians, I don’t know what to tell you [laughs], but the original band for me was the band with Dewey [Martin] and Bruce [Palmer], and then Stephen and Neil and myself. And everything else was just trying to patch it together, and it was difficult to do. And Monterey didn’t have the original band, and it made it difficult. But I sure loved watching Otis Redding and Jimi Hendrix. I mean, it was incredible.

It’s a tragedy that it’s the only known concert footage of the band, and Neil isn’t even in it.Isn’t that incredible? I mean, there’s footage of Bob Dylan. It’s like, “Where did this come from?” And yet you go here and there’s nothing on Buffalo Springfield, nothing. I mean, very, very rare footage of anything.

How often do you listen to Buffalo Springfield?Not very often. It’s interesting, Nancy and I get in the car and we’re driving someplace. We don’t even put music on the radio usually. It’s just like, let’s just have some peace and quiet in this little cubicle that’s going across the country or going to the next town.

Nancy is pretty funny in the film. She’s very candid about how she was going to leave you, but then you told her you became a Christian. She was disappointed you wanted to make it work.I know. There was dead silence. I thought she would be happy, and little did I know she was already making plans for another life. Oh gosh, it broke my heart at the time. It was a very difficult time. That was what was going on in the background during Souther-Hillman-Furay. She was hoping to get out of it and felt, “Oh, I can’t do this now.”

What was it like for your grandkids to watch their grandfather getting this movie about his life?We’ve tried to make it that I’m just a normal guy that just happened to have a different life than others. We go to all the sporting and dancing events, and they’ve grown up with me knowing me more just like this, than trying to put on a rock & roll show or something. They all sat and they watched it. We had to watch it in different rooms because I think there were 20 of us by the time we got everybody together. And I think they were very touched. I had to go home and I had to digest everything that I saw, and I think they were doing the same thing. Now, if it makes it onto some other stages or something, I think it’ll have a whole different impact on them, and on me.

You stopped being a pastor in 2017. What brought on that decision?Nancy and I were sitting at a restaurant on Christmas Eve. We usually have our Christmas Eve services. And she said, “Are you OK? The whole left side of your face is drooping.” And we went over to the doctor and I had Bell’s palsy. I’d never even heard of Bell’s palsy.

And at that particular time, I just got to thinking how fast things can happen, how fast things can change. So I made the decision then. I think it’s time after all these years that we spend more time together focusing on ourselves rather than having other things dictate what we do and why we’re doing it. And that was the decision that I made to step away from the pulpit ministry.

So I don’t have a church today. I’m not going to tell somebody how to believe, but I will tell people why I believe. I’ll say that God gave us the gift to sing these songs to you. So it’s a different kind of lifestyle now. It’s not getting up and preparing and doing things for a Sunday morning service or whatever. It’s just life now. What do you want your legacy to be?[Quoting the Bible]: “Well done, good and faithful servant.” On all regards. I have a precious love for the people that have come out and still support me in a musical way. And I’m humbled and gracious that people would still pay money to come to hear me play the songs. And yet on the other side, I want people to know that I have a very strong faith in Jesus Christ, and I want the Lord, when I finally cross that line into eternity, to say, “Well done, good and faithful servant.”

If there is one thing you could tell a young Richie, what would it be?Stay focused. And I’m sorry, Angie, but don’t believe all the press you get. Whether good or bad. Because if it’s good for a while, it’s all about to blow your ego up, and if it’s bad, you’re going to just kick the can down the road and be discouraged and disgusted. And you just gotta stay focused, and you gotta stay true to yourself.
