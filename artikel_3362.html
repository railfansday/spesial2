Your Favorite THC Drinks Are About to Be Illegal

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

On Tuesday, more than two dozen veterans organizations sent an open letter to bipartisan congressional leaders demanding something that a few years ago might have been unheard of: better access to legal weed. “Veterans have sacrificed for our country, and they deserve safe, responsible options for managing chronic pain, PTSD, anxiety, sleep problems, and other health conditions,” it read. “Many veterans… rely on hemp products as an alternative to opioids. If Congress fails to act, the products they depend on will suddenly become illegal.”

That’s because on Dec. 11, a new law that limits the amount of THC allowed in hemp will go into effect, eradicating the cannabis market as Americans have come to know it. The colorful cans of THC sodas on Trader Joe’s shelves will be gone, as will the sleep-aid gummies from Target and and the bottles of THC “liquor” at Total Wines. “Anyone who produces the seed, grows a crop, processes the crop, makes a product, sells it to the shelf, and the people who buy it — all these people will be impacted,” explains Greg Serrao, co-founder of Willie’s Remedy, one of the country’s best-selling cannabis drinks.

The new definition will close an eight-year loophole that allowed the CBD market to take off, followed by the federally legal THC market. Veterans, farmers, and cannabis entrepreneurs agree that more regulations are needed to help finally wrangle what was, for a long time, a frontier industry. “A lot of people paint this picture of hemp as this wild west,” says Serrao. “That’s not true.”

What is true is that unless Congress acts to amend the new rules, in less than a month, a $28 billion industry will disappear. “Something like 65 percent of our customers are senior citizens, and 70 percent are over 45,” says Willie’s Remedy’s other co-founder, Forrest Dein.

Tim Sheehy (R-Mont.) and Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.) introduced a bill to the Senate on Tuesday that would halt the new rules, instead offering a new set of regulations around hemp-derived THC products, including age limits, testing and packaging requirements, and other guardrails around the plant. “Our bill will put every bad actor in the hemp space out of business and keep intoxicating products out of the hands of children,” Sheehy, a veteran, said in a press release. “It will also stop Congress from turning the farmers, veterans, and cancer patients who rely on these products into criminals overnight.” Editor’s picks The 250 Greatest Albums of the 21st Century So Far The 100 Best TV Episodes of All Time The 500 Greatest Albums of All Time 100 Best Movies of the 21st Century

But unless that advances soon, the businesses who rely on hemp — including the struggling farmers who were told hemp would be the crop to save them — will be shit out of luck.  “The collateral damage is that we axe this American commodity that has so much hope,” explains Morgan Tweet, who runs IND Hemp, a company that processes hemp into products like rope and fiber. “It’s really something special for American farmers, and for American businesses, and manufacturers. There’s nothing in our generation that comes close to being able to show the potential that hemp has with how diverse and how useful of a crop it is, and the magnitude of products you can make from it.”

It all started back in 2018, when Sen. Mitch McConnell accidentally legalized weed — kind of.

On Dec. 20, 2018, President Donald Trump signed the Agricultural Improvement Act, colloquially known as the Farm Bill, into law. In between protections for dairy farmers and changes to the federal food assistance program was a monumental change: for the first time since 1937, growing hemp would be legal in the United States. McConnell himself championed the change, positioning it as a boon to farmers in his home state of Kentucky. The text of the bill defined hemp as a cannabis plant that contained no more than .3 percent delta-nine THC, weed’s intoxicating compound, on a dry-weight basis. “I do not have any plans to endorse the legalization of marijuana,” McConnell said, noting that hemp and marijuana were “two separate plants.” Related Content Summer Walker Files $20 Million Cross-Complaint Against Interscope Julio Iglesias Under Investigation for Sexual Abuse Claims What I Learned About Genocide From the Rohingya Muslims Mullin Says Skipping LSU Game Amid FAA Crisis Would Have Caused ‘Confusion’

By then, hemp was already a popular crop in Europe, where it had long been used for everything from oil to rope. It was also at the center of the growing CBD industry. CBD, another cannabinoid found in hemp, was not intoxicating, like THC, but was starting to be used medicinally for things like epilepsy and pain management.

Once hemp was legalized, American farmers started planting the crop. Some varieties focused on bushy leaves and heavy stalks, great for industrial uses. But others had big flowers, perfect for extracting CBD. The CBD boom was expected; what wasn’t was that scientists quickly figured out a process to convert CBD into THC — and the hemp-derived THC market took off. Not only did they figure out how to make delta-9 THC, the cannabinoid that is known to get us high, they also started to make a wide variety of other kinds of cannabinoids, including delta-8 THC (a slightly less potent intoxicant that is present in trace amounts in the plant), as well as some that don’t actually occur naturally. It was all legal because the products were derived from hemp, not marijuana, which is a Schedule I controlled substance.

Many of these companies self-policed, testing the products themselves and going through established liquor frameworks in order to be sold at national chains. Others, however, skirted the rules, offering shady products made with questionable cannabinoids and no real testing or oversight. This so-called “gas station weed” started to make folks nervous — particularly McConnell. So when Congress was putting together a bill to reopen the government last fall, after a record-long shutdown, McConnell added an amendment to the 2018 definition of hemp to get rid of the market as we know it.

The new law will change the definition of legal hemp. Previously, hemp was allowed to have up to .3 percent THC as measured in its dry weight. Hemp farmers could grow crops that adhered to this, then processors could extract cannabinoids, and companies put them into products so that they had enough potency to see effects. The new law, however, looks at the final product, restricting anything with more than .4 milligrams of THC per container.

There are two parts to the law, explains Neil M. Willner, an attorney with Vicente LLP, a New York-based firm specializing in cannabis law. The first went into effect on Nov. 12, and banned the sale of all synthetic THC products, or THC compounds that are not found naturally in the cannabis plant. On Dec. 11, the low-dose sodas, liquors, and gummies that have become so popular — and usually contain five or 10 mg of THC per serving — will no longer be legal on a federal level. People in areas with state-legal cannabis programs can still go to a dispensary to buy THC products, but the companies built around hemp-derived cannabinoids — and the people in states without legal weed — will be out of luck.

It will also make it nearly impossible to grow hemp, too. “We’ve talked to a lot of hemp farmers, and it’s very difficult for them to manage the THC content of their crops,” says Dein. “It’ll push a lot of farmers out of business.” Tweet, owner of IND Hemp, the processing facility, adds that the new law doesn’t define what a “container” actually is. “I ship 55-gallon drums of hemp seed oil all over the country, and by definition, that’s a container, right?” she says. “Like, we do make a product that likely will go above that 0.4 milligram [limit] just because of that volume. Even though hemp seed oil doesn’t have a lot of cannabinoids in it, it does have some, and so there is some risk to our business.”

In one word, bad. Take, for example, Texas, where outgoing Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick introduced a bill over the summer that would have banned all hemp products that contained THC. This was wildly unpopular with many of the 1.5 million veterans who live in the state, among others. Outgoing Governor Greg Abbott ended up vetoing the bill, instead establishing guidelines for age and marketing restrictions. In November, Patrick lost his bid for reelection, as did Abbott and other Republicans statewide. “I think that’s one of the reasons Texas went blue,” says Serrao.

Everyone who spoke to Rolling Stone for this article agreed that further federal regulations are needed in order for the industry to safely grow. Getting rid of full synthetics, as the law did on Nov. 12, is a positive step, they say, but “what we don’t have is purity standards,” explains Tweet. “We don’t have any regulations, so it’s only the people that self-regulate, that put good products on the market that are the ones you should be trusting. But the reality is that consumers have no idea what’s in their product. They don’t know what they’re buying.”

Willie Nelson, himself a longtime cannabis outlaw, would like to see regulation come to the industry, rather than a step backward to prohibition. “I was born the year Prohibition ended, in 1933, and we know that approach does not work,” he tells Rolling Stone in a statement. “This ban punishes the wrong people. It hurts the family farmers growing a legal crop, and it hurts the millions of seniors, veterans, and patients who rely on hemp products for pain relief, sleep, and wellness. Congress needs to regulate this industry for safety, not destroy it.”
