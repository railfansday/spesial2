Why Connectivity Needs To Become Programmable

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Mobile networks were designed with a person at the end of every connection. A contract carries a name, and a plan is sized for one phone. When something breaks, someone calls support or walks into a store.

That design fits a shrinking share of the people and things now using networks. Delivery robots, connected cars, smart meters and medical wearables are among the fastest-growing users, and none of them has a name on a contract. At the same time, AI assistants handle more everyday tasks for people, but connectivity is still largely out of their reach.

After years of building connectivity for travelers and distributed teams, I see these as one problem. Whether the user is a person or a device, the connection should be set up and managed through software. In other words, connectivity has to become programmable.

But machines don’t sign contracts, so someone has to own the relationship on their behalf. They also move: A sensor shipped from a factory in Poland may spend its working life in Brazil. Their usage looks nothing like a person’s. A smart meter sends a few kilobytes a day for 10 years, while a robotaxi can move gigabytes in an afternoon, and a consumer-style plan suits neither.

People run into the same friction, though it’s less visible. In our support conversations, the hardest questions are usually about status rather than speed: Is my plan active? Why did data stop at the border? Which line am I paying for? A well-designed system should answer those without requiring a support ticket.

Ericsson counted around 4.5 billion cellular IoT connections at the end of 2025 and expects that number to approach 8 billion by 2031. Its June 2026 Mobility Report also notes that uplink traffic is growing faster than downlink for most operators, and that additional AI traffic could push uplink to three times its 2025 level by 2031.

Software is also starting to act for people. Gartner predicted that 40% of enterprise applications would include task-specific AI agents by 2026, up from less than 5% in 2025. If an assistant can book your hotel, people will reasonably expect it to get them online at the destination as well. Today, that step usually leads to a QR code or a carrier portal designed for manual clicks.

Put these trends together, and more connectivity decisions will be made by software acting on someone’s behalf.

A SIM in every device connects the device, but it doesn’t make the connection manageable. Programmable connectivity means the connection itself can be requested, switched, limited and retired through APIs, much as cloud platforms let teams manage servers. I think that requires four capabilities.

• Delegated Identity: Every connection should be traceable to an accountable owner such as a company or a fleet operator, even when software manages it. Otherwise, fraud and misuse are very hard to attribute.

• Programmatic Provisioning: Devices without screens can’t scan QR codes. The GSMA’s SGP.32 specification already allows eSIM profiles on IoT devices to be downloaded, switched and retired remotely. The next step is exposing that capability through APIs, so a fleet platform, an app or an AI assistant can arrange connectivity on demand.

• Policy And Spend Controls: Software should manage connectivity within set limits: which networks, which regions, how much data and what maximum cost. Finance teams already set budgets for cloud usage, and connectivity should work the same way.

• Life Cycle Portability: A connected car or medical device may outlive three generations of network technology and several carrier contracts. Its connectivity has to move with it, across borders and providers, without anyone opening the casing.

Our enterprise customers rarely struggle with the first activation. Problems tend to come months later, when an employee relocates or a device changes hands. Those are the moments where programmable connectivity saves the most time.

A fair objection is that operators will build this themselves, and some already are. Network slicing reserves guaranteed performance for specific use cases, and Ericsson counted 84 commercial offerings built on it in June 2026, up from 65 seven months earlier.

I see the roles as complementary. An operator runs its own network. A platform layer works across networks: countries, operators, device types and budgets. A car built in Germany, sold in Brazil and driven across borders needs both.

I learned this from travel. Travelers plan around destinations, such as Lisbon on Monday and Madrid on Thursday, rather than around operators. The platform’s job is to match that plan with the right network at the right price, without asking anyone to understand roaming agreements. eSIM is what makes this workable: Because the SIM profile is software, a a network identity can be switched or retired remotely, whether on a phone or on a sensor bolted to a shipping container.

Imagine booking a trip through an assistant that, with your permission, also arranges a local data plan within a budget you’ve set. You land, turn off airplane mode and you’re online.

For the person at the center, programmable connectivity should mean fewer decisions. People don’t want to have to compare roaming rates, scan QR codes or follow up on support tickets. Connectivity works best when people don’t have to think about it, and making it programmable is a practical way to get there.​
