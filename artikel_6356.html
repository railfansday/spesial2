Late-night snacking may be putting your liver at risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

A study of nearly 200,000 people links late-night eating to higher risks of fatty liver disease and cirrhosis, even after accounting for diet and weight.

When people think about the health effects of late-night snacking, they usually focus on calories. But new research suggests that when you eat may matter just as much.

Now, a 16-year study involving nearly 200,000 nurses and other health professionals has linked late-night eating to an increased risk of fatty liver disease.

People who said they ate after dinner or after bedtime went on to develop fatty liver disease and cirrhosis more often than people who didn’t.

That gap stayed after weight, diet quality and alcohol were taken into account. The authors can’t say the late eating caused the damage.

What they can say is that meal timing was linked to liver disease on its own, apart from how much or how well people ate.

Robert Wilechansky, a liver specialist at Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU), led the analysis with colleagues at OHSU and Massachusetts General Hospital.

Andrew McHill, a sleep and circadian physiologist at OHSU who studies the body clock, was a co-author of the study.

In an interview with Earth.com, McHill said the point of the study is broader than any one snack.

“It is not only what or how much you eat, but also the timing of when you eat that matters for your health,” he noted.

The data came from three long-running American health surveys.

These were the Nurses’ Health Study, its younger sister study, and the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study.

Together they contributed 199,155 people with no liver disease at the start. Most were women and the median age was 59. At the start, 22 percent had obesity and 5 percent had diabetes.

Every two to four years, participants reported what they ate and when.

Anyone who said they usually ate after dinner or after bedtime counted as a late-night eater. About 29 percent of the participants were in this category.

The surveys also asked whether people skipped breakfast and how many times a day they ate.

Participants also reported whether a doctor had diagnosed them with liver disease during follow-up surveys.

Over a median of 16 years, 4,492 people developed fatty liver disease, in which fat builds up in liver cells. Another 315 developed cirrhosis, the scarring that follows years of damage.

Late-night eaters developed fatty liver disease at a rate 36 percent higher than everyone else, and cirrhosis at a rate 28 percent higher.

Those figures already account for age, body mass index, daily calories, diabetes, cholesterol, smoking, exercise, alcohol and a standard score of diet quality.

Skipping breakfast and eating fewer or more meals a day looked risky at first. Those links disappeared once the same factors were counted, and only the late eating survived.

When the team also accounted for night-shift work, the cirrhosis link grew stronger rather than weaker, to 49 percent higher.

Setting aside anyone diagnosed in the first four years barely moved the numbers either, which argues against disease that was already under way.

McHill told Earth.com the team had expected a link, but not one that size.

“I think we were somewhat surprised by the strength of the relationship,” he said, especially after accounting for body weight, poor diet and drinking.

The liver has its own internal clock. Earlier research has shown that the timing of meals influences how the liver processes nutrients.

“We know the liver is very much in tune with the body’s circadian rhythms,” Wilechansky said.

Eating most of the day’s calories in the evening has been linked to obesity and poorer metabolic health.

A 2026 study found that in people with fatty liver disease, the liver was more resistant to insulin at night than in people with obesity alone.

McHill said eating when the body clock is promoting sleep makes the body less efficient at processing food.

He added that animal studies have shown the liver to be one of the organs most sensitive to a mismatch between eating and sleeping.

Asked by Earth.com what a habitual late snacker should take from this, McHill drew a line between a treat and a habit.

A dessert now and then is unlikely to hurt the liver, he said. A nightly habit, kept up over years, may carry health risks that a person doesn’t notice.

The people in these surveys were mostly white, well-educated health workers. They had lower rates of obesity and diabetes than the country as a whole, and lower rates of liver disease too.

The researchers had no liver scans or blood tests to confirm the diagnoses or assess how severe the disease had become.

Eating times were asked about in only a few survey rounds, so some late eaters were probably counted as early ones.

The authors said that kind of error tends to shrink a real link rather than invent one.

Wilechansky said only a small fraction of people with fatty liver disease go on to serious forms such as cirrhosis.

Whether moving dinner earlier would change anyone’s liver is the question the surveys can’t answer.

That is the next study. McHill said his team is recruiting people for a trial that will record meal times with photographic food diaries and track the liver with hospital imaging.

The trial could help answer a question that years of observational research cannot: Can changing when we eat actually protect the liver?

The full study was published in the journal Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology.
