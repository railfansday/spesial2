7 Social Security changes for 2027 include a 3.6% raise and a higher tax cap

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Social Security's 2027 raise is 3.6%, but Medicare premiums, the tax cap and claiming rules also shape what households keep.

The rules that govern older Americans' money change every year, and many of the changes happen without anyone deciding to make them. Each year Social Security resets the figures that decide how much retirees receive and how much older workers can earn, owe or bank toward future benefits. Many of the 2027 changes follow formulas set long in advance, so they take effect without any new vote. Those formulas rest on two separate measures. The yearly raise follows consumer prices, while the dollar thresholds for earning work credits, paying the payroll tax and working while collecting follow national wages. As a result, the same year can bring a bigger check and a higher bar for a worker to clear, and neither change depends on the other.

A single national adjustment fits almost no household exactly. "A national average reflects millions of individual price experiences; it seldom mirrors a particular consumer's experience," the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the agency that produces the price index, said. Readers should therefore weigh each 2027 figure against their own spending, earnings and birth year rather than against the headline number. Several changes don't involve a dollar figure at all. One set of rules reaches its end point, and another alters only the names the agency uses for claiming ages. For many people, the decisions with the most money at stake in 2027 concern when to claim benefits and whether to keep working, more than the size of the raise.

AARP set out to identify the key changes to Social Security taking effect in 2027. Its subjects range from the Medicare premium deducted from monthly checks to the earnings test for people who work while collecting and the new names for claiming ages. One percentage raise has to serve more than 71 million people on Social Security, according to AARP, whatever their budgets, jobs or ages. Taken together, the changes show that the raise is only the first number a household should look at. How much money a household actually keeps depends on the premiums, taxes, thresholds and claiming rules that apply alongside it. The useful question for each change is whom it reaches and what it asks them to decide. A higher threshold that barely affects one reader can change another reader's plans for work and retirement.

Social Security checks get a noticeably bigger raise in 2027 after a few years of smaller ones. Benefits rise 3.6 percent, the largest increase since 2023, which adds about $75 a month to the average check, according to AARP. Raises since 2000 have averaged about 2.6 percent, which makes 2027 an above-normal year rather than a routine one. An above-normal raise is not a windfall, though. Social Security ties the raise to prices, and a bigger raise mostly means that prices went up faster. For a retiree, the real question is whether those are the prices they actually pay.

The prices behind the raise belong to working households. The 3.6 percent comes from the CPI-W, a price index built around wage earners. Those wage-earner households make up about 30 percent of Americans, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and households of retirees are not counted among them. The people the raise is meant to protect are not the people whose prices set it. The 3.6 percent is best read as a record of what working households paid, not as proof that a retiree's budget has been made whole.

Health care is where that gap is most likely to hurt retirees. Medical care is only one of the major spending groups in the index, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. A retiree who spends heavily on doctors, prescriptions and premiums has a different budget from the household the index tracks. If medical prices go up faster than other prices, the people who spend the most on care are the first whose raise falls behind.

The raise reaches well beyond retirees. Social Security pays the same percentage increase on retirement, survivor, family, disability (SSDI) and Supplemental Security Income benefits, which covers disabled workers, widows and widowers, and low-income recipients alike. For retirement benefits, the increase first shows up in the December payment, which Social Security sends in January 2027.

Retirees should budget the raise from January and measure it against how their own costs changed over the year.

For Medicare enrollees who pay the standard Part B premium out of their Social Security check, the 2027 raise will be smaller once the premium comes out. Medicare is expected to charge $6.60 more each month for the standard premium, an increase of about 3 percent, according to Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services projections reported by AARP. Most enrollees pay that standard rate, so most will give part of their cost-of-living raise back to Medicare without doing anything. A few dollars a month looks small on a statement, but the higher premium adds up to an estimated $79.20 over the year, according to CNBC. The average retirement check is set to grow about $75 a month, and a retiree on the standard premium will lose some of that increase to Medicare before spending any of it. The number worth planning around is what remains after the premium, not the headline raise.

Some enrollees will get help with the higher premium for 2027, but many will not. CNBC reports that the White House has announced a one-time $90 payment. People who pay the income-related surcharge on top of the standard premium won't receive it, and neither will people enrolled in Medicare Advantage. For an enrollee who does qualify, the $90 is more than the extra premium costs over the year. The people who pay the highest premiums are therefore left out of both the standard projection and the relief. Enrollees should confirm that they are eligible before counting on the money. Even those who qualify will see no change to the premium itself, and the following year will bring the same higher deduction with no offset.

The $90 is a cushion for one year, and the budget that holds up is the raise minus the premium.

Most workers will see no difference on their pay stubs from the 2027 payroll-tax change, because the tax rate is the same and only the wage ceiling is higher. The most pay that can be taxed for Social Security is $192,300 in 2027, up from $184,500, while the rate is unchanged, according to AARP. Earnings above the ceiling are not taxed for Social Security, so only pay between those two amounts becomes newly taxable. A worker paid less than $184,500 already had every dollar of wages taxed at 6.2 percent, the rate in effect for 2026 according to the National Finance Center, and that worker owes in 2027 exactly what they owed before. For people paid above the old ceiling, an unchanged rate does not mean an unchanged bill.

High earners will pay more Social Security tax in 2027 because more of their pay falls under the tax. The ceiling applies only to the tax that funds Social Security, not to income tax or Medicare tax. The National Finance Center reports that the most an employee could owe in Social Security tax in 2026 was $11,439 and that Medicare tax had no wage limit that year. With the rate unchanged, the larger amount of taxable pay raises the Social Security maximum for 2027, while top earners were already paying Medicare tax on all of their wages. The higher ceiling changes one line on a high earner's pay stub and leaves the others as they were.

Retirees living on their savings pay none of this tax. Investment income, interest and withdrawals from retirement accounts are not subject to Social Security tax. The higher ceiling therefore matters only to people who still earn a paycheck.

For most workers, the 2027 tax change asks nothing. For the few paid above the old ceiling, it means a larger contribution to the system, even though no one changed the rate.

Deciding when to start Social Security sets the size of a retiree's monthly payments, and people make that choice using names for ages that are easy to misread. The problem is the vocabulary, not the benefit formula. Lawmakers wrote the Claiming Age Clarity Act to rename key claiming ages so people understand how the timing of a claim affects their payments, according to AARP. The renaming effort is tied to behavioral research from the Understanding America Study, which is funded by the National Institute on Aging, according to NIA. The bill rests on the idea that the labels themselves are part of what goes wrong when people decide when to claim. It changes no benefit amount and moves no age. Only the names change. It asks nothing of a household budget, only that people relearn the words for a choice they already face.

The new names are close to becoming law, and the last step is a signature. The House approved the bill on December 1, 2025, NIA records, and Congress passed it in October, with a signature expected in the coming weeks. Until the bill is signed, the new labels have no legal force. A person planning a claim shouldn't rebuild that plan around terms that may not yet be in use. Until the Social Security Administration changes the terms it uses, the current age names and a person's birth year remain the reliable guide.

The year 2027 completes a change to Social Security that Congress set in motion in 1983. Under that law, lawmakers raised the full retirement age in stages by birth year, and AARP reports that the phase-in is complete in 2027. For anyone with a birth year of 1960 or after, full benefits now begin at 67. People born in 1960 are the first to reach that age under the final rule, and they turn 67 in 2027. The target has stopped moving, and every claiming choice these workers make is now measured against that one age.

That fixed age attaches clear dollar amounts to both ends of the claiming window. For a person due $2,170 a month at 67, filing at 62 leaves about $1,519 a month, according to 24/7 Wall St. Waiting until 70 raises the same check to about $2,691. Holding off past 70 adds nothing more, so a worker has no reason to wait beyond that birthday. A person who files at 62 and a person who waits until 70 can start from the same work record and still collect very different checks each month.

With 62, 67 and 70 now fixed points, people can weigh the choice against their own health, savings and work plans instead of guessing where the rule will move next. Workers born in later years face the same three ages, so a plan made now will not need to be redrawn around a new benchmark.

The climb to 67 is over, and each worker now decides when, between 62 and 70, to file.

An early claimant who still draws a paycheck gives up some benefits once wages pass a yearly cap. For 2027, AARP reports that working early claimants lose $1 for every $2 they earn above $25,560, up from $24,480 in 2026. A worker who earns $40,000 in 2027 has $7,220 in benefits held back over the year, and a household living month to month would count that money as lost. It isn't. Social Security stops applying the test at full retirement age, according to CNBC, and the agency then raises the monthly payment to make up what it withheld. Early claimants who keep working receive part of their benefit later instead of losing it.

Getting that money back takes years. The agency repays it only through larger monthly checks after full retirement age, so a worker has to keep collecting for a long time to recover all of it. A worker who dies before collecting those larger checks for long may never get back what Social Security held. Some workers also give up more than they need to by cutting their own pay. Most workers don't know the money comes back, and they "perceive the test as a pure tax and subsequently reduce their earnings or stop working altogether," Rachel Greszler, senior research fellow at the Plymouth Institute for Free Enterprise, told CNBC. For a worker weighing an early claim, knowing about the repayment changes the decision, but it doesn't remove the risk of dying before the repayment is complete.

The test matters most to households that rely on both a paycheck and a benefit. CNBC reports that Johnny C. Taylor Jr., president and CEO of the Society for Human Resource Management, considers the test of no concern to people with high incomes. Social Security loosens the test in the year a worker reaches full retirement age. Until the month of that birthday, the limit is $67,920, and the agency deducts $1 for every $3 above it. Low-wage workers who file several years early carry the most risk. They face the lower limit and the steeper deduction, and they wait the longest to be repaid.

The 2027 limit may not last. Sen. Rick Scott and Rep. Greg Murphy are sponsoring the Senior Citizens' Freedom to Work Act, CNBC reports, and the bill would end the earnings test. The idea has support among older Americans: Dan Adcock, director of government relations and policy at the National Committee to Preserve Social Security and Medicare, says seniors he has met at town halls strongly favor repeal. Lawmakers who are considering repeal also face the state of the program's finances, with Social Security's combined reserves expected to be used up in 2034. That gives them a reason to hesitate, and the outcome remains open.

Until Congress acts, a working early claimant should plan around the 2027 limit, budget for the withholding and count on the repayment rather than on a repeal that may not pass.

In 2027, workers will need slightly more pay to earn the credits that make them eligible for Social Security. A worker must earn $1,970 for each credit in 2027, according to AARP. That is up from $1,890 in 2026, the Arizona Daily Star reports, and most people must collect 40 credits to become eligible for monthly benefits. Each credit a worker earns counts toward that requirement. This rule decides whether a person receives a check at all, not how large the check is.

A yearly limit on credits means most workers will never notice the higher amount. A worker can collect four credits at most in a single year, according to the Arizona Daily Star. In 2027, a worker reaches that maximum with $7,880 in earnings, and pay above that amount adds nothing toward eligibility. Qualifying therefore depends on how many years a person works above that mark, not on how much they earn in those years, and a decade of such years is enough for most workers. Someone whose pay is well above $7,880 every year still collects four credits in 2027, so the increase doesn't slow their progress toward 40.

The higher amount matters only to people whose earnings in a given year land close to the mark, such as low earners and people who work part of the year. A worker who earns less than $7,880 in 2027 gets fewer than four credits for that year and needs more years of work to reach 40. These workers should check their earnings record in any year when they cut back their hours or stop working for a time. In such a year, a small shortfall can mean qualifying later.

Most workers will never notice this change, but for some low earners it may mean qualifying for benefits later than they expected.
