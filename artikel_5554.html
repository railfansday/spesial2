Europeans are asking AI for the best price. They may not be getting it

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

AI now picks the products and prices many European shoppers weigh. Those buyers can still miss a better offer unless they search the stores themselves

Armin Weigel / picture alliance via Getty Images

The European Commission proposed a new law earlier this moth, the Digital Fairness Act, and promised that people in the E.U. "will have clear rights when using an AI agent to buy products on their behalf." The announcement didn't list any of the new rights.

Europeans need to know, because more than half of them have already used AI to help them shop, according to a survey by the consulting firm Deloitte. It took just 18 months for half of European shoppers to start using AI, compared with about five years for online shopping. Of the Europeans already shopping with AI, more now compare products by asking it than by browsing marketplaces, retailers' websites, or store shelves. Few let AI pay, though. For every person in the survey who would let AI complete a purchase, three to four would let it compare products and find prices. Most Europeans still take the short list an AI gives them, pick from it, and then go to a store or a retailer's website to buy.

The price on that short list isn't always the lowest one available. In August, the research firm Productrise compared Google $GOOGL's AI Mode with an ordinary Google search across more than two million product listings in the U.S. and the U.K. For about one in four products found by both AI Mode and an ordinary Google search, AI showed a higher price for the identical item that day, according to a summary of the study by the pricing-data company TGN Data. The study covered only the U.S. and the U.K., and none of the E.U.'s three main consumer laws in force, as the Commission describes them, requires a seller or its software to act in shoppers' interest. Whether Europeans see the cheapest offer depends on whether the AI works for them or for the company that built it.

Most products never reach the short list at all. Only about one in 80 items that ranked in an ordinary Google search also turned up in AI Mode when Productrise ran the identical query. AI Mode never showed its users the other 79, however little they cost. Google hasn't verified the study, and the company says people can click through its shopping results to compare sellers. Deloitte warns retailers that an AI tool will leave their goods off the lists it gives people if it can't read the product information.

Paul F. Accornero, a senior executive at the appliance maker De'Longhi Group, spent about three months watching AI agents choose among items in many categories and wrote in California Management Review in February that they favor products with claims they can check. In one example, an agent compared two brands of noise-cancelling headphones. The first brand listed its specifications in detail, including 30 hours of battery life and a certified rating for how much outside noise the headphones block. The second brand opened its listing with a slogan and lifestyle photos, the product of $2 million spent on photography and influencer deals, and gave few numbers. The agent picked the first brand. Accornero wrote that he couldn't tell whether the first brand's headphones were actually better. The agent picked them because it could verify what their maker said about them.

Most brands haven't adjusted. Across the brands Accornero has studied, 60% to 80% of the basic product details an agent reads are usually missing, or they differ from one site to the next. Over six months, he interviewed 30 executives, and more than half were still planning mainly for human shoppers. If agents keep favoring products they can check, the sellers on AI short lists will be the ones that fixed their product details, whether or not they charge the least.

The companies building AI shopping agents plan to make money from what people buy off these short lists. Meta $META launched its agent, Muse, in the U.S. and Canada in September, and more than 10 million people there had set it up by Oct. 21, CEO Mark Zuckerberg told investors. Shopping is what they use it for most. More than one in four people who open Muse in a given week buy something through it, Meta said on the call, and the average order tops $200.

Meta plans to keep Muse free for most people and make its money by taking a fee on the purchases the agent helps them make. Zuckerberg told investors that Meta would start earning money from Muse sooner than it usually does with a new product, in part because "such a large part of what people are doing is commerce." Once the fee is in place, Meta will earn more each time someone buys an item Muse found.

Mark Bartholomew, a University at Buffalo law professor who studies AI and the law, said in October that people won't be able to tell whether their agent is recommending the best deal. "If, behind the scenes, an AI platform prioritized products that it made itself or that it received a commission from retailers for, that would drain this new technology of its promise," he said. Bartholomew was describing a risk, not a case he had found.

Doubts about Meta haven't kept people off its apps before. Trust in Meta remains low, Kate Winick, a principal analyst at the research firm Forrester, told Business Insider, yet "consumers almost expect to have their data mismanaged in exchange for a high level of connectivity and utility, and have shown that they're willing to keep showing up as long as the product experience delivers."

None of the rights the Commission promised protect anyone yet. The Digital Fairness Act is still a proposal, and the Commission said the European Parliament and the Council, where the member states' governments sit, will now debate it. The Commission set no date for a vote or for the rules to take effect.Until the proposal becomes law, Europeans rely on the E.U.’s three main consumer laws: the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, the Consumer Rights Directive, and the Unfair Contract Terms Directive. The Commission's own 2024 review found gaps in how the three laws handle the tricks sellers use online.

Bartholomew wants a law to protect AI shoppers. In a paper in the William and Mary Law Review, Bartholomew and Samuel I. Becher, a law professor at the City University of Hong Kong, recommend that trusted outside auditors check shopping agents and certify whether they serve the people using them. "It's too hard for someone to figure out on their own whether an AI recommendation is truly trustworthy or not," Bartholomew said. The two professors also want every agent to come with a simple way to switch it off, so people can go back to researching products themselves whenever they choose.

Until the European Parliament and the Council pass the proposal and say what the rights they promised actually mean, Europeans have to trust that the price AI shows them is the best one. To find a cheaper offer, they would have to search the stores on their own and do the chore they had hoped to hand off.
