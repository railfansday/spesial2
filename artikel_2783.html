Where does WAS have a competitive advantage? Believe it or not, the rushing attack. The Bengals are a poor rushing team, averaging 85 yards per game (30th), while Washington averages 117 (13th). And, for all the gnashing of teeth about the state of the offensive line and the running back stable, Washington is in the top 3rd of the league in rushing yards per attempt (4.3, tied for 11th with the Colts and Jonathan Taylor), while the Bengals are 23rd with 3.9 yards/attempt. Moreover, as I showed in the previous post, rushing defense is the key weakness of the Bengals: they currently rank 20th in rush yards against but 11th on passing yards against. Why play into their hands by getting into a passing showdown when the rushing attack is there for the taking?

Remember that Washington abandoned its zone-heavy rushing attack last week in favor of duo (double team blocking on the IOL) and gap, the approaches used in the prior two years.  That kind of attack recognizes the limitation of Paul at LG and their Centers as zone players and takes advantage of what they do best.  (The other three linemen are zone versatile.)  Well, guess what?  Rachaad White is a star in duo and gap.

In zone, which Washington has emphasized most of the year, White's average yards per attempt is 3.93 and his success rate is 35% – far below average numbers in the NFL.  But when he plays duo, he averages an elite 5.27 YPA and has a success rate of 55%.  And when he plays gap, his YPA is an excellent 4.77 and his success rate is 54%. (Data in this paragraph are from Sumer Sports.). It looks like deemphasizing zone and focusing on duo and gap blocking not only fits the OL better, those schemes far better fit their leading rusher.

If I'm the OC, I plan on running White at least 18-20 times, almost all in gap and duo.  That could give him alone over 100 yards rushing.  Doing so will make the play action game work far more effectively, will protect Daniels, will keep the game close, and will set up the kind of passes that can win a close game.

Here's what I can tell you about the sort of suspension: The league determined Nacua violated the league's personal conduct policy, which is a broad policy that serves as a catch-all for what most Westerners would describe as bad behavior. You don't need a criminal conviction to be suspended, and the NFL doesn't have to specify what exact rules were broken when handing down a suspension.

But in a letter sent to Nacua this week, the contents of which were reviewed by The Athletic, the NFL said it found "that you bit the woman without invitation or consent." The "actual or threatened physical violence" against another person, along with "assault and/or battery," are violations of the policy.

Nacua has not been criminally charged for the incident but, according to the letter, "in lieu of filing criminal charges, L.A. city attorney's office referred the matter" to a diversion program. Nacua accepted those conditions and is currently completing the diversion program "to avoid the filing of criminal charges." Essentially, the league's finding of a violation is based largely on Nacua agreeing to be placed in that diversion program.

The alleged incident took place on New Year's Eve, and the league was made aware of it around March, according to a source. The NFL opened an investigation, which included reviewing civil pleadings, text messages and other relevant documents. The league interviewed Nacua in October, I'm told; such an interview is typically done at the end of an investigation.

On Nov. 8, Nacua injured his knee. The Rams knew he would miss time but did not know how long he would be out. It is not always cut and dried on how to proceed with such an injury — some warrant surgery while others heal on their own. Nacua sought multiple opinions, and several advised surgery.

I don't know exactly how many games the NFL wanted to suspend Nacua for, but it was more than three. That desire was communicated to the NFL Players Association and Nacua before the Rams placed him on injured reserve. When the NFL interviewed Nacua in October, it could not have predicted he would go on injured reserve in November. And some within the Rams organization believed last week, before Nacua had surgery, that the suspension was imminent.

Clearly, the timing works in Nacua's favor, even if he misses the rest of the season and perhaps the postseason. This suspension, like many others over the years, was reached via settlement among the league, the NFLPA, Nacua and his representation. Nacua accepted the league's three-game punishment — which could be more accurately described as a three-game check forfeiture — and the league walked away with what it considers a precedent-setting victory, even if it's not viewed that way on the outside.

"Puka would have fought harder had he not been hurt," one high-ranking official with a team said. "IR gave everybody an out."

The league will receive blowback from what seems like a flimsy suspension, but it also sets an important precedent for future cases of bad behavior — especially regarding allegations from women who are not domestic partners — that do not involve criminal charges.

Nacua clearly recognized that he would eventually be suspended, and he took the opportunity to serve it while on the mend. If he kept fighting, it could have dragged into the latter part of the season when the Rams try to make the Super Bowl. If he successfully fought to delay the punishment until next year, it would likely come after either a franchise tag or mega extension, when the financial hit would be far more painful.
