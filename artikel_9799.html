Mumbai Police QR-code ID cards: how to verify an officer and spot impostors

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Posted on Nov 23 • Originally published at igrs.xyz

Mumbai Police are issuing metal ID cards with a QR code that citizens can scan to check an officer's identity. Here is how the check works, where it falls short, and what to do when someone claiming to be police asks you for money.

On Saturday, 21 November 2026, Maharashtra Chief Minister Devendra Fadnavis inaugurated a set of technology projects for Mumbai Police. The one most relevant to ordinary citizens is a new identity card for police personnel that anyone can verify with a phone.

According to The420 and the Free Press Journal, the new card is metal-based, carries an embossed photograph and a unique QR code, and covers all 51,308 sanctioned posts in the Mumbai Police. The420 and NewsBytes also report an NFC chip, which The420 says authorised staff use to enter restricted police premises. The Maharashtra government sanctioned about Rs 4 crore for the cards in February 2026, both outlets report.

A few cards, including some for traffic police, were handed over at the launch. A police official told reporters that printing is complete and every officer should receive a card within a month. The420 adds that carrying the card will be mandatory on official duty, in uniform or plain clothes, including home visits, questioning and investigations. That last detail appears in only one report, so treat it as reported rather than confirmed.

The same event launched the Mumbai Police Citizen App, around 50,000 Android tablets for investigators, the Mumbai Police Information System (MPIS) for personnel and administrative records, and an Anti-Narcotics Cell investigation van, as reported by Asianet Newsable (via ANI) and The Live Nagpur. Commissioner Deven Bharti said several of these projects began in 2017, stalled, and were revived in 2022.

Today's India Cyber Brief carried this story in one paragraph. This piece looks at the scam the card is meant to counter, what the card can and cannot prove, and what to do when someone claiming to be police contacts you.

Free Press Journal reports the problem this targets: people posing as plain-clothes officers who approach senior citizens on morning walks and businessmen, sometimes to extort money. When the cards were first approved in May 2025, the Maharashtra Home Department noted that the old printed cards could be copied with basic desktop publishing software, and cited counterfeit IDs used in "digital arrest" frauds.

Police impersonation sits at the centre of two common frauds in India.

A man in plain clothes, sometimes with an accomplice, stops a victim on the street or comes to the door. He flashes a card, claims a "checking" or a pending case, and asks for cash, jewellery or a phone to "hold as evidence". A printed card with a crest and a photo is often enough because few people know what a genuine card looks like.

Here the "officer" never appears in person. A call claims the victim's Aadhaar, SIM or a parcel is linked to a crime. The caller moves to a WhatsApp or Skype video call, sometimes from a set made to look like a police station, shows a fake ID card or warrant, and keeps the victim on camera for hours or days while demanding transfers to "verify" funds. Today's brief alone reported a Kolkata case with Rs 1.78 crore lost over 42 days and a Visakhapatnam case of Rs 1.94 crore.

The Enforcement Directorate faced the same problem with forged summons. In its press release of 8 October 2025, ED introduced system-generated summons with a QR code and a unique passcode that can be checked on its website, and stated that there is no "digital arrest" or "online arrest" under the PMLA; arrests are made physically under due process.

Analysis: The card is a real improvement for face-to-face encounters. A forger can copy the look of a printed card, but a scan that shows a different face, name or posting exposes the impostor at once. Three limits are worth knowing.

Analysis: The card helps answer "is this person a Mumbai police officer?" It does not answer "is what this person asking me to do lawful?" Demands for cash, gold, phone handovers or bank transfers are red flags whatever ID is shown.

Analysis: Three things will decide how much the card helps: whether all personnel actually receive and carry it within the promised month; whether Mumbai Police publicise exactly how a genuine scan looks inside the app, so citizens can spot a fake page; and whether other police forces adopt a similar check. Until then, the old rule still holds: a real officer will not settle a case over a phone call, and will not ask you for money.
