SBA chief defends policy barring green-card holders from loans, even if they can serve in U.S. military

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Small Business Administration chief Kelly Loeffler said Tuesday it’s “completely not true” that her agency’s new loan rules restrict immigrants. The rules she was defending bar green-card holders, who can legally work, pay taxes and, if otherwise eligible, enlist in the U.S. military, from SBA-backed loans.

Under a policy that took effect March 1, businesses must be wholly owned by U.S. citizens or nationals to qualify for SBA-backed loans, cutting out lawful permanent residents, commonly known as green-card holders, who were previously eligible. In fiscal 2025, the agency approved 3,358 loans to businesses partly owned by lawful permanent residents, about 4% of its 85,000 loan approvals, according to the SBA. Speaking at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit, Loeffler cast the rule as a question of who goes first: “Foreign nationals cannot get an SBA loan ahead of an American citizen.”

“It strikes me as odd that we can send somebody to a battlefield on behalf of the United States,” Fortune Brainstorm Editorial Director Andrew Nusca told Loeffler. “We can take their taxes, but we can’t extend to them a loan.”

“We have not yet run into a case where we’ve had to reject a veteran for that purpose,” Loeffler said. She didn’t directly address why green-card holders who serve in the military should be excluded. Instead, she pointed to the agency’s work supporting veterans, including a policy waiving down payments for eligible veteran entrepreneurs.

Loeffler also argued that the SBA has limited funding and accounts for only a small share of the broader small-business lending market. Those excluded from its loan guarantees can still turn to banks, credit unions, venture capital firms and private lenders for financing, she said.

The SBA generally doesn’t lend directly to businesses. Instead, it guarantees loans made by private lenders, helping eligible businesses obtain financing that might otherwise be difficult to secure.

America has roughly 36 million small businesses across America, but the country’s entrepreneurial success has long depended on immigrants. Nearly 46% of companies on the 2026 Fortune 500, or 229 businesses, were founded by immigrants or their children, according to the American Immigration Council. Together, those companies generated $9.8 trillion in revenue last year and employed 15.3 million people worldwide.

Nusca pointed to some of the country’s best-known business leaders, including Tesla’s Elon Musk, Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, AMD’s Lisa Su, and DoorDash’s Tony Xu, as examples of immigrant entrepreneurs who helped build major American companies.

The immigration restrictions are among several changes Loeffler has made to the agency, which was established in 1953. Since taking office, she has reduced its workforce by more than half and announced plans to relocate field offices away from cities the administration designates as sanctuary jurisdictions.

Loeffler said the overhaul has made the agency more effective, citing $100 billion in capital backed over two years and efforts to move staff closer to growing small-business communities.

“Small business is big business,” she said.

She also defended changes to the agency’s 8(a) Business Development Program, which supports socially and economically disadvantaged small businesses seeking federal contracts. Loeffler said the administration was tightening eligibility to ensure contracts reach businesses that qualify under the program’s rules.

Despite the agency’s shrinking workforce, she said about half of its loans last year went to businesses with fewer than 10 employees.

Loeffler also rejected the idea that artificial intelligence would necessarily displace small-business employees, arguing that labor shortages make automation increasingly valuable.

“This is a productivity tool. It’s not a job replacement tool,” she said.

She pointed to manufacturers adopting robotics and automated equipment to handle jobs they struggle to fill, freeing existing employees to take on more skilled work.

“We have prioritized lending to Americans because we have limited statutory availability of funding to go out to American citizens,” Loeffler said.

Tatiana Sataua is a News Fellow at Fortune covering business, technology, AI, and consumer culture.
