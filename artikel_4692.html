Tech Outage Anxiety

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

This week, millions of Americans experienced the direct effects of a series of major technological outages. Banking, flights, email, the app store, and parts of the government were all disrupted.

While most people were annoyed, some experienced these outages as existential threats. The outages did not feel temporary or safe. Even after the platforms were back up and running, some people were left with a sense of dread.

When we have a hyper-reliance on technology, temporary shutdowns of platforms can makes us feel vulnerable.

The series of outage events this week may have been coincidences, but the anxious brain is unlikely to interpret them as such.

Strong reactions to the outages were most likely amplified by these three common cognitive biases.

On a broader level, people had already been experiencing other forms of technology anxiety.

People with anxiety are hypervigilant to danger, perceived or real. Technology and the ways it could harm us can represent a scary new battleground.

We were also poised to find these outages troubling and stressful because we are already trying to integrate the enormous technological advances of the past few years with artificial intelligence. People are unsettled by the uncertainty of how this technology will change our lives and our livelihoods.

Every new technological advancement brings with it fear about the unknown, but AI perhaps more so than most.

For most laypeople, this advancement has an unsettling sci-fi feel. Many of us feel helpless in the face of the technology’s shocking, exponential growth.

Mental health clinicians must learn to recognize these new subtypes of technology-related anxiety. As a society, we must help each other cope with rapid change, temporary loss of access to technology, and any real danger therein.
