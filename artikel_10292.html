Screwed up at work? Here’s how to learn from your mistake without overcorrecting

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Part of what separates great leaders and employees from merely good ones is the ability to learn effectively from mistakes. Often, we talk about learning from mistakes as if most people don’t. But while we can always point to a few people who seem blissfully unchanged by their errors, a more dangerous trend is the continuous attempt to fight the last war—which ultimately leads to ineffective learning.

That is, we focus too hard on the most recent failure. Our biggest concern becomes avoiding the mistake or bad outcome that happened last. As a result, we forget the many other lessons we have learned. That tendency to make significant changes based on the most recent mistake can lead our behavior to veer wildly and lead to other mistakes down the line.

Here are three things you can do to avoid changing too much as the result of an error.

1. Focus on the smallest fix posible

When you make a mistake at work, and particularly when it’s a big one, it tends to dominate your thinking. The consequences may still be felt across the organization, and you’re embarrassed. You may even imagine that everyone you see is thinking about what you did wrong.

Because this recent error is at the forefront of your mind, you are likely to emphasize avoiding that same mistake in the next thing you work on. Unfortunately, when you do that, this error is going to be overrepresented in the planning you do for whatever project you’re working on next. You may become so focused on that problem that you miss other potential difficulties. You may even change routines that have served you well in the past just to avoid the same mistake again.

Instead, when you develop an idea about how to correct your mistake, think intentionally about the smallest thing you can change. If you purposefully underemphasize the change you want to make, that will provide a counterweight to the attention you’re paying to that recent mistake. It will help dampen the tendency to overreact.

2. Think about what works

If you’re a reasonably conscientious person, you probably don’t make mistakes all the time and, in fact, are probably successful at most of what you do. However, it’s likely you find the errors you do make to be embarrassing and unacceptable.

You certainly don’t want to make the same mistake twice. But instituting a correction needs to be done in the context of the many things that you do well.

It is certainly important to do some kind of after-action report to determine what went wrong that led to your mistake. But after you figure out what went wrong, make sure that you also think about what goes right most of the time. Understand how your current way of working leads to good outcomes most of the time.

The aim is to ensure that when you make a correction, the changes you make preserve as much of what typically leads to good outcomes as possible and focus just on those things that are most likely to lead to the mistake you made.

3. Track your learning

We live in a limited window of the present. That is, it is hard to remember all of the things that you do that have become habits and how those influence the effectiveness of your work. You might be able to remember your work style from a few weeks ago, but the way you did your work last year or the year before that may feel like a mystery. It can be even harder to remember what aspects of work you struggled with.

It can be helpful to keep a diary of the way you work, your struggles, and the biggest mistake you can remember from a given time period. Every three months, put a half hour on your calendar to update your journal. Write about the three key projects you’re doing at that moment. Think about the changes you have made to your work routines over the past few months. Write about what aspects of work are going well and which feel like a struggle.

When you make a mistake at work, go back over that journal and read about the evolution of your work over time. Reading your journal has several benefits when you’re dealing with a new mistake you have just made. It reminds you of ways you have struggled in the past that are no longer a problem. It may remind you of changes you made in the past that you are no longer keeping up and should return to. It can also give you a sense of the many ways you have improved your workflow over the years.

All of these elements can be helpful when correcting a mistake. When you are no longer struggling with things that caused you fits in the past, it helps you to be aware that the habits you developed are largely successful. You may also realize that your current mistake reflects that past changes have fallen by the wayside and need to be reinstated. Recognizing the many ways you have adapted your work over the years can also help you to avoid undoing past changes in the zeal to avoid a current mistake. All of these can help you to right-size the correction you are making now.
