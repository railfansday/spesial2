Rocket Lab Hit 100 Launches Faster Than Any Other Commercially Developed Rocket. I'd Buy a Small Stake.

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The small rocket has proven it can fly often. Does that tell investors anything about the big one?

Rocket Lab (RKLB -6.76%) launched its 100th Electron rocket on Saturday, Nov. 14, sending a radar-imaging satellite to orbit for Japanese customer iQPS from its launch site in New Zealand.

Rocket Lab says no other commercially developed rocket has reached 100 launches faster. Electron first flew in 2017, so the company did it in under a decade.

The growth stock hasn't exactly celebrated. Shares trade near $59 as I write, off about 4% on Monday and about 60% under their May 27 record close of $150.23.

After the company's third-quarter report last week, I wrote that the stock was worth a small position, with any case for adding resting on Neutron, the far bigger rocket Rocket Lab is still developing.

I don't think the 100th launch changes that sizing. But it does make Electron's part of the business easier to count on.

Electron's launch rate keeps rising. Rocket Lab flew 10 Electrons in 2023, 16 in 2024, and 21 in 2025. It's already flown 21 in 2026, with around six weeks of the year left.

And the busier schedule is showing up in the numbers. Through September, Rocket Lab's launch services revenue hit around $161 million for the year, up 31% from the same stretch of 2025, as completed Electron missions rose to 18 from 14. Average revenue per launch climbed to $8.7 million, versus $8.2 million in the first nine months of last year. What's more, average cost per launch dipped to $4.9 million, down from $5.5 million, which management credits mostly to efficiencies of scale. That means Rocket Lab made about $3.8 million a launch above the cost of flying it, up from roughly $2.7 million.

Sure, revenue per launch shifts with the customer mix. In the third quarter alone, revenue per launch dropped to $7.8 million, down from $10.1 million a year earlier.

The reliability record has firmed up as well. Every one of Electron's 21 launches this year has succeeded, putting the rocket at 96 successes in 100 launches.

"The next 100 Electrons are already booked so we're only accelerating from here," said CEO Sir Peter Beck in Rocket Lab's announcement of the milestone.

Rocket Lab's third-quarter report put its total launch backlog at a record 117 launches covering Electron, HASTE (a suborbital version of Electron used in hypersonic tests), and Neutron, with 16 Neutron missions.

At this year's rate, the Electron and HASTE launches in the backlog work out to over four years of flying.

I think this is what 100 launches prove. Rocket Lab can make and fly a small rocket on a predictable schedule, and customers are willing to book years of these flights in advance.

But 100 Electron launches can't tell investors much about Neutron. Electron can carry up to 300 kilograms to low Earth orbit. Neutron is designed to carry around 13,000 kilograms in its reusable version.

Rocket Lab also raised Neutron's base price to $70 million for several recent missions, versus Electron's average of $8.7 million a launch through September. Neutron is a much bigger rocket, and probably a much bigger business, than anything Electron has proven.

And it's behind schedule. In October, Rocket Lab moved Neutron's first launch to 2027.

That matters for profits, too. Rocket Lab's non-GAAP (adjusted) earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA) are still negative. Chief financial officer Adam Spice told analysts on the company's last earnings call that adjusted EBITDA should go positive within around six months of Neutron's first successful test flight.

Also worth noting, launch is still the smaller piece of Rocket Lab's business. Launch services brought in around $53 million of the company's record $268 million in third-quarter revenue, or about 20%. The rest came from space systems, which makes satellites and spacecraft parts.

Meanwhile, investors are paying a lot for what's next. Including preferred shares that convert to common stock, Rocket Lab's market value is near $40 billion. Without positive earnings, a price-to-earnings ratio isn't much use.

Divide that market value by the last four quarters of revenue instead, and the stock trades at over 45 times sales. That valuation arguably needs Neutron to fly -- and then to fly often.

Not yet. Electron's record makes Rocket Lab's launch business easier to count on, and I think it supports buying a small position at about $59. But the larger bet is on a rocket that hasn't flown, so I'd wait for Neutron's first successful flight before adding to it.
