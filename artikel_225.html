Latest botched execution prompts calls for US moratorium on lethal injections

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Reinaldo Dennes screamed in pain and took over an hour to die – but Texas officials branded the execution a success

A botched execution in Texas on Thursday night that lasted more than an hour and caused the prisoner to scream out, begging the Lord to end his suffering, has led to renewed calls for a nationwide moratorium that would pause all lethal injections.

It took 63 minutes for Reinaldo Dennes, 70, to be proclaimed dead in the Huntsville penitentiary. The execution team had struggled to find a vein through which a lethal dose of the sedative pentobarbital could be administered.

Descriptions of the botched lethal injection bear chilling similarities to the recent failed execution in Tennessee of Christa Pike who in September became the only person ever to survive a full dose of execution drugs. Both Dennes and Pike were subjected to lethal injection protocols using pentobarbital, in both cases staff struggled to establish intravenous lines, and in both the prisoner expressed experiencing excruciating pain and burning sensations.

“The world saw what happened to Christa Pike, and now to Mr Dennes,” said Robin Maher, executive director of the authoritative Death Penalty Information Center. “The question now is, how many more of these botched executions will be tolerated by the American people?”

A harrowing eyewitness account of the night’s events was given by the prisoner’s spiritual adviser, the Rev Jeff Hood, who stood beside the gurney with his hand on Dennes’s shoulder. Hood describes how for up to five minutes Dennes fought against the pain.

“The pain!” Dennes shouted, according to Hood’s account.

“My leg, my leg, my leg,” he screamed, adding that his leg was “on fire”.

Hood writes in his account that Dennes “shifted on the gurney, back and forth, back and forth. He contorted. He grabbed hold of the leather straps and pulled against them until his body lifted. His shoulder drove up into my palm. Then it fell away. Then it drove up again. Twice he screamed, ‘It’s not working like it’s supposed to. Fuck. Fuck. Fuck. What is going on? Fuck. Fuck. Fuck.’”

Throughout the ordeal, Hood reports, the prisoner continued to pray, begging for an end to it.

“Take me home, Father. I don’t think this is normal. Take me, Father. Don’t let me suffer like this. C’mon.”

Dennes was convicted of capital murder in the 1996 death of Janos Szucs, a diamond dealer who was shot and killed in Houston.

Maya Foa, chief executive of the human rights group Reprieve, called for a moratorium on lethal injections. She said: “Lethal injection is torture – and Reinaldo Dennes was not sentenced to be tortured. Even the most ardent supporters of capital punishment must recognise that the dominant method of execution in the US is broken and cannot be allowed to continue.”

The Texas department of criminal justice insisted that the execution went fully according to plan. Its spokeswoman, Amanda Hernandez, told the New York Times that “as far as this agency is concerned, it is 100 percent not a botched execution. This was a successful execution, and we did follow all of the proper protocols to do it correctly.”

But such a blanket dismissal of any concerns failed to dampen a chorus of criticism. “Americans are now seeing the harsh reality of the death penalty, and I just don’t know how much longer state officials think they can double down on a story that is false,” Maher said.

Texas has been under the spotlight for many years for the way it obtains and stores its stocks of lethal execution drugs, a manoeuvre which it is careful to shroud in secrecy in an attempt to avoid public scrutiny. In the wake of an international boycott, the state’s prison service has taken to buying pentobarbital from compounding pharmacies that make up the chemicals to order.

Lawyers for condemned prisoners have long argued that the supplies can be of variable quality and are often held by the state beyond their expiration dates, running the risk of causing excruciating deaths.

The way that Texas conducts executions is likely to come under fresh review given the political upheaval that the state has experienced in the midterm elections. Not only did the governor’s mansion fall to the Democrat Gina Hinojosa, but Democrats flipped 20 state House seats as well as taking all seats up for election in the Texas supreme court and court of criminal appeals that have enormous power over capital cases.

Jonathan Groner, an expert on execution methods and emeritus professor of clinical surgery at Ohio State University, said that the Dennes execution illustrated why lethal injections were a “medical charade”. He said: “They imitate general anesthesia which is done in hospitals across the country thousands of times a day, by people with expertise, but when it is done in the death chamber without necessary equipment or skills, people suffer.”

Groner said that the location of the two IV lines placed in Dennes’s body – in a foot and in his shoulder – indicated that the execution team was struggling. “Placing IVs in the neck is very painful and difficult, and a last resort; and the foot is not a top choice because there is a long way to the heart for the medication to be circulated.”

He added: “It’s time for the medical profession to make a stand on executions. People are being tortured. We need to stop, and take a hard look at why we are still doing this.”
