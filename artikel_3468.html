Prime Video's 32-Episode Thriller Series Is So Good, It Invented A New Genre

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

One Prime Video action thriller series was such a massive streaming success, the show managed to spawn an entire new genre in the wake of its popularity. There are shows that are big, there are shows that are massive, and then there are the rare shows that manage to either revive an existing dormant genre or spell the start for an entire new genre.

While there were rare cult curios like Carnivale and True Blood that blended R-rated content and fantasy plot elements before the 2011 series, HBO’s George RR Martin adaptation Game of Thrones single-handedly proved that R-rated mature epic fantasy shows could work. The show’s budget was unprecedented in TV history, and its massive mainstream popularity spawned the spinoff House of the Dragon and A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms, but also helped usher Netflix’s The Witcher and The Sandman, Prime Video’s The Wheel of Time, and SyFy’s The Magicians into existence.

Similarly, Prime Video’s Reacher spawned an entire subgenre thanks to the success of the historically huge Lee Child adaptation itself and its spinoff, Neagley. Although Reacher and Neagley are as massive as Alan Ritchson’s hulking title character himself, the show’s impact spans far beyond just its popularity. Debuting in 2022, Reacher effectively helped midwife Dad TV into mainstream ubiquity, even though the genre had its forebears in earlier decades.

With its clear, straightforward plotting, protagonists who are typically unstoppable and unflappable, blatantly evil and thus comfortingly simplistic bad guys, and fantasies of macho heroism, “Dad TV” is a pretty recognizable phenomenon. Prime Video’s Reacher remains the greatest example of the trend, as the series follows a drifter who travels from city to city dishing out violent vigilante justice against reliably cartoonish villains.

Reacher’s military background justifies his absurd strength, hyper competence, and fearlessness, while the comically evil depiction of Reacher’s villains makes it easy to cheer on their eventual demises. After decades of shows like Homicide: Life on the Streets, NYPD Blue, and, eventually, The Wire and The Shield offering progressively more morally complex and dramatically challenging takes on small-screen policing, Reacher is a return to the simple joys of Neo-Western heroics.

Even more than actual Neo-Western hits like Yellowstone, Reacher offers viewers an unambiguously heroic good guy to root for and equally unambiguously terrible villains to boo. This, combined with fast-paced action and simple premise, proved so effective and popular that stories of tactical experts using lone wolf skills to take down elaborate evil criminal conspiracies soon cropped up in the streaming hits The Terminal List, The Night Agent, Lioness, and Cross.

Before long, even traditional network TV was getting in on the action. For viewers who couldn’t wait for Reacher’s next adventure, CBS offered Justin Hartley’s survivalist Colter Shaw in Tracker, based on the 2019 Jeffrey Deaver novel The Never Game. Tracker soon became a massive hit for the network, and now the Yellowstone spinoff Marshals has dropped family saga storytelling of earlier franchise instalments in favour of a focus on law enforcement.

The success of shows like Tracker, The Night Agent, and Neagley proves Reacher’s popularity is more than a one-off for Prime Video. Instead, it is clear that the Dad TV formula can be applied to various streaming services and networks with some serious success. Although Prime Video’s high-profile Patricia Cornwell adaptation Scarpetta proves that not all these efforts work, a pattern is emerging.

Tracker, The Night Agent, Cross, and, of course, Reacher itself are all based on bestselling books. These novels are proven crowd-pleasers, and their adaptations play up the black and white storytelling to offer an antidote to the morally murky, complex plotting of many streaming hits. As a result, Reacher’s streaming fad has grown into a full-blown pop culture phenomenon.
