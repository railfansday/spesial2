Netflix's 8-Part Horror Thriller Is the Perfect Binge for 'Haunting of Hill House' Fans

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Amanda M. Castro is a Network TV writer at Collider and a New York–based journalist whose work has appeared in Newsweek, where she is an Associate Editor, and The U.S. Sun, where she previously served as a Senior Consumer Reporter.

She specializes in network television coverage, delivering sharp, thoughtful analysis of long-running procedural hits and ambitious new dramas across broadcast TV. At Collider, Amanda explores character arcs, storytelling trends, and the cultural impact of network series that keep audiences tuning in week after week.

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, Amanda is bilingual and holds a degree in Communication, Film, and Media Studies from the University of New Haven.

Few things are worse than meeting your intended partner's family for the first time. Add a remote mansion in the woods, a set of extremely questionable family traditions, and enough red flags to design a whole wedding reception, and elopement suddenly turns into a perfect idea. Rachel Harkin is in that exact situation when she meets her fiancé's relatives in Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen, an eight-part horror miniseries that turns wedding worries into something truly nightmarish.

In addition to being created by Haley Z. Boston, who has also worked on series like Brand New Cherry Flavor and Guillermo del Toro's Cabinet of Curiosities, it is executive produced by the Duffer Brothers – the creators of Stranger Things. Don't expect another monster-of-the-week show, though; this is a completely different form of horror. It's not based on being afraid of something that comes out of the woods to frighten you; instead, the horror comes from fears about family secrets, trauma from the past, and even the possibility that your loved one might not be the ideal person for you to be with forever.

Rachel (Camila Morrone) and her fiancé, Nicky Cunningham (Adam DiMarco), expect a relatively straightforward wedding week. They've traveled to the secluded Cunningham family home in the snowy woods of Upstate New York, where they'll exchange vows in an intimate ceremony surrounded by Nicky's relatives. One problem, however, is that Rachel has a terrible feeling about it all. Her instincts aren't exactly helped by the circumstances surrounding their journey. Strange encounters, disturbing discoveries, and increasingly ominous warnings make the trip feel like the opening act of an unfortunate true-crime documentary. Things don't improve when Rachel meets the Cunninghams; Nicky's family is wealthy, eccentric, and distinctly unwelcoming, with everyone seeming to know something she doesn't.

The family features Nicky's mom, Victoria (played by Jennifer Jason Leigh), with a truly weird personality that seems hard to understand; Nicky's dad, Boris (Ted Levine), a doctor with a weird fascination for taxidermy; and Nicky's two brothers, Portia (Gus Birney), and Jules (Jeff Wilbusch). Portia's wife, Nell (Karla Crome), is also Nicky's former girlfriend. As Rachel spends more time with the family, their strange behavior becomes increasingly difficult to dismiss. Old stories, unsettling traditions, and mysterious warnings suggest something much bigger is happening. And with only five days until the wedding, Rachel has precious little time to figure out what's going on.

A horror story can only be as credible as the person the audience is following, and Morrone provides the series with a wonderful centerpiece performance. Rachel is observant, sarcastic, and much smarter than her companions realize early on. She certainly doesn't make the same mistakes everyone else does; she realizes something isn't quite right at the start. Unfortunately, knowing something is wrong isn't always the same as knowing how to escape it.

Morrone makes Rachel a compelling combination of vulnerability and stubbornness, so her increasingly desperate actions, even if difficult to witness, seem justified. There's also a lot of enjoyable personality beneath her worries, especially because she doesn't just respond as a victim of another Cunningham family issue. Meanwhile, DiMarco gives us an interesting contrast as Nicky, who looks very nice but is clueless about the severity of Rachel's fears. Their relationship has layers more complex than a simple horror-movie setup where a clueless guy is in a relationship with a suspicious girlfriend. The series also asks viewers what makes a relationship work, and whether loving someone is enough.

Leigh, Levine, Birney, and Wilbusch also make the Cunningham family an entertainingly unpredictable bunch. Birney deserves particular attention for her portrayal of Portia, whose enthusiasm and increasingly erratic behavior make her one of the show's more memorable characters.

The series' isolated setting works so well for a reason. The Cunningham estate feels like a place where visitors should probably keep their shoes on and their car keys close. Snow-covered landscapes, shadowy interiors, peculiar family portraits, and unsettling discoveries all contribute to the sense that Rachel has wandered somewhere she doesn't belong.

The directing team, led by Weronika Tofilska, does an excellent job establishing that unease. The cinematography, production design, and soundscape work together to make even relatively quiet scenes feel threatening. The show also knows when to lean into more traditional horror techniques, using jump scares and carefully constructed sequences to keep viewers guessing.

The pacing requires some patience. The first part builds the mystery around Rachel and the Cunninghams before the full scope of the mythology emerges. The slower shift also lets the series move beyond the clichéd nightmare-in-laws narrative and explore other areas. In the second half, the storyline focuses primarily on Rachel and Nicky, exploring issues like marriage, trust, and expectations in romance.

Ultimately, Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen is at its most compelling when it brings those two sides together. There's plenty of gruesome horror, but the show's most uncomfortable questions are surprisingly recognizable. How well can you really know another person? What happens when the future you've planned suddenly feels wrong? And how much should you trust that little voice telling you to run?
