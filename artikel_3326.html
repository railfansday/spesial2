Everything Is Bigger, Bloodier & Darker In The Hunting Wives Season 2 | Review

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Margo and Sophie's love story is as pulpy as ever, but The Hunting Wives season 2 is a darker chapter of the unapologetically spicy erotic thriller.

When The Hunting Wives first exploded onto the scene in the summer of 2025, eyebrows were raised and pearls were clutched. The erotic thriller about a Boston wife and mother with a dark past and an East Texas society queen bee with an even darker one falling under each other's spell as the bodies and the twists piled up around them was as much about unbridled female desire as it was about murder and secrets, with no apologies or f***s given.

Naturally, such a series had its detractors, who brandished pejoratives like "trash" and "insane" like pitchforks. Well, those in that camp, who detest joy The Hunting Wives season 1, will find absolutely nothing redeemable about season 2.

But those who reveled in the debauched antics of Sophie O'Neil (Brittany Snow), Margo Banks (Malin Åkerman), and the sinful town of Maple Brook will get an absolute kick out of The Hunting Wives' second installment. While season 1 adapts May Cobb's 2021 novel of the same name, The Hunting Wives season 2 is an original story that creator Rebecca Cutter promises to be "another sexy, twisted, bats**t crazy ride through Maple Brook" (via Netflix). She wholeheartedly delivers.

In the Hunting Wives season 1 finale, Sophie learned the truth about Margo killing Abby Jackson (Madison Wolfe) and confronted her about it, only to end up killing Kyle (Michael Aaron Milligan) after he threatened her. Now in season 2, Margo is in a lonely place, frozen out not only by Sophie but also by her husband Jed (Dermot Mulroney), at least outside the appearances they keep up for his governor campaign. However, when a gang of bloodthirsty bikers from Margo's Mandy Burkett past comes looking for Kyle, Margo and Sophie are forced back together with plenty of unfinished business — in and out of the bedroom.

As Callie (Jaime Ray Newman) begins filming her reality show and the body of another teenage girl is found, it's another sordid, sinful chapter in Maple Brook. But calling The Hunting Wives season 2 a guilty pleasure is a mistake. There ain't nothing guilty about it. It's a bold-as-brass, unadulterated, shameless pleasure — even when it goes to some very dark places.

The Hunting Wives season 2 had its work cut out for it, having bumped off a not-small portion of the cast in season 1. However, as much as these casualties are missed, their absence frees up room for the surviving characters to be even more fleshed out, while the new additions add more shades of color to the already vibrant show.

Newman had an absolute ball playing Callie Baskin as Margo's "cuckoo for Cocoa Puffs" spurned ex-lover who's dripping with mean-girl envy over her bestie's relationship with the shiny new Sophie in season 1, and she has even more to do in season 2. Callie was someone who was happy playing second fiddle as long as Margo's light shone on her, but now that she's the star of Married Justice, Callie basks in not only the glamorous perks of being top banana, but also allows herself to question what she truly wants and entertain the idea of going after it.

This was a theme of the show previously reserved only for Margo and Sophie, and by exploring it with Callie — especially in contrast to the other two women — The Hunting Wives is all the better for it, even if it comes at the cost of less time with the rest of the titular group. Callie's husband, Sheriff Jonny Baskin (a fantastic Branton Box), also gets a storyline upgrade.

After spending most of season 1 as an incompetent cop cozy in Jed's pocket, Jonny had one of the show's most intriguing, spicy bedroom scenes, making me realize The Hunting Wives ​​​​​​had been sleeping on this character. Thankfully, the writers rectify this in season 2, as we get to see more of Callie and Jonny's marriage — arguably the sweetest in the entire series — and the latter begins to grow a backbone.

Perhaps the biggest treat of the season comes with Karen Rodriguez and Hunter Emery being promoted to series regulars. In shows about terrible people (or at least, complicated people with a serial commitment to doing terrible things), genuine empathy can be, quite frankly, a buzzkill, but Wanda Salazar and Walter Flynn are perhaps the most entertaining moral anchors in television history with their combination of lived-in chemistry, humor, and warmth.

In particular, Wanda's compassion and desire for justice help quell any icky feelings about The Hunting Wives introducing yet another dead girl found in the woods. Honestly, as we see throughout the investigation, Wanda probably cares more than she should given the demands of her profession, but she more than makes up for it with sterling detective work that screams spinoff potential.

It's a testament to Cutter and The Hunting Wives' writing room that even though season 2 introduced approximately 10,000 new characters, I could not only keep track of them all but enjoyed seeing most of them whenever they popped up. I could have done without the cheesy electric guitar music to announce the bikers' incoming presence, but any cringe was quickly replaced with delight as soon as Dale Dickey (who, between this and Widow's Bay, is having a deserved banner year) appeared onscreen as gang leader Zelda Moffitt, more interesting in flashback than she is in the present.

Alex FitzAlan also deserves praise for his role as Lincoln Trout, the red pill-coded political wunderkind who commandeers Jed's campaign. Trout takes the reins from Brad Thompson (George Ferrier) as the show's most punchable character, yet his boiling-frog menace perfectly fits season 2's twistedness.

Åkerman and Snow's chemistry is electric enough to make viewers' hair stand on end—even when Margo and Sophie keep their clothes on—and season 2 throws them back into their toxic cycle, this time with bigger feelings and bigger stakes.

However, amidst all the expertly plotted chaos, The Hunting Wives never loses sight of whose story this is. What does it mean when the person you love the most brings out both the best and the worst in you? This is something both women grapple with, amidst a host of other problems of their own making, as they ponder the question on a deeper level than in the fun-and-games-heavy season 1. But fear not, there are still plenty of those to be had, as bad behavior is not a habit these two can easily kick — or even want to.

And for those wishing to condemn Margo and Sophie, consider that their crimes aren’t even a casual Friday for the male antiheroes of Breaking Bad and The Sopranos. The only difference is that Walt and Tony never had this much fun.

At its core, The Hunting Wives is a show about gun-toting, God-fearing Texans who present as penguin-monogamous 0s on the Kinsey scale only to carry out taboo, often same-sex affairs in private. Nobody really talked about it until Sophie showed up, and now season 2 sees the characters reckoning more directly with their personal and political contradictions, though without losing The Hunting Wives' steamy appeal.

This is most strongly seen in Callie's show and Jed's campaign. Though Married Justice is thoroughly for the red meat and red state crowd, California-based producer Nadia Kelly (Kim Matula) introduces Callie to what desire looks like when it's not restricted to behind closed doors. Meanwhile, Jed's conservative, values-based platform clashes directly with Margo's feelings for Sophie. Far from eat-your-vegetable messaging, these storylines organically take The Hunting Wives season 2 in an overtly political direction.

Things get darker as a result of both this and the fact that murder and violence are, well, sad and disturbing. Maple Brook has seen a lot of it, and not everyone can shake it off as easily as Margo Banks. Brad, once destined for golden-boy greatness as a freshman basketball star at Baylor University, is grieving the loss of his mother, Jill (Katie Lowes), whom he believes murdered his girlfriend.

When a video of him — captioned #SadBrad — morosely playing his guitar goes viral, Brad's storyline seems almost cartoonishly siloed from the rest of The Hunting Wives season 2, but the way it devolves is thematically on-brand enough that it just works. What doesn't is the wrapping up of every character's ending.

With this big an ensemble in a season only eight episodes long, someone's going to get the short shrift, and as the body count mounts, the storytelling gets less satisfying. It's not enough to derail the show, but between some disappointing deaths and the darker tone, The Hunting Wives season 2 loses some steam in the home stretch. But nothing can keep Margo and Sophie down, and they'll be back for season 3, where they will hopefully right these wrongs — and make plenty of new ones.

All eight episodes of The Hunting Wives release Thursday, November 26, on Netflix.
