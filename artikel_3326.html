‘The Fantasticks’ Review: A Welcome Broadway Debut Seven Decades In The Making

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Two trunks for storing theatrical props share the stage early in the new production of The Fantasticks, opening for the first time ever on Broadway tonight. One is “Hayes Theater” and the other “Sullivan Street Playhouse,” as clear a mission statement as any fan of this stalwart of ’60s whimsy could want.

With a new approach that reimagines the musical’s star-crossed boy-meets-girl plot for a boy-meets-boy world, this revival directed by Christopher Gattelli arrives in the 21st Century with both a fresh attitude and a devotion to the spirit of a work that all but defined Off Broadway charm nearly 70 years ago. Granted, that’s a charm that might well be lost on some audiences today, much the way Pippin and Godspell could seem impossibly twee in a Broadway where Downstate just opened and a dance club take on Shakespeare has New Yorkers lining up outside the Winter Garden. Then again, this is also a Broadway where the very sweet Two Strangers (Carry A Cake Across New York) held on longer than anyone imagined. Watch on Deadline

Gattelli and his fine cast of newcomers and veterans know perfectly well what they’re up to – and up against, staying faithful to the winsomeness that kept The Fantasticks running Off Broadway for a total 42 years (!). They know that abandoning the ingratiating amiability and theater-kid appeal of this Tom Jones-Harvey Schmidt musical that gave early breaks to Jerry Orbach and many generations of soon-to-be stars would be like cutting “Try To Remember” from the score.

No small part of this revival’s pleasures are the coming together of relative newcomers – Maxim Chlumecky and Nathan Levy as the two boys, Fire Island‘s Zane Phillips making an impressive Broadway debut as El Gallo – with beloved stage figures Leslie Rodriguez Kritzer and Ruthie Ann Miles (as the mothers). They’re all terrific singers with crowd-pleasing comic skills – Kritzer and Miles perform a splendid song-and-dance number, “Never Say No,” early in the show – and their matched by a supporting cast that includes David Patrick Kelly, Kevin del Aguila and Ahmad Simmons.

The musical’s lovely score, including the stand-out “Try To Remember” as well as once-familiar songs like “Much More,” “Soon It’s Gonna Rain” and “I Can See It” that crossed over to wider audiences when Barbra Streisand and others covered them back when the stage was a routine conduit to pop charts, retains its appeal (even if purists might sniff at Sam Davis’ appealing new orchestrations that add strings, woodwinds and some brass to the famously minimalist piano and harp sound of the original production and its 1960 cast album.

So, the plot – and keep in mind it falls somewhere between allegory and fairy tale, with a touch of the foolery and disguised motivations of Shakespearean comedy tossed in, and never quite matching its intended heights much less its sources. The two youths – Matt and Luisa in the original, Matt and Lewis here – are nextdoor neighbors, in love and pining but secretly, lest their feuding parents – fathers in the original, mothers here – demand a cease and desist.

Unbeknownst to the boys, the parents are actually great friends, and merely invented the fake feud to push the sons together. As the moms sing in the ode to reverse psychology “Never Say No,” “Dog’s got to bark, a mule’s got to bray/Soldiers must fight and preachers must pray/And children, I guess, must get their own way/The minute that you say no.”

The parents further conspire to recruit the famous magician, El Gallo, and two itinerant actors to fake-abduct Lewis so that Matt can play the hero and seal the lovers’ happy ending. All goes as planned, until the moonlit bliss of Act I gives way to the buyer’s remorse of Act II. The boys long for freedom – from one another – and the mothers descend to bickering and frayed nerves.

Another happy ending, older and wiser, eventually arrives, and here, as always, the musical’s increasingly cloying second act drags and strains under its own contrivances. Gattelli, with the same know-how he brought to Schmigadoon! and Death Becomes Her, goes quite a way in almost making us overlook the musical’s weaknesses, and he’s helped by his really game cast (Chlumecky, as Matt, the more boyishly adventuresome of the boys, steals just about every scene he’s in).

Gattelli’s restrained choreography, including some full-on dance numbers but just as often simpler movement and joyful bounding, works well with the minimalist traditions of the show, which extend to Jason Sherwood’s set design (the usual brick theater backdrop, a cut-out crescent moon, wheeled-in window frames now and then, a sweet flowering of greenery and starlight at the right moment) and Linda Cho’s costumes that blend the boys’ normcore with the showily sexy bandit flash of El Gallo.

As is this show’s custom, the wall that’s erected by the parents to keep the kids faux-apart is played by a silent actor (Ahmad Simmons, in concrete-gray formalwear and stovepipe hat) who stands between the two households (each side represented by a small plot of vegetation, flowers on one side, veggies on the other).

In this production, that character retains the traditional billing as “The Mute,” a dated term that here survives, unlike the original’s use of the word “rape” that, even in 1960, was an archaic synonym for “abduction” (as in Alexander Pope’s 1712 poem “The Rape of the Lock”). Here, as in most productions of the musical, they make the wise decision to just say abduction.

Even without doing a word-for-word comparison of the original Tom Jones (no, not that Tom Jones) lyrics and the ones he revised for this version shortly before his death in 2023, the musical supports the gender-switch with remarkable ease. Levy portrays Lewis (née Luisa) with a disarming celebration of guilelessness and an ingénue’s “I feel pretty” self-infatuation. The casting is on-point, with the small-framed and black-haired Levy to a nice visual counterpoint to Chlumecky’s more athletic build and blond halo of curls. They, and everything else in this production, work together to bring a theatrical warhorse galloping into 21st Century Broadway, surprising us with just how much pluck it can still carry.
