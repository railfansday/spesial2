How Many Draws Does It Take to See All 49 Numbers?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Imagine a lottery that draws seven different numbers, from 1 to 49, in every draw. How long would you have to watch before you had seen every one of the 49 numbers at least once?

It is a classic probability problem, known as the coupon collector problem: with 49 items to collect, and seven handed to you in each draw, how many draws should it take, on average, to hold the complete set?

The harmonic-number calculation — The standard approximation for the expected number of draws is: Expected draws ≈ (49 / 7) × H₄₉. Here H₄₉ is the 49th harmonic number, the sum of the reciprocals from 1 to 49: H₄₉ = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/49 ≈ 4.479. That value can be built up from three parts: the natural logarithm of 49, about 3.892; the Euler constant, about 0.577; and a small correction term, 1 / (2 × 49), about 0.010. Adding them gives 3.892 + 0.577 + 0.010 ≈ 4.479. Multiplying by 49 / 7, which is 7, gives the answer: 7 × 4.479 ≈ 31.4 draws. So, on average, it takes about 31 draws to see all 49 numbers at least once.

The intuition is simple. Early on, almost every number drawn is new. As the unseen pool shrinks, more of each draw repeats numbers already seen, and progress slows; the harmonic number captures that slowdown.

Two cautions belong with the result. First, 31.4 is an expected value, an average over many hypothetical runs, not a promise. Second, this is the standard approximation for draws taken seven at a time, not an exact value — but it can be checked by a second calculation.

A cross-check, one number at a time — Pick one number, say 17. In a single draw, the chance that 17 does not appear is 42 / 49, which simplifies to 6 / 7. After t draws, the chance that 17 still has not appeared is (6 / 7)^t. After 31 draws, that chance is (6 / 7)^31 ≈ 0.0084. Multiply by all 49 numbers, and the expected number still unseen is 49 × 0.0084 ≈ 0.41 — less than half a number, on average. That lines up neatly with the harmonic-number answer: by about 31 draws, the collection is essentially complete. After 50 draws, the chance falls to (6 / 7)^50 ≈ 0.00045, and the expected number unseen drops to about 0.02.

There is also a real-data anchor. In the full Macau archive of 2,443 draws, every one of the 49 numbers has appeared at least 310 times. Collecting the whole set was completed long ago in the real record; the interesting question is only about those first few dozen draws.

Seeing every number is not the same as matching all six — It is easy to confuse this result with a very different probability. The chance that a single ticket with six numbers matches all six drawn numbers is 1 in 13,983,816, from the combination count C(49, 6) = 13,983,816. Those two numbers answer two different questions. About 31 draws to see every number at least once describes a collection built up gradually, draw after draw. One in 13,983,816 describes a single ticket matching a single draw exactly.

What the answer does, and does not, tell us — The coupon collector calculation is descriptive mathematics. It describes how long a complete collection takes to build up, on average. It says nothing about which numbers will appear in the next draw. Every number starts each draw with the same chance as every other, whether it was seen yesterday or has been missing for a while. A simple question — how long until we have seen them all? — turns out to have a simple answer, about 31 draws, backed by two calculations that agree with each other. The mathematics is satisfying precisely because it stops there.
