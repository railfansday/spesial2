A selection of 9 prediction-market bets and stories from 2026 midterm night, as more than $1.48 billion rode on the vote.

Traders on prediction markets spent Tuesday night betting on the U.S. midterm elections as polls closed across the country. These platforms won a wide audience in 2024, when their odds signaled a Donald Trump presidential victory that traditional opinion polls did not. Media outlets and investors around the world have tracked those prices since then. Kalshi and Polymarket, the two biggest venues, advertise the accuracy of the odds they post. Their odds sit beside polls and returns as one more signal of who might win, and anyone trying to read an election has to decide how much faith the figures deserve.

More than $1.48 billion rode on the midterms, which turned election night into a fresh test of whether these markets merit trust. Analysts warn that the industry's reputation could suffer if results stray far from market prices, with record sums at stake. The markets have stumbled before. They missed several outcomes in U.S. primaries, and prices on Kalshi and Polymarket gave Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva a 20% chance of beating his right-wing rival, Flavio Bolsonaro. Lula won last month. Questions about integrity add to the strain. Several candidates have bet on their own races, and the Anti-Corruption Data Collective, a Washington nonprofit, found that relatively small wagers can move political markets. Forecasts that miss the result, or that a handful of bets can push around, would undercut the precision the companies promote.

Reuters filed its report from Washington on election night, Nov. 3, and drew on figures from two outside groups. BETINT, an analytics firm, aggregated market prices and studied the last-minute bets, while the Anti-Corruption Data Collective supplied trading volumes and market counts. The one exception is a Monday volume figure that Kalshi reported for itself. The Senate contest totals date to Oct. 29, and the election results reflect a 10 p.m. Tuesday snapshot taken before any final outcome. Academic research lends prediction markets some support. Studies tend to find these markets well calibrated at scale, among them a 2025 Vanderbilt University paper that examined nearly 2,500 markets tied to the 2024 election. Broad measures of that kind can miss close contests, though, since most matchups are uncompetitive. The nine entries below present a selection of bets and market stories from the night.

Prediction-market prices on Tuesday signaled a Democratic sweep of Congress, with traders backing the party to capture both the House and the Senate. The forecast reflected the market as a whole, the pooled judgment of buyers and sellers pricing each chamber at once. Odds swung toward Democrats at speed once the first polls shut at 6 p.m. ET, and the evening became a live test of the call. The shift tied the sweep price to incoming returns as well as to pre-election sentiment.

Early returns gave the House half of the bet firm footing. By 10 p.m., NBC News and CNN had each projected a Democratic win in the House of Representatives. Two networks arriving at the same projection gave that half of the forecast broader backing. Democrats also outran expectations in several Florida House districts that lean Republican. Gains on GOP-friendly turf fit the broad reach the sweep odds implied.

The Senate half stayed unsettled at the same hour. Democrats had seized Republican-held seats in North Carolina and South Carolina, yet it was too early to tell whether those pickups would deliver a majority. Each flip shrank the distance to a Democratic majority without closing it. Traders had priced an outcome the vote count had not confirmed.

Observers will scrutinize the implied sweep as final tallies arrive. The market's two-chamber call hinges on Senate contests the 10 p.m. returns left without a verdict. A sweep requires both halves, so a projected House win confirms part of the forecast. The Senate count will decide whether the market read the whole night or only half of it.
