Birds that are supposed to be completely safe from extinction are actually losing their genetic diversity very quickly

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Sixty-two common songbirds lost nearly half their genetic diversity in 50 years, and the most abundant species lost the most.

A songbird you can find on almost any hillside seems like it would be part of an abundant bird species with nothing to worry about in terms of population decline. In the Gaoligong Mountains of southwestern China, the bigger a songbird’s population, the faster it has been losing genetic diversity.

Across all 62 species the researchers compared, genetic diversity fell by an average of 43% to 49% in about 50 years. Every one of those species is listed as Least Concern, the lowest level of extinction risk.

Ri Liu, Feng Dong and their colleagues at the Kunming Institute of Zoology, part of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, sequenced DNA from birds collected in the 1960s and 1970s. Then they compared it with DNA from birds sampled in the same mountains since 2000.

Only songbirds were sampled, and only in this one range. Birds have lost more genetic diversity than any other major animal group worldwide, so the team expects other animals here to be losing some too.

Genetic diversity is the sum of the small differences in DNA across a population. A species with a lot of it has more ways to adapt when conditions change.

The team measured that diversity two ways. Across a whole population, variation fell by an average of 48.8%, with species ranging from 18.1% to 77.2%.

Within a single bird, variation between the two copies of its genome fell by 42.9%, ranging from 21.8% to 80%. Not one of the 62 species held steady.

By either measure, that’s a far steeper fall than earlier work had suggested. A 2019 analysis of 91 species, which used short repeating stretches of DNA rather than whole genomes, estimated a loss of about 6% over roughly a century. The Gaoligong birds lost more than that every decade.

The losses do match the steepest theoretical forecast. A 2021 projection, built on the 73% drop in monitored vertebrate populations since 1970, predicted that genetic diversity could fall by more than half in 50 years.

The team then asked which traits went with the biggest losses. Several did. The unexpected one was the size of a species’ population worldwide.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dong said the direction of that result surprised the team. “We expected large populations to buffer against genetic erosion,” he said.

“Instead, we found the opposite: the more abundant a species was, the more genetic diversity it lost over the past five decades.”

The reason, in Dong’s account, is that a large population only stays genetically rich because birds keep moving and breeding across one continuous stretch of habitat.

Cut that habitat into pieces and a big population becomes many small, isolated ones, each losing variation on its own. “So the very species we assumed were safest turn out to be experiencing the heaviest genetic erosion,” Dong said.

Population size was a minor factor, though. It accounted for less than a tenth of the variation between species in how much they lost. And the population figures are global estimates, which may not match how many of each bird actually live in these mountains.

When people clear a forest, two things happen to the birds. Some habitat disappears outright, so fewer birds fit in what’s left, and what remains breaks into patches, so birds in one patch stop meeting birds in the next.

If the problem were outright loss, species that produce few young should suffer most. If it were isolation, strong fliers should suffer least, because they can still cross the gaps.

Strong fliers lost the least. The team scored each species’ ability to travel from its wing shape, and the better travelers lost less diversity per generation.

Clutch size, adult survival, and breeding elevation made no measurable difference. The only stronger predictor was the size of a species’ recent population decline.

Forest patches across the Gaoligong range have multiplied more than tenfold in recent decades, and the average patch is now under a tenth of its old size.

The worst breakup is at low to middle elevations, and that matters because rivers and steep terrain already isolate the range. The lowlands are the routes birds use to move between neighboring mountains.

Conservation planning “should treat connectivity – not just population size or protected area coverage – as a core conservation target,” Dong told Earth.com. In practice, he said, that means keeping and rebuilding low-elevation corridors between otherwise isolated populations.

The old birds came from a museum. The Kunming Natural History Museum of Zoology holds specimens collected across the Gaoligong Mountains in the 1960s and 1970s, and the team extracted DNA from them.

The modern DNA came from tissue samples taken between the 2000s and 2020s and stored in a tissue bank for wild species in Kunming.

Each species was represented by four or five birds per era. That’s a small number, and the team cites earlier work showing it is enough to capture how much variation a population holds.

The median collection year was 1973 for the old birds and 2022 for the new ones. For these small birds, 49 years is about 20 generations.

And those museum specimens were collected before forest fragmentation here intensified, so they show what the birds’ genomes looked like beforehand.

On the IUCN Red List, all 62 species are in the lowest-risk category, Least Concern, and Yunnan province’s own red list rates them about the same. The range itself already has formal protection.

The Gaoligong Mountains lie where the eastern Himalayas meet the mountains of Indo-Burma and southwest China, and they are part of a World Heritage site. They hold nearly a tenth of the world’s songbird species on about 0.02% of Earth’s land.

The losses are also larger than the world’s biodiversity agreement treats as tolerable. The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, adopted in 2022, set a 10% loss of genetic diversity as the limit for a species to keep its ability to evolve. The Gaoligong songbirds are already well past it.

Dong said conservation groups should stop treating “Least Concern” as a synonym for “genetically safe,” and that genetic monitoring should extend to common species rather than stopping at the rare ones.

The study ruled out mutation rate and elevation as explanations for the losses. It can’t say whether past hunting, climate change, or invasive species added to the damage alongside fragmentation.

The oldest birds in the comparison date from the 1960s, and the authors wrote that their figures may still understate the true historical loss.

The bigger open question is whether other animals and plants in the same mountains are losing diversity the way these songbirds are. The team expects they are, to a lesser degree, and hasn’t measured it.

Dong said genomic monitoring should start where the gap between a species’ official status and its actual genetic health is likely to be widest.

That means biodiversity hotspots with rapid recent fragmentation, and common, widespread species that go unmonitored because they aren’t listed as threatened. Low-elevation corridors in mountain hotspots, where connectivity loss is most severe, would be among the priority regions, he said.

Genetic indicators have been proposed for the global biodiversity framework, but they aren’t yet part of routine monitoring. The baseline for that monitoring, Dong said, already exists in museum collections like the one in Kunming.

The full study was published in the journal Science.
