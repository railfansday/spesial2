Exit polls are surveys, not vote counts: how they work and where they strain

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How exit polls work, from precinct sampling to reweighting, and what notable elections in the U.S., U.K. and Ukraine show about their limits

The first numbers that appear on an election-night broadcast usually come from a survey, not a count. They reflect what a sample of voters told interviewers on the way out of their polling places. That survey is built to be corrected as real returns arrive. Understanding how exit polls work explains why the early picture can shift before the night is over.

Exit polls do two separate jobs. The first is to help news organizations project winners once polls close. The second is to explain the result: who voted for which candidate, how age, race and education lined up with those choices, and what views voters brought with them. The demographic tables that networks publish after an election come from this second job.

The method has limits. Pollsters interview voters at a sample of precincts rather than at every polling place. Early and mail voters never walk past an interviewer, so pollsters reach them by telephone. Some voters decline to take part. If one candidate's supporters refuse more often than the other's, the raw numbers lean the wrong way. Figures that leak during the afternoon are unweighted, raw survey results. They have not yet been adjusted to match counted votes. That is why they can differ from the breakdowns published late at night or the next day.

Those limits have surfaced in public more than once. In the U.S., networks called Florida twice in 2000 and withdrew both calls. In 2002, the consortium that supplied exit poll data to the networks failed on election night, and its members later disbanded it. In 2004, leaked numbers pointed toward John Kerry in key states before George W. Bush won. In the U.K., the 1992 exit poll pointed to a hung parliament before John Major's Conservatives won a majority.

Not every case is a story of bad data. In 1980, the controversy centered on when NBC projected the winner, not on whether its numbers were right. In Ukraine in 2004, exit polls were cited as evidence that the official count was wrong.

The first four slides cover the mechanics: what an exit poll is, how precincts and voters are chosen, where the method came from and why the numbers move. The other six examine elections in the U.S., U.K. and Ukraine where projections, data or systems went wrong or collided with official results.

An exit poll is a survey of voters taken on election day, mostly as people walk out of their polling places. Respondents say how they voted and answer questions about their age, race, education and views on the issues.

That pairing of choice and profile sets an exit poll apart from the vote count. Official tallies, which election officials compile and certify, record how many ballots each candidate received. They say nothing about who cast those ballots. An exit poll fills that gap by linking each answer to a voter's characteristics.

In the U.S., Edison Research runs the exit poll for the National Election Pool. The pool is a group of news organizations that share a single survey instead of each commissioning its own. Its members include ABC, CBS, CNN and NBC.

Not every major outlet is still in the pool. Fox News and the Associated Press stopped using the National Election Pool's exit poll. Both now rely on AP VoteCast, a separate survey run by NORC at the University of Chicago.

The two products reach voters on different schedules. The Edison exit poll centers on election day, with interviewers stationed outside polling places. AP VoteCast draws on interviews with voters both before and on election day. Its sample is not confined to people interviewed at a polling place on the final day.

The split matters for anyone switching channels on election night. A breakdown of how younger voters or college graduates voted on CNN or NBC comes from the Edison survey. A similar breakdown on Fox News or in an AP story comes from AP VoteCast. The two are separate surveys with separate methods. Their estimates for the same group of voters can differ.

Comparing figures across outlets starts with a basic check: which survey produced them. Neither survey replaces the official count, which remains the final record of who won.

Exit pollsters do not station interviewers at every polling place. They pick a sample of precincts designed to represent a whole state. Interviewers at those sites then approach departing voters at a fixed interval and hand them a short paper questionnaire.

The precinct is the first layer of the sample. A large state can have thousands of polling places, and staffing all of them on a single day is not practical. Pollsters instead choose a smaller set intended to reflect the state's electorate as a whole. The quality of the final estimate depends on how closely that set of precincts mirrors the state.

The individual voter is the second layer. At each sampled precinct, the interviewer does not simply approach whoever looks willing to talk. The interviewer follows a set interval instead, such as every third or fifth person to walk out. A fixed rule keeps the interviewer's own judgment out of the selection. Without it, a survey could tilt toward people who seem friendly, unhurried or similar to the interviewer.

Voters who agree to take part receive the questionnaire. It asks whom they voted for. It also covers their age, race and education, along with their views on the issues in the race. The respondent fills in the form rather than answering out loud. No one has to announce a choice to a stranger outside a polling place.

In-person interviews miss a sizable group of voters: people who cast ballots early or by mail. Those voters never walk out of a precinct on election day. An interviewer at the door has no way to reach them. Pollsters add telephone interviews to capture that group. The phone results are combined with the in-person questionnaires to describe the full electorate.

The method's central weakness is non-response. Some voters who are approached decline to take part. Refusals alone do not ruin a survey. The problem arises when one candidate's supporters say no more often than the other candidate's supporters. In that case, the completed questionnaires overrepresent one side. The raw result then skews toward the side that answered more readily, even if every precinct in the sample was well chosen.

Warren Mitofsky, a statistician at CBS News, is credited with developing the exit poll. CBS used the method in the 1967 race for governor of Kentucky, the starting point for a survey format U.S. networks still rely on.

Before joining CBS, Mitofsky worked at the U.S. Census Bureau. The federal agency runs the national census and a wide range of household surveys.

The exit poll changed the question pollsters asked. A pre-election survey asks people how they plan to vote, and some of them never turn out. An exit poll asks people who have just voted what they did. That gives a news organization a read on actual voters rather than likely ones, before any official tally is released.

Mitofsky did not stop at the survey itself. He built the exit poll into the system the networks used to project winners on election night. Those projections let anchors call a race on air without waiting for every ballot to be counted. The same data also gave reporters a way to describe which groups of voters backed each candidate.

His work in polling reached beyond election day. With Joseph Waksberg, a Census Bureau statistician, he developed the Mitofsky-Waksberg method. It is a technique for drawing random samples of telephone numbers. Random-digit dialing of this kind became a widely used approach for telephone surveys. Mitofsky also helped create the CBS News/New York Times poll, a long-running partnership between the network and the newspaper.

Decades after Kentucky, his name returned to the national exit poll. After Voter News Service collapsed in 2003, Edison/Mitofsky took over the U.S. exit poll for the networks. The operation paired Edison Research with Mitofsky International, the firm Mitofsky ran. Its system for the National Election Pool was first used in the 2004 presidential election.

Mitofsky died in 2006. The exit poll format he introduced in Kentucky remains part of how U.S. networks cover election night.

Exit poll figures shift over election night because the first numbers are raw survey answers, and pollsters later reweight them to match actual vote counts. The breakdowns posted late at night or the next day are adjusted figures, not the same data that circulated in the afternoon.

Unweighted numbers come straight from the questionnaires collected so far. They reflect only the voters interviewed up to that point in the day. Morning voters can differ from those who vote after work. A partial day of interviews may not resemble the full electorate.

The adjustment begins once real returns come in. As precincts report, pollsters compare the survey's estimate of each candidate's share with the counted vote. If the survey shows a candidate doing better than the tally, the responses are reweighted so the poll's topline matches the count. The answers about age, race, education and views move along with that adjustment.

That step changes the demographic picture too. If one candidate's share in the poll drops to match the count, the groups that favored that candidate look different in the published tables. The estimate of how a particular group voted can move between an afternoon leak and the final release.

This is why leaked afternoon numbers are an unreliable guide to the outcome. Those figures have not yet been checked against a single counted ballot.

Exit polls serve two separate purposes, and reweighting matters most for the second. On election night, the survey helps decision desks project winners, often alongside early returns from sample precincts. Once votes are tallied, the same survey becomes a central record of who voted for whom and why.

The weighted version is the one that lasts. Analysts and journalists draw on it in the weeks after an election to describe each candidate's coalition and voters' priorities. Matching the survey to the result keeps that description consistent with how the electorate actually voted. Without the adjustment, it would reflect only the people who agreed to fill out a questionnaire.

On election night in 1980, NBC projected that Ronald Reagan had defeated Jimmy Carter while polls were still open in western states. Carter conceded before voting had ended on the West Coast.

The accuracy of NBC's call was never the issue. The exit poll data behind it pointed to the correct winner. NBC was then one of the three major U.S. broadcast networks, alongside ABC and CBS. Reagan, the Republican challenger, unseated Carter, the sitting Democratic president. The final Electoral College tally was 489 votes for Reagan and 49 for Carter.

The criticism focused on when the call reached viewers. A national election in the U.S. spans several time zones. Polling places in the East close hours before those in the Pacific states. A projection broadcast after eastern polls close can reach voters in California, Oregon and Washington state while they still have time to cast a ballot.

Carter's concession added to the timing problem. The sitting president had publicly accepted defeat. Western voters who had not yet gone to the polls heard from the candidate himself that the presidential race was over.

The episode fed a long-running debate over whether early projections discourage people from voting. The concern is that a voter who believes the top race is settled may stay home. Staying home also means skipping contests further down the ballot, such as races for Congress, state offices and local measures.

The debate turned on a question an exit poll cannot answer. A survey of voters leaving polling places can indicate who is winning. It cannot measure how many people skip voting because they heard a result on television.

The 1980 case differs from exit poll controversies in which the data went wrong. The numbers did not overstate a candidate, the system did not fail, and NBC did not have to retract its call. The issue was the gap between knowing a result and broadcasting it while part of the electorate was still voting.

On election night in 2000, the major U.S. television networks projected Florida for Al Gore and later took the call back. In the early morning, they projected the state for George W. Bush and retracted that call too.

The first projection rested partly on exit poll results and partly on returns from a sample of precincts. Those numbers pointed toward Gore. The networks made the call before polls had closed in the western Panhandle. That part of Florida sits in the Central time zone, while most of the state runs on Eastern time. Some Florida voters could still cast ballots after the state had already been projected.

As actual vote counts arrived, they no longer supported a Gore win. The networks withdrew the projection and returned Florida to the undecided column. With the rest of the map largely settled, the presidency now depended on that one state.

Florida carried 25 electoral votes in 2000. When the networks called the state for Bush in the early morning, they were in effect calling the election. Gore phoned Bush to concede. He then called back to withdraw the concession as the gap between the two candidates narrowed. The networks retracted their Bush projection as well.

The state was decided by a margin of a few hundred votes. The result came only after a recount and a ruling from the U.S. Supreme Court. In Bush v. Gore, decided on Dec. 12, 2000, the court halted the recount. Bush carried Florida and won the presidency, although Gore won the national popular vote.

The episode exposed two separate weaknesses. One was statistical. An exit poll is a sample survey. No sample survey can reliably separate two candidates in a contest decided by a few hundred votes out of millions cast. The other was timing. Projecting a state while some of its polling places remain open raises questions about whether the call affects who turns out.

Network executives were called to testify before a House committee in early 2001 about their election-night projections. Florida in 2000 showed that a projection is a judgment built on survey data and partial returns, not a final count.

Voter News Service, the shared exit poll operation of the major U.S. television networks and the Associated Press, failed on election night in November 2002. Its system did not deliver the usual exit poll data and vote results to its members, who disbanded it in January 2003.

The consortium rested on a simple idea. Instead of each network paying for its own nationwide survey, the members shared one exit poll and one stream of vote results. Voter News Service had supplied that exit poll data since 1990, through presidential and midterm elections alike.

That arrangement made every member dependent on a single supplier. A failure at Voter News Service would affect all of them at once, not just one network.

That is what happened in the 2002 midterm elections. On election night, the exit poll figures and vote results the members expected did not arrive in the usual way. The pooled design meant the gap reached every member newsroom at the same moment.

The 2002 episode was a failure of delivery. It was not a case of a poll arriving on time and leaning toward the wrong candidate. The members did not receive the data and results they had built their election-night coverage around.

Its members ended the arrangement rather than carry Voter News Service into another election cycle.

A replacement was ready in time for the next presidential election. Edison/Mitofsky built a new exit poll system for the National Election Pool, the group of news organizations that took over the shared survey. That system was first used in 2004, the year George W. Bush faced John Kerry.

The new setup kept the pooled approach but changed who ran the survey. The networks still shared one exit poll. Edison/Mitofsky, an outside research firm, now carried out the work for them.

The 2002 failure showed that an exit poll depends on infrastructure as well as method. Its numbers are of use to a newsroom only if they arrive on election night.

The 2004 U.S. exit poll overstated support for Democratic nominee John Kerry, and early numbers that leaked online made him look like the leader in key states. George W. Bush, the Republican incumbent, won a second term instead.

The leaked figures were raw, unweighted survey results. They had not yet been adjusted against any counted votes. Read at face value, they pointed toward a Kerry victory.

The final count went the other way. Bush won 286 electoral votes to Kerry's 251. One elector cast a ballot for John Edwards, Kerry's running mate and a senator from North Carolina. A candidate needed 270 electoral votes to win.

Ohio, with 20 electoral votes, decided the outcome. Without it, Bush would have fallen short of 270. Kerry conceded the day after the election.

The poll was conducted by Edison/Mitofsky for the National Election Pool, the group of news organizations that sponsors the U.S. exit poll. It was the first presidential election for the system Edison/Mitofsky built after Voter News Service was disbanded in 2003. That made 2004 an early public test of the new system.

In January 2005, Edison/Mitofsky released an evaluation of its election-day survey. The report found that the exit poll had overstated Kerry's support. Its explanation turned on who agreed to respond. Kerry voters, the report concluded, had been more willing than Bush voters to complete the questionnaire.

The pattern is known as differential non-response. An exit poll records only the answers of people who agree to take part. When one candidate's backers turn interviewers down more often, that candidate looks weaker in the survey than at the ballot box. In 2004, that meant the raw data understated Bush and inflated Kerry.

The same survey also supplied the demographic breakdowns of the 2004 electorate. They were reweighted to match the final results once the votes were counted.

The broadcast exit poll for the 1992 U.K. general election pointed to a hung parliament, with no party holding a majority in the House of Commons. The actual result gave John Major's Conservatives an overall majority of 21 seats.

The BBC and ITV commissioned the poll for their coverage of the vote on April 9, 1992. Its projection suggested that neither the Conservatives nor Neil Kinnock's Labour Party would be able to govern alone. A hung parliament would have meant talks over a coalition or a minority government.

That outcome never arrived. The Conservatives returned to power for a fourth consecutive term, and Major stayed in Downing Street. Kinnock resigned as Labour leader after the defeat.

The exit poll was not the only survey to miss. Most pre-election opinion polls had shown Labour level with the Conservatives or slightly ahead. The gap between the surveys and the count popularized the phrase "shy Tory." The label describes Conservative voters thought to be less willing to tell pollsters how they planned to vote or had voted.

Seat projections in Britain depend on vote shares in marginal constituencies, where small shifts decide which party wins. A modest underestimate of one party's support can turn a projected hung parliament into a working majority. In 1992 the projection understated the Conservatives.

Later British exit polls performed better. The 2015 and 2017 exit polls came much closer to the final results than the 1992 poll did. Since 2010 the exit poll has been a joint project of the BBC, ITV and Sky News.

The 2017 exit poll stood apart from the pre-election surveys. Theresa May called an early election that year, and many pre-election polls pointed to a Conservative majority. The exit poll released as voting ended signaled a hung parliament instead. The count produced exactly that. The Conservatives remained the largest party but lost their majority. May then formed a government with the backing of Northern Ireland's Democratic Unionist Party.

In Ukraine's November 2004 presidential runoff, exit polls showed opposition leader Viktor Yushchenko in the lead. The official count declared Prime Minister Viktor Yanukovych the winner instead. That clash helped set off mass protests. A court-ordered rerun later gave Yushchenko the presidency.

The runoff pitted the two leading candidates against each other after neither won outright in the first round. Yanukovych was the sitting prime minister and had the backing of outgoing President Leonid Kuchma. Yushchenko, a former prime minister, led the opposition.

Yushchenko's supporters pointed to the exit polls as evidence that the official count did not reflect how people had voted. Large crowds gathered in Kyiv, centered on Independence Square $SQ. The demonstrations became known as the Orange Revolution, after the color of Yushchenko's campaign.

The dispute moved to the courts. Ukraine's Supreme Court threw out the runoff result and ordered the vote held again. The repeat runoff took place on Dec. 26, 2004, and Yushchenko won it. He took office as president in January 2005.

This case runs in the opposite direction from many well-known exit poll controversies. In the 2004 U.S. presidential election and the 1992 U.K. general election, the official count served as the benchmark, and the exit poll was the number that missed. In Ukraine, the exit poll was cited as the check on the official count.

The two measures capture different things. An exit poll records what a sample of voters say they did after leaving the polling station. An official count records the ballots that election authorities tally and report. Either one can go wrong. A survey rests on a sample, and a sample can miss. A count can go wrong through error or manipulation in how ballots are recorded and reported.

A gap between the two is a signal to investigate, not proof of which number is correct. In Ukraine, the rerun produced a result that matched the direction the exit polls had shown, not the original official tally.
