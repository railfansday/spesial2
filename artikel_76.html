A tropical Chinese city is half-empty and ready for the summit.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

After flying to the city of Shenzhen from the Philippines, President Trump will attend an economic summit and meet with Xi Jinping of China and Vladimir V. Putin of Russia.

Peter BakerLily KuoPaul Sonne and Keith Bradsher

Peter Baker reported from Manila; Paul Sonne and Keith Bradsher from Shenzhen, China; and Lily Kuo from Taipei, Taiwan.

The leaders of China and Russia met in China ahead of President Trump’s arrival there on Wednesday, a display of solidarity between two authoritarian leaders aligned against Western influence.

The talks between the Chinese leader, Xi Jinping, and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia in the Chinese city of Shenzhen came a day ahead of their meeting with Mr. Trump — the first-ever three-way gathering of the leaders of the United States, China and Russia.

The trilateral summit is expected to cap Mr. Trump’s whirlwind trip to Asia, which has been shadowed by the Nvidia scandal, trade conflicts and critical infrastructure breakdowns at home.

Mr. Trump left Manila on Wednesday afternoon for Shenzhen, a center of China’s artificial intelligence and robotics industries, for a meeting of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, a group of 21 member economies known as APEC. The ultramodern metropolis is almost spotless this week, its streets bedecked with pastel banners but mostly emptied of the crowds typical of many cities in China.

The APEC meeting, set to focus on digital trade, artificial intelligence and cross-border e-commerce, is expected to highlight how much economic ties along the Pacific Rim have been transformed since Mr. Trump first took office nearly a decade ago. The orthodoxy of free trade that once animated APEC has given way to Mr. Trump’s protectionist tariff policies as he attempts to unravel the move toward globalization of recent decades.

Even as Mr. Trump has been on the road, his administration was grappling with computer breakdowns involving the technology, airline and banking industries. On Wednesday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who is traveling with the president, said there was no evidence that the failures were related. He said the rush to patch systems before they are hacked by new A.I. capabilities may be contributing to the failures.

For Mr. Trump, it will be his third personal encounter of the year with Mr. Xi. Mr. Trump visited Beijing in May, then hosted Mr. Xi for a lavish state dinner at the White House in September. Although Mr. Trump once regularly lashed out at China over its trade practices, this year he has become more intent on forging friendly ties between the world’s two largest economies.

The visit, however, may have a see-no-evil, hear-no-evil quality because Mr. Trump has said he does not intend to raise issues of Chinese interference in American affairs. Nvidia’s chief executive, Jensen Huang, was forced to resign following revelations about an extramarital affair with a businesswoman whom U.S. intelligence determined was spying for China, but Mr. Trump has said he will not discuss the matter with Mr. Xi.

That follows a pattern of Mr. Trump avoiding direct confrontation with his Chinese counterpart over questions involving American security. Even after he made a prime-time address to the nation in July accusing China of hacking or purchasing U.S. voting files, he did not raise the matter in public when he hosted Mr. Xi in September, nor is he expected to this week.

Manila stop: Mr. Trump’s China visit will be the second stop on a lightning-fast trip to Asia. He stopped first in Manila, where he joined a meeting of another international group, the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, or ASEAN.

North Korea: Mr. Putin could meet with Kim Jong-un if the North Korean leader also travels to China for the APEC summit, Russian state news agencies reported, citing the Kremlin’s spokesman. It was not clear whether Mr. Kim would attend the summit.

Ukraine: Mr. Trump also said he would talk about Ukraine, on which Mr. Putin’s aides are engaging in talks with the U.S. and Ukrainian officials, but also renewing demands that NATO pull back its troops to where they were before the alliance expanded toward Russia.

Putin and Xi are expected to tour the campus that Moscow State University, Russia’s top university, set up in Shenzhen with the Beijing Institute of Technology. The leaders agreed to establish it a decade ago. The Shenzhen outpost looks like a mini version of the Moscow university, complete with a replica of its Stalin-era tower.

Putin and his team held talks with Xi followed by a smaller working breakfast. They have met dozens of times; this is at least their fourth in-person meeting this year. The talks lasted more than two hours, according to the Russian state news agency Tass.

By meeting before Trump arrives in China, Xi and Putin will have an opportunity to set some of the terms of the historic meeting between the three leaders, which is scheduled for Thursday.

Xi and Putin will likely want to align on nuclear arms control, a topic that Trump has said he is eager to discuss. Xi is reluctant to broach the subject, unwilling to place limits on China’s arsenal because it still remains much smaller than those of the United States and Russia.

Putin arrived in Shenzhen alongside a major show of force. Russian strategic nuclear bombers landed in Vietnam close to when Putin landed in China, according to satellite footage, marking the first publicly documented arrival of such warplanes in Vietnam since the Soviet era.

Russia and Vietnam have deep ties dating back to the Soviet era, and Russia supplies much of Vietnam’s military hardware. But Vietnam has also deepened ties with the United States in recent years, as it looks to bolster its defenses amid concern about China.

Putin’s decision to deploy the nuclear bombers to Vietnam alongside his arrival in China sends a clear message ahead of the trilateral talks with the United States and China: Russia remains the world’s largest nuclear power and has global reach.

Shenzhen is remarkably calm despite all the world leaders descending on the city. When Putin arrived at the airport early Wednesday morning, he was greeted by a Chinese group waving Russian flags and pom-poms. He is expected to meet with Xi today as well as Thai and Vietnamese leaders.

World leaders coming to Shenzhen this week are arriving in an almost spotless, ultramodern metropolis, its streets bedecked with pastel banners but mostly emptied of the crowds typical of many Chinese cities.

Shenzhen lies slightly closer to the Equator than Havana, providing a balmy venue for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation gathering. Under sunny skies with mild temperatures, a festive mood prevails in the city, which bears a strong resemblance to Miami — only much larger and with taller, newer high-rises.

Schools in Shenzhen have closed this week, many residents have left town and local authorities have instructed companies to allow those employees who are still here to work from home. The result has been a holiday atmosphere of sorts in a city with a tech sector known for its punishing “996” work schedules: 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week.

Youthful volunteers — mainly students at local universities — stand on street corners wearing bright red vests, having been offered academic credit in exchange for being trained as guides. Thousands of gawkers have been promenading past the large conference center that will host heads of state from across the Asia Pacific region, taking photos of themselves in front of the flower gardens inside the fence.

Among the crowds walking outside the high steel fences around the conference center was a local resident who had brought her parents and her dog. The resident said that workers had been planting flowers for a year ahead of the summit, and added that a community worker had given her a big package of napkins as a gift after she agreed not to drive her car in the coming week. Shenzhen has halved fares this week on its extensive subway system to help clear the roads for summit buses.

At the same time, however, residents are aware of occasional hassles. “Many friends who live around here plan to travel outside the city because of the inconvenience of transportation — many roads will be closed to traffic,” said the resident, who gave only her family name, Zhang. As is common at high-profile and high-security events in China, she was wary of giving her full name, which can be risky for Chinese citizens.

Few dare to express any misgivings, however, accepting the disruption with resignation. “Even if we’re unhappy about it, we can’t really say so,” said a resident surnamed Wang. “The government wouldn’t welcome our dissatisfaction.”

While gatherings of world leaders always prompt extensive security, China’s efforts in Shenzhen are particularly intensive. Surveillance cameras are ubiquitous along streets in China and not noticeably more common this week in Shenzhen, but there are other, unobtrusive signs of the elaborate security effort underway.

Xiangmei Road, located near the main summit venue and lined with some of China’s costliest condominium towers, was much quieter than usual on Monday. A cleaner outside one of the towers who gave only her family name, Li, said that the family employing her has simply left town this week.

Police have been stationed in apartments on upper floors facing the conference center, drones have been prohibited and the remaining residents have received social media messages urging them to support the gathering, Ms. Li said.

“This place is usually very crowded,” she said, “but look how quiet it is now.”

Xi has begun talks with Putin in Shenzhen, according to Chinese state media. The two leaders were expected to meet on the sidelines of the APEC summit ahead of a trilateral summit with Trump.

While President Trump and President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr of the Philippines met in Manila, President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia was speaking to business leaders in Shenzhen, China, at the APEC CEO summit. Putin said his coming meeting with Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping had “great potential benefits.”

When China’s then-paramount leader, Deng Xiaoping, toured Shenzhen in 1992, he used the southern Chinese city to signal an embrace of open markets, setting off decades of rapid growth that would make his country the world’s factory floor.

Since then, Shenzhen has grown from a sleepy town into a gleaming metropolis that dwarfs its more established neighbor, Hong Kong. Apple’s main assembler, Foxconn, runs a sprawling campus there, and Walmart manages its China business from the city. It is home to many Chinese brands like Huawei, BYD and the drone maker DJI.

This week, China’s leader, Xi Jinping, is hosting a regional economic summit in the same city, using it to make the case that China’s growth can benefit the world. Shenzhen has been primped for the occasion, with bus shelters plastered with the slogan: “Building an Asia-Pacific Community to Prosper Together.”

But some of that message may be difficult to square for many of China’s trading partners. To them, Shenzhen also embodies much of what makes the world uneasy about China today: a flood of cheap exports; tech giants that pose security risks; and a Communist Party that has traded Mr. Deng’s freewheeling experimentation for more state control.

Mr. Xi’s moment in the spotlight at the Asia Pacific Economic Cooperation forum will also be clouded by allegations of a Chinese spy scandal at the American chip giant, Nvidia, that has already prompted Washington to tighten Chinese access to advanced American chips.

And he is set to meet for the first time with President Trump and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia, a gathering that could unsettle smaller powers like Ukraine. (Mr. Trump has also suggested that he might see the North Korean dictator Kim Jong-un while in China.)

In some ways, the setting itself could be read as a statement of defiance.

Shenzhen is home to companies Washington has labeled national security risks, like Huawei and ZTE, as well as Chinese artificial intelligence labs racing to close the gap with American rivals. Many of the companies have been subjected to American sanctions and blacklists.

Mr. Xi wants to show that “China can do well despite America’s all-out effort to kill it” with those trade restrictions, said Minxin Pei, a professor at Claremont McKenna College who studies Chinese leadership politics.

That is an image that Beijing has tried to cultivate after Washington began restricting direct sales of advanced chips to China in 2022, pointing to Huawei’s homegrown processors and DeepSeek’s and other Chinese labs’ breakthrough A.I. models as proof that U.S. restrictions have failed.

Beijing is also casting itself as a partner to other countries in technological advancement, offering Chinese cooperation and know-how on A.I., an approach that competes with the United States’ attempts to unite partners around A.I. supply chains independent of China.

For China, the news had threatened to distract from the upcoming summit. Less than three weeks before the gathering, The New York Times reported that a Chinese businesswoman who had a close relationship with Jensen Huang, the chief executive of Nvidia, was found to be passing intelligence to Beijing. In the days since, Mr. Huang has stepped down and the company’s board has said it would conduct an investigation.

The allegations, which China has denied, could have scuttled talks with Mr. Trump and put the countries’ fragile trade truce at risk. The claim of such a bold espionage plot was also a potential source of embarrassment for a country hosting business and government leaders at a major event.

China has carried on as if it were business as usual. And in a way, Beijing has already scored a win: The controversy has failed to derail the scheduled talks between Mr. Xi and Mr. Trump, who has downplayed the alleged spy plot.

Rather than declaring that he would confront China about the Nvidia scandal, Mr. Trump has said he does not intend to bring it up. “I don’t think you want to insult China just before you get there,” he told reporters outside the White House on Monday when asked about divisions within his administration about how tough to be on China.

But the scandal is already costing China. On Monday, the Trump administration issued a rule to stop Chinese companies from accessing advanced Nvidia chips through data centers in third countries, and to require licenses for chip exports to companies in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil.

China’s export machinery is practically unbeatable. This month, China reported that its exports exceeded its imports by more than $1 trillion in the first 10 months of this year, putting it on track to eclipse last year’s record.

But as its factories have churned out solar panels, electric cars and other goods for consumers globally, China has faced pushback not only from the United States but from around the world. It needs to head off efforts by trading partners like the European Union to block its exports.

That’s why China is billing the event as an opportunity for the region to champion free trade and oppose protectionism.

In reality, China has been turning inward. Beijing has sought to squeeze out foreign technology, electronics and auto brands in favor of Chinese firms. The Communist Party, which under Mr. Xi has tightened its grip on the economy, vowed last month to expand its presence inside internet companies. Shen Weizhong, a former policy planner with China’s Foreign Ministry, called the approach “manageable openness,” in which security weighs as heavily as growth.

Mr. Xi’s willingness to turn the world’s dependence on Chinese goods into leverage has also alienated other countries.

Beijing imposed controls on exports of rare earths last year, and it has recently moved to restrict the exports of dozens of technologies, including drones, artificial intelligence models and agents, and know-how about advanced medicines and solar panels. China says these measures are a response to U.S. blacklists and export restrictions.

“Chinese leaders recognize that the golden era of globalization is over, and geoeconomic competition has become the new normal,” said Yuen Yuen Ang, a professor of political economy at Johns Hopkins University.

Mr. Xi is expected to sit down with Mr. Trump and Mr. Putin on Thursday, a meeting that is without precedent. China’s Foreign Ministry on Monday called the meeting one “of groundbreaking significance.”

For Beijing, the gathering casts China as a power broker that can bring Washington and Moscow to the same table. Mr. Xi wants to show the world, and especially his own people, that China under his leadership is “consequential, influential and highly respected, with enormous convening power,” said Mr. Pei of Claremont McKenna.

Mr. Trump said on Monday that he planned to discuss the war in Ukraine in Shenzhen. Beijing is reluctant to take the lead on peace talks, said Zhao Long, a senior research fellow at the Shanghai Institutes for International Studies, lest it create “the wrong impression” that “major powers are dividing up the interests of other countries.”

And Mr. Trump has raised the possibility of another guest: the North Korean dictator, Kim Jong-un. He told reporters on Monday as he left the White House: “And we may meet with Kim Jong Un, also. So, that’ll be good. We could — we have a chance to accomplish a lot.”

A spokesman for China’s foreign ministry, Guo Jiakun, asked on Tuesday if Mr. Kim would be attending, said: “I do not have information on this.” Russian state news agencies reported that Mr. Putin could meet with Mr. Kim if the North Korean leader travels to China, citing Dmitri S. Peskov, the Kremlin’s spokesman.

Javier C. Hernández reported from Tokyo, and Gal Koplewitz from London.

When China’s leader, Xi Jinping, met with President Trump at the White House earlier this fall, the two leaders toasted to friendly relations.

But in private, Mr. Xi offered Mr. Trump a stark warning about Japan, a U.S. ally, according to two officials briefed on the talks, who requested anonymity to discuss private conversations. Mr. Xi told Mr. Trump that Japan’s recent military buildup threatened to destabilize Asia, the officials said, and that Mr. Trump should be cautious about working with Japan’s prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, describing her as a “militarist.”

The warning from Mr. Xi was part of a broader effort by China to isolate and undermine Japan after Ms. Takaichi said last year that Japan could be forced to defend Taiwan in case of a Chinese invasion. China considers Taiwan, a self-governed democracy that lies just 69 miles from Japan’s westernmost island, part of its territory.

Since the comments, Mr. Xi has overseen a campaign involving propaganda and diplomacy to target Japan, according to interviews with officials and scholars, and an analysis by The New York Times of Chinese Foreign Ministry briefings and state media. It is all part of a push, analysts say, to persuade the world that Japan is a resurgent aggressor.

China has deployed words and images to portray Japan as a World War II-era enemy, using phrases like “remilitarization” and “neo-militarism,” and videos that compare Japan to a “rampaging gray rhino.”

Chinese officials have repeatedly sought to belittle Ms. Takaichi, Japan’s first female prime minister, omitting her title in official briefings, breaking with longstanding practice. State media has depicted Ms. Takaichi in short skirts and a nurse’s uniform in cartoons, and described her as “Abe’s girl,” referring to Shinzo Abe, the former Japanese prime minister and Ms. Takaichi’s mentor.

In private conversations with Western scholars, Chinese academics have recently pushed the narrative that Japan is the “Israel of East Asia,” poised to lead the United States into conflict and destabilize the region.

Russia has helped amplify China’s talking points, branding Japan as a threat. Moscow has echoed Beijing in describing Japan as an “enemy state” that is not to be trusted, invoking a World War II-era designation used against former Axis powers.

Mr. Xi and Ms. Takaichi will cross paths this week at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit meeting in Shenzhen, China. The encounter is expected to be frosty; Mr. Xi might try to use the summit as an opportunity to alienate Ms. Takaichi from Japan’s allies, experts say.

The analysis covered Chinese state-run and Communist Party-run outlets, including China Central Television, the Xinhua news agency, Global Times and People’s Liberation Army Daily.

China has long been at odds with Japan. But the current scale and coordination of its campaign have little modern precedent, experts said.

“Anti-Japan propaganda is just absolutely everywhere now,” said David Bandurski, executive director of the China Media Project, an independent research group. “It’s a far more focused use of anti-Japanese framing than we’ve seen at any point.”

Ms. Takaichi told Japan’s Parliament on Nov. 7, 2025, that any attempt by China to blockade or seize Taiwan could constitute a “survival-threatening situation” for Japan, implying a potential military response. Her comments reflected a long-held Japanese view, but one that has rarely been verbalized.

China reacted furiously. The government urged millions of tourists to avoid Japan, limited the export of some critical minerals, restricted Japanese seafood imports and increased patrols in disputed waters.

Mr. Xi appears to be concerned that Ms. Takaichi, a close American ally who has been a critic of China, could get in the way of China’s military and economic expansion in Asia. Analysts said that he is seeking to drive a wedge between Japan and the United States, as well as countries like Australia, South Korea, India and Britain.

Ms. Takaichi has accelerated a military buildup to counter China, raising military spending to 2 percent of gross domestic product two years ahead of schedule and loosening postwar rules that restricted the export of weapons. She is expected to push for even more military spending next month as part of a new national security strategy.

In the year since Ms. Takaichi’s comments on Taiwan, China has sharply escalated its rhetorical attacks on Japan, according to the Times’s analysis, and there has been a surge in propaganda aimed at both domestic and global audiences.

Since last November, the Chinese Foreign Ministry has publicly accused Tokyo of “remilitarization” or “neo-militarism” at more than 60 briefings, the analysis found. People’s Daily, the Communist Party mouthpiece, cited Japanese “neo-militarism” in 65 articles, and warnings of Japanese “militarism” surged across the outlets we analyzed.

Chinese officials have repeatedly invoked the image of a “gray rhino,” a phrase used in Chinese to describe a looming threat, to characterize Japan. One video that circulated on Chinese news sites, generated by artificial intelligence, shows a rhino, draped in a rising sun flag, wreaking havoc across land and sea.

China Daily, a state-controlled news organization aimed at an international audience, published a cartoon in May showing Ms. Takaichi dressed in samurai armor and carrying a sword. A People’s Daily cartoon describes Japan’s military ambitions as “sugarcoated poison,” showing Ms. Takaichi wrapping skulls to give to children.

The Times parsed more than 20,000 transcripts from the Chinese Foreign Ministry’s briefings since 2013; nearly 400,000 articles in People’s Daily; more than 100,000 items from Guangming Daily, P.L.A. Daily, Global Times and Xinhua; and thousands of published readouts of Chinese leaders’ meetings, state broadcasts, ministry releases and social media trends.

The analysis found that Chinese media has helped amplify official messaging. When interest in China’s feud with Japan faded from April to June and questions from foreign news outlets fell, reporters from state-controlled media in China tried to keep the story alive by asking about Japan at a far higher rate than before.

In December, a TV reporter from Shenzhen noted that scholars across the region were warning of Japan’s “remilitarization,” prompting a spokesman for the Chinese Foreign Ministry to answer with a list of Japan’s wartime atrocities. In August, on the anniversary of the bombing of Hiroshima, a Xinhua reporter said at a news briefing that “81 years later, Japan’s right-wing forces are stirring, plotting to restore militarism.”

The propaganda has sometimes spilled into the policy arena. When China’s Commerce Ministry placed 80 Japanese industrial, defense and research listings on export control blacklists and watch lists this year, a stated goal was to curb “remilitarization” and “neo-militarism.”

Mr. Xi’s government has made a point of singling out Ms. Takaichi. A year after taking office, she is still relatively popular at home and has been a vocal supporter of Taiwan throughout her career. She has also built a warm relationship with Mr. Trump.

One tactic used by the Chinese government is to omit Ms. Takaichi’s title, a pointed break with diplomatic protocol. From July through September, officials dropped “prime minister” in six of seven mentions of Ms. Takaichi.

On Weibo, a popular Chinese social media site that is heavily curated by the government, hashtags that mocked Ms. Takaichi’s wardrobe, emotions, voice or hugging style trended at least 30 times over the past year, the analysis found.

China also appears to be working to foment opposition to Ms. Takaichi inside Japan’s political system. Chinese officials have tried to persuade visiting delegations of Japanese lawmakers and executives recently that Ms. Takaichi does not understand the economic risks of her tough approach to China, and that they should stand up to her, according to two people briefed on the conversations.

After one such meeting in September, Wang Yi, China’s foreign minister, said that it was time for Japan to “correct the erroneous words and deeds” of its leader.

In trying to stir concern about Japan, Chinese scholars have told their counterparts in the West that Ms. Takaichi’s Japan is becoming the “Israel of East Asia.” A delegation of Chinese scholars in South Korea used the phrase during a recent meeting with American scholars, according to David Sacks, an expert on China at the Council on Foreign Relations who took part in the meeting.

Mr. Sacks said his Chinese counterparts repeatedly raised concerns about Japanese militarism. They told the Americans that they feared Japan was seeking to become the most powerful country in East Asia and could draw the United States into conflict.

The aim, Mr. Sacks said, was to push Mr. Trump to “rein in Japan.”

“They want the United States to tell its ally to knock it off and to de-escalate with Beijing,” he said.

During his visit to the White House earlier this year, Mr. Xi suggested that Mr. Trump should join China’s efforts to counter Japan. Mr. Xi invoked World War II to highlight friendship between Beijing and Washington and cited Japan as a common enemy. He described Imperial Japan as “fascist” and “militarist” aggressors.

China and Russia have increasingly reinforced each other’s attacks on Japan, the analysis shows, using common terms like “remilitarization.”

Analysts say it is all part of President Vladimir V. Putin’s recent pressure campaign against Japan. In August, he made his first-ever visit to islands claimed by both Moscow and Tokyo. Last month, Russia detained a Japanese journalist on espionage charges. And in recent days, Mr. Putin has seized Russian assets of Japan Tobacco.

During a visit to Beijing in May, Mr. Putin signed a statement with Mr. Xi that included a lengthy section devoted to denouncing Japan’s military moves. The statement said, “Japan’s current acceleration of ‘remilitarization’ poses a serious threat to regional peace and stability.” The statement added that Japan should “resist ‘new militarism’ and ‘remilitarization.’”

During an appearance in Moscow last month, Mr. Putin said that he wanted good relations with Japan. But he added, “We are concerned by Japan’s plans for militarization.”

The Times collected hundreds of thousands of documents from multiple official Chinese sources: China’s Foreign Ministry briefings, articles from People’s Daily, P.L.A. Daily, Guangming Daily, Global Times, China Daily and Xinhua, as well as Chinese military, ministry, broadcast and social media records. Most sources were downloaded from official websites, and the rest from public digital archives, like the Internet Archive. To quantify the appearance of terms like “remilitarization,” “rearmament” and “neo-militarism” in Foreign Ministry briefings, we used A.I. models developed by Anthropic and Google, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Carbon, to strip out reporters’ questions and measure phrase frequencies per 1,000 Chinese characters. We also used A.I. to separate official statements from third-party quotes, generate initial translations, and classify the topic of news stories, briefing questions and social media trends.

Every quote and translation used in the story was verified against the original Chinese records.

Damien covers global affairs from Vietnam.

President Trump’s trip to Asia this week will largely be seen as a test of American power in the main region where the United States and China compete for influence.

His meetings in Manila and Shenzhen, China, will be scrutinized for shifts on trade and security. Deals he might strike with Beijing — plus a gathering with President Xi Jinping of China and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia — will put the world’s great-power contest on display, as all three seek to show strength.

But Asia is not just a theater of competition. Many countries in the region have become wealthier, more technologically advanced and increasingly interconnected. Especially now, as China’s military becomes more threatening and the United States less reliable, they are hedging against greatness and relying more on neighbors.

Asian students and workers are moving to neighboring states. Countries with expanding defense industries are selling weapons to new partners. Nations that compete for high-tech investment are even making plans to cooperate on energy and cybersecurity.

“They’re all looking at what more they can do together when the great powers are behaving so badly,” said Ted Osius, a former U.S. ambassador to Vietnam and adjunct professor of geopolitics at Fulbright University in Ho Chi Minh City. “If you’re a middle power, you’re thinking ‘I need more options.’”

Here are a few numbers that provide a sense of what the region contributes to the global economy, and how its nations are becoming more bound together to shape the world.

The population of the 11 nations that make up the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, a regional grouping that is holding its annual summit in Manila. ASEAN approved a digital framework agreement on Saturday that aims to create a multilateral digital market. If it rolls out smoothly next year, a small business in Vietnam could sell and ship products to Singapore with invoices, payment and customs handled through a regional online system.

The value of the Asia-Pacific market for medical tourism — the world’s largest. With portable insurance and medical visas, it’s becoming common to travel for better or more affordable cancer care, orthopedics, dentistry or cosmetic surgery. Life expectancy in high-income countries (like Japan) is around 84, compared with 79.8 in the United States, where health care costs are roughly twice as high.

The share of the world’s semiconductor chips that are manufactured in Asia. Without China, about 55 to 60 percent are made in Asia, led by South Korea and Taiwan, which makes more than 90 percent of the world’s high-end chips. The global market is worth roughly $1.5 trillion.

The number of top-grossing musical acts in Asia that were K-pop performers from August 2025 to July 2026. The top four earners for live shows in Asia were all K-pop stars, with Seventeen topping the list. Lady Gaga ranked fifth in the region.

The number of universities in the Asia-Pacific region in the QS global top 100. Among them are 11 in mainland China and Hong Kong, plus nine in Australia. There are 37 in Europe and 26 in the United States.

The number of airline passenger journeys across the Asia-Pacific, excluding China, in 2024. Driven by booming demand in India and Southeast Asia, regional traffic is projected to more than double to 4.1 billion passengers over the next two decades.

The approximate cost of Indonesia’s recent deal to buy India’s BrahMos supersonic missile systems for coastal defense. India has inked similar deals with Vietnam and the Philippines, as the region seeks alternatives to expensive Western weapons systems.

The total number of Uniqlo stores worldwide, up from 1,795 in 2016. Even as its retail footprint in China has declined, growth in Southeast Asia, India and Australia has continued. The Japanese fashion brand is now one of the world’s fastest growing clothing retailers.

The number of Japanese visits to South Korea in 2025 — a record for two countries that have been gradually overcoming a long history of resentment. The curiosity goes both ways: Koreans also set a record for trips to Japan last year, with 9.46 million visits.

The number of cross-border energy projects planned among ASEAN countries. With high-voltage lines on land and plans for some of the longest power cables ever to be placed underwater, the goal is to more than double transmission capacity and to pave the way for a multilateral power market that is more reliant on renewable energy, like hydropower.
