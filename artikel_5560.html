The average 401(k) balance across all ages is $167,970, and the median is $44,115, according to a 2026 Vanguard analysis cited by Ally. Both numbers describe the same pool of savers, but each one answers a different question.

The average is the mean. It comes from adding every account balance together and dividing the total by the number of accounts. Every dollar in every account counts toward the result.

The median works differently. It is the balance in the middle once all accounts are sorted from smallest to largest. Making the largest account bigger or the smallest account smaller does not change it.

That difference shapes how each figure moves over time. A sharp gain in a handful of very large accounts can lift the mean on its own. The median shifts only when enough accounts cross from one side of the midpoint to the other.

Each figure gives a different yardstick for a personal balance. A saver who compares an account with $167,970 is measuring against a figure that every account helps set, large or small. A saver who compares an account with $44,115 is measuring against the account at the exact midpoint.

The average shows how much money the accounts would hold if the total were split evenly among savers. The median shows where a typical account actually lands.

Both figures combine all age groups. A worker in their first job and a worker close to retirement are counted in the same totals. Neither number is a benchmark for any single stage of a career.

Each figure is also a single snapshot from 2026. They show where balances stood, not how quickly any individual account grew to reach that level. A saver's own balance reflects years of contributions, market performance and withdrawals that the snapshot does not capture.

The figures cover 401(k) accounts only. They do not capture savings held in IRAs, taxable brokerage accounts or other retirement plans. A household with money spread across several account types may have more set aside than its 401(k) balance alone shows.

A relatively smaller number of high-balance accounts could be what lifts the overall average 401(k) balance to $167,970, well above the median of $44,115. Ally presents those large accounts as a possible cause of the gap, not a proven one.

Both figures come from a 2026 Vanguard analysis and describe the same set of accounts. Each one answers a different question about that set.

An average, or mean, adds every balance together and divides the total by the number of accounts. Every dollar in every account feeds into that result. A single very large balance raises the figure for the whole group. A handful of them can move it a long way.

A median ignores how big the biggest accounts are. It sorts every balance from smallest to largest and reports the one in the middle. If the largest account in a group doubled overnight, the median would not budge. The average would rise.

Statisticians describe the median as robust, or resistant to outliers. That is why it is often the preferred figure for measures with a few extreme values, such as household income or home prices.

Data with this shape is called right-skewed, or positively skewed. Most values cluster toward the low end, while a long tail of large values stretches toward the high end. In that kind of distribution, the mean typically lands above the median.

Retirement account balances fit that shape. A balance cannot fall below zero, but there is no ceiling on how large it can grow. That leaves room for a long upper tail and none for a matching lower one.

The pattern repeats across age groups. A 2026 analysis that breaks balances down by age shows the average above the median in every band. That holds from savers under 25 through those 65 and older.

For a saver measuring a personal balance against national numbers, the median offers the more realistic yardstick. The average still measures total assets spread across all accounts, but it says less about where most individual savers sit.

Savers under 25 hold an average 401(k) balance of $7,259 and a median balance of $2,234, according to a 2026 analysis. Both are the lowest figures for any age band in that data.

The spread between the two numbers is already wide at this age. Most savers in this group hold far less than the average suggests. For someone under 25 checking their progress, the $2,234 median is the more realistic comparison point.

The first goal in this decade is to make regular contributions a habit. In a 401(k), contributions come straight out of each paycheck. Saving then continues without a fresh decision every month.

Choosing an amount that fits the budget helps the habit last. A manageable contribution is easier to keep up than one that strains each paycheck.

Collecting the full employer match is the other early priority. A worker who contributes at least enough to qualify for the whole match leaves none of the employer's matching money unclaimed.

Two setup steps are easy to miss in a first job with a retirement plan. One is choosing the investments the contributions go into. The other is naming beneficiaries, the people who would receive the account if the saver dies.

Starting early gives compounding more time to work. Money contributed before 25 has more years to grow than money added later. Any returns it earns can then produce returns of their own.

A 401(k) is seldom the only claim on a paycheck at this stage. Buying a house, paying down debt, starting a family and investing in an IRA all compete for the same income. Each of those goals draws on money that could otherwise go into a workplace plan.

It does not appear in a 401(k) balance, though. The figures for this band may therefore not capture everything a saver in this age range has set aside for retirement.

A raise or a promotion offers a clear moment to act. Increasing the share of each paycheck that goes into the plan at the same time as a pay increase means the higher contribution arrives alongside more income. Raising the rate later, without a pay increase, lowers take-home pay. Pairing the two changes ties the decision to an event that already alters the paycheck.

Some workplace plans offer auto-escalation. The feature raises the contribution rate automatically over time. The saver does not have to remember to make each change. Workers can check their plan documents or benefits portal to see whether their plan includes it.

The investment mix deserves a fresh look during these years too. A portfolio chosen early in a career may no longer match how far away retirement is. It may also no longer match how much market risk the saver is willing to accept.

A review should test the current allocation against both measures. One is the time left before retirement. The other is the saver's comfort with swings in the account's value. An allocation that fails either test is a reason to adjust it.

For a saver in this decade, the median is the more practical yardstick. An account near $78,730 sits around the middle of its age group, even though it trails the average by a wide margin.

The contribution rate should keep pace with salary growth through these years. Pay often rises in this stretch of a career. If the percentage stays flat, each raise lifts the dollar amount saved but not the share of income going to retirement. Raising the rate along with salary keeps saving in step with earnings.

Asset allocation needs a fresh look in the mid-40s and early 50s. The bigger the balance, the more the investment mix shapes how the account performs.

Two items specific to 401(k) plans also call for review at this stage. The first is how much of the account sits in the employer's own company stock. That holding ties part of a saver's retirement money to the same company that pays the saver's salary.

The second is any decision about borrowing from the 401(k) through a plan loan. Money taken out as a loan is no longer invested in the account until it is paid back.

An IRA is a possible additional account for savers in this group. It is opened and held separately from the workplace plan.

These years usually fall near the end of a working career. That timing changes which questions matter most. Ally treats the period as a turning point and centers its guidance on three tasks.

The first is reviewing investment risk. An allocation picked decades earlier was built for a long horizon. A saver a few years from needing the money has a much shorter one. A review checks whether the current level of risk still fits the time left before withdrawals begin.

The second is planning future withdrawals. Savers in this band are nearing the point where the account stops mainly taking in contributions and starts paying money out. Ally advises mapping out that plan before the first withdrawal comes due. The plan and the risk review are linked. The expected timing of withdrawals sets how long each part of the balance has left to stay invested.

The third is watching for early-withdrawal penalties. Pulling money out of a 401(k) too soon can trigger them. Ally flags these charges for savers at this stage. The same caution applies to anyone weighing the account as a source of cash for expenses before retirement.

Many people in their early 60s still contribute to a 401(k). At that stage, they are also deciding when and how to start drawing money out.

A salary-multiple benchmark sets retirement savings goals as a share of pay. The targets are one times annual income saved by age 30, three times by 40, five times by 50 and seven times by 60.

Ally lays out these milestones in its guide to 401(k) balances by age. The guide does not name the research behind them. It offers the multiples as suggested targets rather than fixed rules.

The yardstick ties savings to income instead of a set dollar amount. The goal moves with earnings. A higher salary means a higher target, and a lower salary means a lower one. Two savers of the same age can be on track with very different balances.

Checking progress takes one step. A saver divides the current retirement balance by annual pay and compares the result with the multiple for the nearest milestone age. The answer is a ratio, not a dollar figure. That makes it usable at any income level.

The milestones rise by two times annual income each decade after 30. The first checkpoint asks for one year of pay. Each later one adds two more years of pay to the total. The target grows by the same amount from one decade to the next.

The multiples measure the whole balance, not yearly contributions. Contribution rates are a separate question. A saver can reach a milestone through steady saving, investment growth or both.

The right number for any one saver depends on that person's retirement goals and expected lifestyle, according to the guide. A saver who expects to spend more in retirement may need more than the benchmark suggests.

Debt repayment is part of the picture for many savers. For people paying down loans, Ally's guidance is to handle both at once. That means continuing to save for retirement while staying on the loan repayment schedule. Under that approach, retirement saving does not wait until the debt is paid off.

Set aside 5% to 15% of every paycheck in a tax-advantaged retirement account. A 401(k), a traditional IRA and a Roth IRA all fit that description.

The guideline is a share of pay, not a dollar amount. Two savers at the same rate put away different sums if they earn different salaries. A raise lifts the dollar contribution even when the percentage stays the same.

The range is wide enough to fit very different household budgets. Rent, childcare, loan payments and other bills all draw on the same paycheck. A saver with heavy fixed costs may sit near the bottom of the range. One with more room may sit near the top. Either end meets the guideline.

Because it is stated as a percentage, the range applies at any age and income. A worker in a first job and one nearing retirement can use the same yardstick. What changes over a career is the number of dollars the percentage produces.

The guideline is not tied to one type of account. A 401(k) is sponsored by an employer, and contributions usually come out of each paycheck automatically. A traditional IRA and a Roth IRA are individual accounts opened outside the workplace. A worker whose employer offers no plan can still aim for the same range through an IRA.

The three accounts handle taxes differently. Traditional accounts generally defer income tax until money is withdrawn. Roth accounts take after-tax contributions, and qualified withdrawals come out tax-free. Which treatment fits better depends on each saver's own tax situation.

Savers who can't yet set aside 5% to 15% of each paycheck can start with a smaller share and raise it by 1% every year. The increases keep going until contributions land inside that range.

The approach fits a 401(k), a traditional IRA or a Roth IRA. All three are tax-advantaged accounts, and the yearly step works the same way in each.

Starting small lets a saver begin at a level the current budget can handle. The alternative is waiting until the full range feels affordable, with nothing going into the account in the meantime.

Even a modest first contribution builds the account and the habit together. In a 401(k), contributions come out of each paycheck through payroll deduction. The money reaches the plan before the saver can spend it. Saving becomes routine from the first pay period.

Some workplace plans can handle the yearly increase on their own through a feature called auto-escalation. Once it is turned on, the plan lifts the contribution rate on a set schedule. The saver does not need to submit a new contribution election each year.

Not every plan offers auto-escalation. The plan's own materials show whether the feature is available, how often the rate rises and whether the increases stop at a set level.

Without the feature, the saver makes the change by hand. That means updating the 401(k) contribution rate through the plan once a year. In an IRA, the same step means raising the amount sent into the account.

When an employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute at least enough of your own pay to collect all of it. That contribution level serves as a floor for anyone whose plan includes a match.

A match is money an employer adds to a worker's account based on what the worker contributes. The formula differs from plan to plan. Each plan's own documents set out the terms, including how much a worker must put in to receive the full amount.

The match is tied directly to the worker's own contributions. A worker who contributes below the match level leaves part of the employer's offered money unclaimed. Contributing at or above that level captures all of it.

The matched money also adds to the account without coming out of the worker's paycheck. Only the worker's own contribution reduces take-home pay.

The advice to capture the full match applies at every age. It is among the first steps for savers in their 20s, when the habit of regular contributions is just forming. It also appears among the general steps for improving a 401(k) balance at any point in a career.

Capturing the full match is a separate goal from a broader savings-rate target. A saver can keep contributions at or above the match level while working toward a higher overall rate. The two goals run side by side, with the match level as the minimum.

Workers become eligible for catch-up contributions at age 50. A catch-up contribution is money added to a retirement plan on top of the regular yearly contribution amount.

Eligibility begins at 50 and does not end when a saver leaves their 50s. It gives workers in the years before retirement a separate way to add to an account beyond the standard amount.

Timing affects how far that money can go. A dollar contributed at 50 has fewer years to grow before retirement than a dollar contributed in a person's 20s or 30s.

The Thrift Savings Plan, the retirement savings plan for federal employees, attaches a specific condition to catch-up contributions. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's National Finance Center describes the rule. A federal employee must expect to contribute the full yearly maximum of regular contributions, either to the TSP or to a comparable tax-deferred employer plan.

TSP catch-up contributions come out of the employee's pay. Under that requirement, catch-up money is added on top of a full regular contribution rather than replacing part of it.

That condition is a TSP rule for federal employees. It should not be read as a general 401(k) rule. Workers saving through a 401(k) can check the plan's documents or ask the plan administrator about catch-up eligibility and the current limits for that account.

In your 60s, a 401(k) shifts from building savings to paying for retirement. Ally recommends reviewing four decisions at this stage: withdrawal timing, rollovers, consolidation options and required minimum distributions.

The change rarely happens all at once. Many people in their early 60s are still putting money into their plans. At the same time, they are working out when and how to start drawing it down.

That overlap is why withdrawal timing comes first. The date of the first withdrawal marks when the account stops serving only as savings and starts serving as a source of income. The other three decisions all depend on it.

Rollovers and consolidation come next. Both concern where the money sits once it starts paying out. A saver with plans at several former employers can check whether combining them makes a withdrawal plan easier to manage.

Required minimum distributions are withdrawals the tax code requires from tax-deferred retirement accounts such as a traditional 401(k). Their timing has tax consequences. Putting off the first required minimum distribution means two distributions land in the same year, according to CNBC. Taking both in one year could push a retiree into a higher tax bracket.

Keeping costs low and leaving the money untouched help a 401(k) hold on to the balance it has built. Fees may come down when a saver consolidates accounts or chooses certain funds. Avoiding early withdrawals, where possible, keeps the savings in place for retirement.

Consolidation is one way to address fees. A worker who changes jobs can end up with retirement money spread across more than one account. Bringing those accounts together may lower the fees the saver pays. It also leaves fewer statements and investment mixes to track.

Fund selection is a second way. The funds inside a plan do not all carry the same costs. Choosing certain funds over others may reduce fees. How much a saver can cut this way depends on the plan and the options it offers.

The other half of protecting a balance is leaving it alone. The general guidance is to avoid taking money out before retirement whenever possible. An early withdrawal shrinks the balance. It can also bring early-withdrawal penalties.

Staying invested for the long haul works alongside that rule. Money left in the account keeps its full time to grow. Pulling out of investments early cuts that time short.

A periodic look at the account's asset allocation rounds out the routine. The check confirms the investment mix still fits the saver's goals.

Plan rules, fund costs and penalty terms differ from plan to plan. The right choices depend on each saver's circumstances. This information is general and is not investment or tax advice.
