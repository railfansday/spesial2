Karen Read’s Murder Trial Made Her Famous. A New Doc Explores the Story People Don’t Know

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

It’s been almost five years since Canton, Massachusetts resident Karen Read found the body of her police-officer boyfriend John O’Keefe unresponsive in the snow in the early hours of Jan. 29, 2022. Since that day, the circumstances around O’Keefe’s death and the subsequent investigation have turned Read into one of the most divisive murder defendants in the nation. Now, a new docuseries is tackling the internet-famous case and the behind-the-scenes details people don’t know.

Directed by Danielle Johnson Cutts and released Nov. 23, The Trials of Karen Read charts Read’s journey from unknown Canton resident to a true-crime mainstay. The fervor around Read’s trial was complicated by social media accounts alleging a police conspiracy was using Read as a scapegoat. The series’ biggest revelations focus less on chasing down the minute details of Read’s defense and more on building out a picture of O’Keefe that his family and friends say was lost in the fervor of Read’s case.

“The intention was always to make a well-rounded series, and we wanted to try and tell this story from multiple firsthand perspectives,” Johnson Cutts told Netflix’s Tudum. She added that O’Keefe’s friends and family only spoke to “center John [in the story] and remind what has now become a global audience of their pain: [the fact] that they have lost a father figure, a brother, and a son. And that has very, very much been lost in the melee of the trials of Karen Read.”

It was supposed to be a routine night. On Jan. 28, 2022, Read and O’Keefe met friends at Canton’s Waterfall Bar and Grille and the group decided to keep the hangout going at the home of Brian Albert, a fellow police officer. Read has maintained that she was driving under the influence and has a shoddy memory of the night. She told police she dropped O’Keefe off at the house and returned in the early hours when she realized he hadn’t made it home, where she found him unresponsive in a snowbank. Police have a different theory: They believe Read backed up into O’Keefe with her car and then left him to freeze to death. She was arrested and charged with second-degree murder, manslaughter while operating under the influence of alcohol, and leaving the scene of personal injury and death. Read’s first trial, which started on April 16, 2024, ended with a mistrial. “Johnny was the strong one and the pillar of strength,” O’Keefe’s brother, Paul O’Keefe, says in the documentary. “You want someone to be held accountable for taking a loved one from you. We just want justice for our loss.”

Read and her representatives declined to participate in the three-part docuseries, but it does include  interviews from O’Keefe’s friends, family, and investigators involved in the case. It also shows some never-before-seen footage, including police cameras, O’Keefe’s home videos, and unreleased audio from an interview Read did in 2023 with Boston Magazine.

“At the end of the day this was about John O’Keefe. And his death has become an absolute circus,” Jen McCabe, Albert’s sister-in-law, says in the docuseries. “People have profited off it and people have used it to destroy so many lives and the victim and his family, they lost. People should remember John and the amazing guy he was. Not Karen Read.”

Here are four things we learned from The Trials of Karen Read.

Read and O’Keefe dated briefly in 2004 but reconnected after the Covid-19 pandemic. When they started dating exclusively in 2020, Canton residents and close friends of O’Keefe say that the two seemed very happy with each other. O’Keefe was the legal guardian of his niece and nephew, Kayley and Patrick Furbush, and they quickly welcomed Read into the fold. “She treated me like a friend,” Kayley, now about 18, says in the documentary. “She’s funny. She was always laughing or making us laugh. She meshed really well with me and my brother. I definitely thought Karen was going to be someone I could talk to and relate with the way my friends got to with their moms. That was exciting.”

In the docuseries, O’Keefe’s friends say Read quickly fit into the group. “She was super nice. She was friendly. She was funny. She was outgoing,” McCabe says. “I loved her. I think John loved having a companion and someone to go out and have a good time with.”

McCabe, who was friends with both O’Keefe and Read, was with Read when she discovered O’Keefe’s body, and says the scene was immediately chaotic. But even after police left, O’Keefe’s family and friends had unanswered questions about how exactly he died. When police arrested Read on Feb. 1, their theory was that Read had accidentally hit O’Keefe with her car and was so drunk she didn’t notice. But further investigations, including a data pull from her car, revealed her back up cameras and sensors were completely functional, making an accidental strike unlikely.

Read’s case quickly caught the attention of online true-crime enthusiasts. One of the most prominent YouTubers who first posted about the case was Aidan Kearney, known as Turtleboy on YouTube. Kearney posted dozens of videos claiming Read was being set up by the Canton Police Department; popularizing alternative theories about O’Keefe’s death, including the possibility of a fight inside Albert’s home or a dog attack. (Kearney did not participate in the documentary). He often showed up to homes of O’Keefe’s friends, or confronted them in public places like sporting events. He also hosted several “Free Karen Read” rallies and protests, one of which involved marching protestors by the homes of Albert and McCabe. He was especially focused on Colin Albert, a teen who he believed might have fought with O’Keefe and accidentally killed him.

In the docuseries, Albert speaks out in a rare public interview and says Kearney’s video sent waves of targeted harassment towards him, both online and in person. “I am not the kid that these people were making me out as,” he says. “I am scared every single day about somebody coming up to me, whether they try to attack me, whether they try to do something horrible. I’m scared every day of my life for that.”

During Read’s first trial, her defense team uncovered multiple text messages from

Massachusetts State Police Trooper Michael Proctor, the lead investigator on her case, in which he referred to Read as a “bitch,” and a “wack job cunt,” adding “hopefully she kills herself.” This eroded trust in his investigation, and Proctor was fired in 2025 after months of suspension. On Nov. 10, Massachusetts State Police and Read settled a civil lawsuit alleging police misconduct during her arrest and trial.  The suit claimed the department had “an embedded culture of bigotry, misogyny, systemic failures” and cited Proctor’s text messages.

“I feel like I let down the O’Keefe family. I feel like I let down my family. I let down the state police, the DA’s office,” Proctor says in the docuseries, a rare look into his feelings on the public misstep. “I just feel like I let down so many people.”

Two jurors for Read’s second trial, identified only by their first names in the docuseries, say that Read’s case was so big that the jurors couldn’t avoid news about it before the trial. Many of the people on the jury were aware of Read’s case before they heard any testimony and several were familiar with the internet theories of a police coverup or conspiracy.

While the jury was torn on whether actual police corruption occurred, both jurors say that there were too many holes in the evidence and available testimony to prove that Read was guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. It’s a defining characteristic that not only determined Read’s fate but has continued to dog her case.

“I really did think, most likely, that she did it,” Janet, the other juror, says. “But I think by our legal system’s standards, she’s not guilty because there were so many different possibilities. He could’ve gone into the house, he could’ve gotten beaten up, he could’ve got hit by a plow. It’s almost like ‘doubt, doubt, doubt’ kinda hanging out there. It was a lot of doubt — it was more than reasonable doubt.”
