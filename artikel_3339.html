The Mosquito Bowl Won’t Let You Forget What It’s Really About

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

In this ScreenRant Cover Story, director Peter Berg and original author Buzz Bissinger break down the harrowing new war movie coming to Netflix.

On Christmas Eve in 1944, on the Pacific island of Guadalcanal, two regiments of US Marines training for the invasion of Okinawa played a game of football. That game is, ostensibly, the subject of The Mosquito Bowl, the new film from director Peter Berg that will be available to stream on Netflix from November 25, as well as the 2022 nonfiction book by Buzz Bissinger that inspired it. Considering the pairing of Berg and Bissinger once yielded Friday Night Lights, you might feel like you've heard enough to know what you're in for.

I can assure you, you have not. The Mosquito Bowl is in some ways about football – it is certainly about a handful of football players – but it's really a movie about war, and everything war costs. It follows four young men with hopes, dreams, and plenty of talent on the gridiron, who are diverted from whatever paths their lives may have taken and sent into a world of horrific violence and death. A world that is quite shockingly depicted.

"The goal of the movie was to grab the audience by the throat and make them feel something," Berg told ScreenRant's Liam Crowley in an interview alongside Bissinger. "To make something that feels like a participatory experience, not a spectator experience. I've had people see this film, and after it's like, 'Jesus Christ, I need to just go be quiet.'"

For this ScreenRant Cover Story, Berg and Bissinger talked us through what went into The Mosquito Bowl to create such a gripping watch, from its relationship to the true story and the real people that lived it to the quartet of actors and practical filmmaking that brought their experience to life.

Peter Berg and Buzz Bissinger have been looking for more opportunities to collaborate since Friday Night Lights. Creatively, they just clicked. "I don't think Buzz is the only genius writer out there," Berg told us, "but he is a genius writer, and he's my kind of genius writer."

"We knew each other, we knew each other's movements," Bissinger said. Plus, "we are actually legitimate second cousins."

Each time Bissinger embarked on a new writing project, he'd talk to Berg to see if it had big screen potential. But when he started work on what would become The Mosquito Bowl: A Game of Life and Death in World War II, he knew he had something that his cousin would be interested in.

BISSINGER: [In] 2018-2019, when I was beginning to write it, I told him about it. "Pete, this is perfect for you. It involves war, it involves football. You can blow a lot of things up. I think it's great."

Berg, for his part, got the same feeling, even before he had a chance to read the finished book.

BERG: I just could tell he was kind of on one. He was feeling it, he was doing intense research. I was getting the same energy from him that I got when I started reading Friday Night Lights and hearing that he had moved his family to Odessa, Texas, for a year and was living with this football team. I just sensed that. And knowing him as I do, and having read so many of his articles for Vanity Fair when he was just writing these incredible exposés on different aspects of American culture – I find him to be uniquely American in his perspective. I just knew that there was going to be something in the heart and the soul of what he was doing that was going to be very emotional for me and that I'd be able to connect to.

Bissinger's research came with some serious challenges. For one, of the 65 men who participated in the real Mosquito Bowl, "64 were dead by the time I got involved." Speaking to the last surviving player didn't prove all that helpful: "He honestly said, 'I don't remember what I did yesterday, so I definitely don't remember what I did 80 years ago.'"

So, he relied on what so often become the basis for such historical research, and which, he admits, have become "a lost, dying form": letters.

BISSINGER: These guys had a paper trail... The letters were simple, they were beautiful, they were homespun. You could feel their sense of loss; you could feel how much they missed home; you could feel how much they missed their fiancées. And there was a kind of gentle intimacy and beauty to them.

And then, you take the letters, and it's like any reporting: "What can I draw a string on here? How can I enhance it?" You look at a ton of articles, you look at film, you look at everything you possibly can, and interview other people – although there weren't that many, because they were so young when they died. You just interview as many people as you can, and keep drawing that string, and then you make the decision. "All right, I got enough."

Over time, a story took shape that homed in on four of the game's participants: John McLaughry, who was an All-American as a player at Brown University; Dave Schreiner, a future College Football Hall of Famer from Wisconsin; Bob Bauman, Schreiner's Badger teammate; and Tony Butkovich, who led the nation in rushing in his year at Purdue.

Hopefully, if you see this film, you are going to feel like you are in it.

Part of the choice to focus on these four was the drama in their stories – it will come as no surprise, watching the movie, that not all of them make it home from the war. But it was also "a matter of access."

"John McLaughry was a big star at Brown... His family, who I contacted, had kept everything – everything. Every letter, every report card, everything John had done in his life."

BISSINGER: Bauman was very lucky. I didn't get letters from Bauman until the last six months. I had written the book and his family said, "You know what? We have a foot locker from Bob we have never opened." This is his niece. "We've never opened it." It was his uniform, there were hundreds of letters, there were game balls... That's one of the delicious moments of reporting. You don't expect it, you have no idea, and they call and they say, "Well, by the way, you may be interested, we got hundreds of letters." What? Are you kidding me? I'm dying for that!

Both Berg and Bissinger admit it took a little time to crack the dense book's transition to film. But, eventually, Berg latched onto a story about the loss of innocence in war and a beautiful, fleeting moment when that innocence was recaptured.

Berg's vision for The Mosquito Bowl was "to make something that feels immersive... meaning hopefully, if you see this film, you are going to feel like you are in it." One unexpected source of inspiration in defining this approach? Gaspar Noé's Climax.

BERG: I can't recommend that film, but I will recommend that film if you're up for a challenge. It's a very intense movie, but it's experiential. I wanted to hit at that level. And as part of that, let's do it real.

Let's get real sets. Let's use real bombs, real flamethrowers, as safely as we could. Let's shoot 360 degrees, all that sort of stuff. And we were able to make things feel pretty visceral by doing that.

The impact of The Mosquito Bowl's practical, immersive approach to filming is felt from the very first combat sequence, which also happens to be one of the war's most traumatic. Our four protagonists have arrived in the Pacific and are preparing for their first taste of battle on Tarawa Atoll; they wonder to each other whether several hours of bombardment from US ships will have even left them anyone to fight. But as they approach to make their landing, their Higgins boat runs aground on a shallow reef, still a good distance from shore.

They barely have the chance to realize they've become sitting ducks before enemy fire comes raining down on them.

BERG: These guys have not had that much training, but they have been trained. They think they know what's going to happen. They think they're going to make the beach and fight, and they think that the air superiority and the bombardment from the battleships has softened the enemy. And from the moment it starts, they hit the reefs and everything goes to hell. That, for me, was certainly one of the moments that really pierced me when I read the book.

BISSINGER: I've read a lot about Tarawa. I can tell you, that is depicted [in the movie] to a tee. It is totally accurate. They knew the tide might not be high enough, but they let these kids go out anyway in these boats, and they became sitting ducks. They got hung up on the coral. All of a sudden, they're jumping off these boats. They got their rifles over their heads, because they don't want to get [them] wet, and they're being strafed and mortared by Japanese who were dug into the beach. At least 500 men died, and it was one of, maybe the greatest bloodbath and greatest strategic error that the Americans made during World War II.

To give all that visceral violence the emotional anchor it needs requires the right actors to play the four leads, and at least one decision came naturally. "I think it was Bill Skarsgård first," Berg recalled, "just because I really liked him." He plays Bob Bauman as a tough but upbeat man whose transition from football field to war zone feels the most natural of the group. Though he wouldn't seek it out, he is a little more comfortable with killing, something Skarsgård lends a slightly unsettling quality.

Nicholas Galitzine, who plays John McLaughry, and Ray Nicholson, who plays Tony Butkovich, both came through the audition process. "I was seeing so many actors, and I saw Ray, and I saw Nick... and they both kind of popped for me." Galitzine brings a poise that sells McLaughry as an athlete-scholar, and as the one most inclined to see the poetry in the world, his feelings run closer to the surface. Tony Butkovich, meanwhile, flashes that Nicholson family smile, and it tells you all you need to know about how he came to earn the nickname Touchdown Tony.

Before either of them came on board, however, Berg knew he'd found his Dave Schreiner. He just couldn't tell Netflix yet.

BERG: Right around [the time I cast Bill Skarsgård], I went and saw Sunset Boulevard on Broadway, and I saw Tom Francis. And I went back the next night. I saw it twice. He was so good in it. But I couldn't really offer him the role, because nobody knew who he was. I have a bit of say in casting, but if I'm just telling my bosses at Netflix, "It's Tom Francis," and they're like, "Huh? Who?" I had to read him, but I kind of knew he'd be next...

To see this young kid stand up there and command the stage like he did, that kid has real star power. I mean, I think they all do, but Francis, anyone who's seen Sunset Boulevard knows, has got this sequence where he just walks out into Times Square singing – it's mind-blowing. He's a very, very confident man.

Francis' performance balances sturdiness and vulnerability. Bissinger described the real Schreiner as "a natural," saying that "if you wanted to invent an All-American, Dave Schreiner would be it," and this quality comes through on-screen. He has a strong moral center that makes him the kind of character others would naturally follow. But he's also plagued by doubt, which Francis allows to peek through in very affecting ways.

The Mosquito Bowl cast also features a couple of notable cameos. NFL fans may recognize Pat McAfee, the former punter turned sports analyst, as the primary drill instructor yelling at our heroes as they train on Parris Island. He deflates a football with a bayonet, and even gets to recite the Rifleman's Creed.

Right there alongside him, though? Buzz Bissinger.

BISSINGER: Of course, Pete being who he is, I get to the set, and he says, "What are you doing here?" I say, "Pete, well, I wrote the book, and I'm observing, and I think you know that." He says, "No, get to costume, get to wardrobe! What are you doing?" I said, "Pete, why?" He said, "You're playing a marine drill sergeant." I said, "All right, fine."

I get to costume, they cut my hair, they fit me in an outfit. It was very easy for me to do, because all you have to do is tell people to shut the f**k up. That's all I said. And I was so good, I made the cut. That's one of the proudest moments of my life.

BERG: He stole the scene from Pat. We had to tone him down.

BERG: Once Buzz got going, he started barking at a level of intensity that McAfee couldn't keep up with.

Peter Berg also appears much later in the film, as a commanding officer on Guadalcanal who addresses the men in very frank, chilling terms. That small part was intended for Yellowstone franchise boss Taylor Sheridan, who was going to do it until "he realized how far of a flight it was from Texas to the Gold Coast." Berg was his own second choice, but he has no regrets about playing the role. He explained to SR that he believes acting in his films actually makes him a better filmmaker.

BERG: For me, acting in a film, I always try to give myself a little part, and I just like, five, ten years down the road, looking back and seeing, "Oh wow, I remember what I looked like and what I was feeling..." And the other thing, for me as a director, is I started off as an actor, and I haven't really acted in a long time substantially. And a director can forget or not know what the experience of acting is. And it's a uniquely weird job. It's stressful, and it can bring up insecurities, and you don't really feel like you have a lot of agency...

And as a director, sometimes I forget that, and I just might not be paying enough attention to a specific actor if they're struggling, or they want a little extra attention. Playing a part, and going to set as an actor, and going to the little trailer – which is basically a prison cell that you sit in, waiting for them to bring you into hair and makeup – going through that process humbles me, and reminds me of what I'm asking of the actors, because I forget it sometimes. So, I think it actually makes me more empathetic and probably a better director. My actors would maybe disagree with that, but I think it's true.

Not everything that happens in The Mosquito Bowl exactly mirrors history. Take the game itself: In real life, the two teams of Marines played to a scoreless tie. Staying true to that would've been tantamount to cinematic malpractice.

In approaching the adaptation, Berg made the distinction between 'fact' and 'truth.' Some facts were "critical" – where these men died, for example – and it was important that the movie maintain them. "Then I had to bring them together in ways that [were] not necessarily accurate to how they really all came together," he explained. "I had to condense timelines. They were in different battles, and I had to condense it and put them in one, often, certainly with Tarawa." Or, as he put it, "I changed some of the facts, but I kept the truth."

...you will never understand what these kids went through until you see this film.

Throughout, he trusted Bissinger to be his guiding light.

BERG: There's a line of credibility if you're going to say this is based on a true story. And I know that Buzz is going to be my audience, and he's got a real nose for this kind of thing, and he's going to be protective. I would send him pages and I would talk to him about it – and he jokes and says, "Well, if you're the writer of a book, and you agree to a movie, you got to take the money and shut the f**k up."

There's a little truth to that, but there's not, because the truth is also he would tell me if he thought I was crossing a line or doing something that was offensive or fundamentally violating the authenticity of what he's done. He'd tell me. He wouldn't be able to keep his mouth shut.

BISSINGER: I thought what Pete did – and I see this a lot in the film, having seen it now several times – is he took a basis in fact, and then he would have to mold it in a certain direction, because he's got a vision. He's the director. But I think a lot of it was intact. He even put a parrot into it, which I was shocked, because there's a little scene involving a parrot. Watching it was amazing.

Once creative license had its way, the throughline linking every scene was the unique way that football and war intersected for these young men. The movie's ideas emerge from how these two things spark against each other. According to Bissinger, this, too, is a truth extracted from the facts.

BISSINGER: Football played a curious role... The Navy saved college football because the Navy believed in football as the single best source of combat training for officers. What does it teach you? It teaches you the values that we talk about all the time. It teaches you discipline. It teaches you work ethic. It teaches you how to play when you're hurt. It teaches you about teamwork. It teaches you about realizing your dreams. So, it had a place in World War II.

And of course, the Mosquito Bowl was this unbelievable, bizarre, surreal, serendipitous, incredible football game that was really played on the island of Guadalcanal between not just those four guys, but some of the best college players in the country.

Of the 65 who played, 20 were drafted or would be drafted by the pros. There were seven captains. There were three All-Americans. The best receiver in the country was at the Mosquito Bowl. The best sophomore running back was at the Mosquito Bowl. You had talent. They would've beaten, if you put them together, these regiments, NFL teams. And so there was that moment of football.

But this is not a football book. This is a book about kids who play the game, who love the game, who are getting a ton of exposure, and whether they like it or not, they're going to war. This book is about one thing: war. It's sanitized all the time. War is about murder, war is about death, war is about brutality, and you will never understand what these kids went through until you see this film.

Like the book, The Mosquito Bowl is first and foremost about (thoroughly unsanitized) war. The characters are dragged through some of the worst that humanity has to offer, and the audience right along with them. But as much as the movie catches many of Bissinger's observations about how war and football may be complementary, Berg also uses football as shorthand for everything war is not.

The film opens with a quote from Herbert Hoover: "Older men declare war. But it is youth that must fight and die, and it is youth who must inherit the tribulation, the sorrow, and the triumphs." As much as football may prepare someone for the demands of being a soldier, it is also a game that emphasizes the youth of these men on screen. And, paradoxically, it is also inextricably tied to the futures that these talented players could have had. It's both the innocence and the potential that war costs its participants, all wrapped up in one.

For Berg, the Mosquito Bowl itself is the one moment this all shines through. In the historical record, it may be just one blip on the road to the Battle of Okinawa, which serves as this film's heartrending climax. But it's also this story's reason for being.

"It was the heart and soul of the book," Berg said, "even though it was a small moment, it was a powerful moment. It's what made it all kind of click for me, that you introduce trauma, you go deeper, you go deeper, you go deeper, you know you're headed for real trauma, Okinawa, and there's this moment of respite.

"This moment where these kids just get to be happy, as Buzz says, and just be kids and play."
