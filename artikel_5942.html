Control of Congress is at stake today. And, judge to consider motion in Clancy case

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Election Day has arrived. This campaign has the biggest price tag in U.S. history. A record number of lawmakers are stepping down, setting the stage for Democrats to potentially regain control of Congress. All the while, President Trump's popularity is at an all-time low. Here's everything you need to know about today's election, and four types of House races to watch as results come in. NPR has your guide for how to watch the results and when polls close, hour by hour.

Iran has been leveraging the U.S. midterms in its pressure campaign to push Trump back to negotiations. The elections could signal a possible return to conflict with the U.S. and Israel. The Associated Press reports that the U.S. conducted defensive strikes on Iranian targets in the Strait of Hormuz last night, including Qeshm Island, which is a key launch point for Iran's strikes. While NPR could not independently confirm the details, Iran reported explosions on the island. This pattern of strikes has occurred several times in recent months, coinciding with heightened Iranian attacks on shipping vessels in the Strait.

A Massachusetts judge is considering a motion to dismiss the case against Lindsay Clancy. Plymouth Superior Court Judge William Sullivan will announce his decision later this month. In court yesterday, Clancy's attorney requested that the judge dismiss the case or initiate a new trial. Clancy faces first-degree murder charges for the 2023 deaths of her three young children in their Duxbury home. The trial ended in a mistrial in September when jurors failed to reach a unanimous verdict. The Plymouth County district attorney's office has not disclosed its next steps, including whether to seek a new trial or pursue lesser charges. In a recent motion, prosecutors stated they aim to "pursue justice" for Clancy's children.

For half a century, America hasn't had enough special education teachers. It's a shortage that's only gotten worse as the number of students with disabilities grows rapidly — 8.2 million according to the most recent federal count, or about 1 in 6 students.

Research explains some of why these roles are so hard to fill: These teachers enjoy working with students but spend more time on legally required paperwork and tasks other than teaching. They often don't get paid more despite having more responsibilities than many other teachers. And because there's such a widespread shortage, the teachers who stay in the profession are often handed a bigger load.

NPR wanted to go beyond the research and hear directly from educators, so NPR senior producer Lauren Migaki and I have been exchanging voice memos with more than a dozen teachers from big and small districts around the country. So far, they've shared tales of joy when a student found success, tearful moments when they felt like they had failed, and waking up before dawn to make sure they were prepared.

For example, Jeffrey Carroll, who teaches in Newport News, Va., said last year, he was teaching students across three grade levels and multiple subjects: second, fourth and fifth. It meant he was constantly running from one classroom to the next. "I just didn't feel like I was teaching, I was never in a class long enough to be a meaningful part of it." This year, he was relieved to only be teaching across two grade levels. "It's been good to support my students in the classroom. I get to do some co-teaching. The lesson plans come to life."

Lauren and I will stay in touch with these teachers throughout the rest of the school year and visit some in their classrooms to get a better understanding of what it takes to do this essential job.
