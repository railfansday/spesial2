Why buyers may get more from seller concessions than a price cut

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

With mortgage rates above 7% and pending sales down year over year, buyers often have leverage to ask for seller credits. For many loans, credits for closing costs or rate buydowns can deliver more value than a similar price cut.   AI Summary

Contract signings dropped 7.3% in September, pushing the National Association of Realtors’ Pending Home Sales Index to its lowest level since the index began in 2001. Signings were down 11.3% from a year earlier. October brought no relief. Redfin’s weekly data for the four weeks ending Nov. 1 shows pending sales down 16.4% year over year, the lowest level in its records, with declines in every major metro it tracks.

With mortgage rates still above 7%, the buyers who remain in the market are dealing with sellers who need them far more than they did a year ago. Most of those buyers will spend their leverage on the asking price. The instinct makes sense, and it often leaves the more valuable concession unclaimed. Buyers should approach this negotiation as a financing decision. That is usually where the larger savings sit.

The national numbers hide a market that is splitting by region. In Redfin’s data from late October, Seattle, San Diego, Atlanta, Denver and Las Vegas posted the steepest pending-sales declines, each down between 22.6% and 30.5% from a year earlier. Sale prices have already fallen in Austin, Seattle and Fort Lauderdale. In September, Redfin found that nearly one in five contracts was canceled in Jacksonville, Tampa, Las Vegas, San Antonio and Phoenix.

Sellers in those markets are watching listings age and deals unravel. They will take a request for closing costs seriously.

San Francisco is another story, with its median sale price up 17.3% year over year. Nassau County, NY, and Montgomery County, PA, are still classified as seller’s markets, and contracts there rarely fall through. Buyers in those areas can ask for concessions, but they should expect a shorter answer.

For everyone else, local days on market and the share of listings with price cuts offer the best read on how hard to push. Nationally, 21.2% of listings carried a price drop in the latest Redfin data. A home that has sat well past the local median, with a cut or two behind it, belongs to a seller who is ready to talk.

Consider a $600,000 home bought with 20% down at a 7.25% rate. A $10,000 price reduction shrinks the down payment by $2,000 and the loan to $472,000, bringing monthly principal and interest from roughly $3,271 to $3,217. The buyer saves about $55 a month.

If the buyer takes a $10,000 seller credit toward closing costs instead, the price holds at $600,000. Compared with the price cut, they walk away from closing with about $8,000 more in their pocket. At $55 a month, the price reduction needs around 12 years to catch up. Few buyers keep the same loan that long, and a buyer who refinances when rates come down will likely never reach the breakeven point.

Sellers often prefer the credit too. Their net proceeds come out about the same, and a credit keeps the recorded sale price intact for the comps that will shape their neighbors’ values. That makes it an easier concession to win.

Price, however, is still important. A lower purchase price reduces the interest paid over the life of the loan and starts the buyer with more equity. With rates this high, few buyers will keep the same loan for 15 years without refinancing, but those who plan to could reasonably weigh price more heavily. The home also has to appraise at the contract price. If it comes in low, a concession won’t close the gap, and the buyer may need a price reduction anyway.

Closing-cost credits are the simplest use of a concession, but buyers focused on the monthly payment can put the same money toward a rate buydown. A temporary 2-1 buydown lowers the rate by two percentage points in the first year and one in the second. Discount points lower it for the life of the loan. In the example above, $10,000 in points could take roughly half a percentage point off the rate, depending on lender pricing. That works out to about $160 a month, nearly triple what the price cut delivers.

Buyers can also ask for both. Going back to the $600,000 home, suppose the buyer wants to pay $560,000. Asking the seller for a $40,000 credit is a big request, and it would likely exceed the buyer’s total closing costs. A $30,000 price reduction paired with the same $10,000 concession gets the buyer to their number, and a seller is more likely to agree to two moderate asks than one large one. How that concession gets used depends on where the buyer feels the pressure. Buyers stretching to cover cash at closing may get more from credits, while those with a tight monthly budget may be better served by a buydown.

Credits also have formal limits. Conventional loans cap seller contributions at 3% to 9% of the price depending on the down payment, and FHA allows up to 6%. Any credit beyond the buyer’s eligible closing costs goes unused, and non-QM programs vary widely by lender. Buyers should structure financing before the offer goes in so they know exactly how much credit they can use.

The most motivated sellers right now are the ones watching their listings sit in markets where buyers keep walking away. What they want most is a contract that makes it to closing. A buyer who arrives with the financing already worked out can offer that certainty and should expect the seller to pay for it.

Buyers who negotiate only on price in this market are likely shortchanging themselves. So are buyers who treat price and concessions as an either/or choice. In the few metros where things remain competitive, the leverage is yet to materialize. Across most of the country, buyers have room to ask for both.
