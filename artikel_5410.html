Hackers breach Air Force's Cloud One: What's at risk and what's still unknown

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Anonymous sources say hackers breached Cloud One, the Air Force's flagship cloud. Six slides separate what is confirmed from what is still unknown.

For years, the U.S. military has moved software off scattered computer systems and onto shared cloud platforms that are easier to manage but give attackers one larger target. The Air Force's flagship version is Cloud One, and hackers have now reportedly gotten inside it, Reuters reports. Cloud One's leaders had claimed a 100% success rate at repelling penetration attempts in a 2023 FedScoop commentary, according to the same report.

The breach's investigators have asked Cyber Command to help measure the harm. No one has established who the hackers are, how far they went or whether they took any data.

Cloud One brings Microsoft $MSFT, Amazon $AMZN, Oracle $ORCL and Alphabet $GOOGL under a single Air Force platform, so users reach all four companies' computing through one system. The Air Force started it in 2017, and Leidos has held the main contract to run it since 2025, replacing SAIC. Nothing on the record connects that change of contractor to the intrusion.

One platform means one place to manage, and one place to break into.

The Air Force's munitions-tracking software, the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, runs on Cloud One and keeps count of weapons at more than 200 sites around the world. So does Cloud-Based Command and Control, which pulls radar and other feeds into a single air-defense picture of North America. Nothing reported so far places the intruders inside either system.

These systems mark the outer limit of what the breach could cost.

The Air Force has paused plans to move back-office software onto Cloud One and frozen updates to the applications already there, so teams can't patch them even as major new software flaws surface across the industry. Some users have also lost administrator access. The Air Force said it "does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems."

The clearest damage so far is a platform the Air Force can't fully use.

One of five unnamed sources said the intruders used a software flaw to reach Cloud One's control plane, the layer that runs the whole platform. Access at that level could let an intruder check which aircraft are ready to fly and follow where spare parts are being shipped. No specific flaw has been identified, so no one outside the investigation can confirm it has been fixed.

The control-plane claim is the gravest risk in the breach, and it rests on a single source.
