The staggering emissions of AI data centers, according to a new study

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The total emissions of U.S. hyperscale data centers measured by Harvard researchers rival the emissions of some states and even some countries

The Amazon Web Services IAD10 data center in Sterling, Virginia. (Lexi Critchett/Bloomberg via Getty Images)

A little more than 400 hyperscale data centers in the U.S. produced about 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide between May 2024 and April 2025, according to new research, rivaling the carbon emissions of some states and even some countries. That is two to three times higher than an earlier estimate for 2018, researchers led by the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and the Harvard Data Science Initiative said in a new study.

The team published the study on Monday in Nature Sustainability. Hyperscale data centers are the largest class of data centers, supporting cloud computing, large language model training and the deployment of AI tools.

Depending on operating scenarios, the facilities drew anywhere from 68 to 99 terawatt-hours of power, the researchers said. The upper estimate approaches the annual consumption of states such as Indiana, Michigan and Tennessee. The demand equaled about 1.8% of total U.S. electricity consumption. The total is almost as much as the annual emissions of Bolivia or Denmark, according to The New York Times. Reuters described it as equal to Denmark's annual emissions.

About 54% of the electricity powering the facilities came from fossil fuels, mainly natural gas (38%) and coal (15%). About a quarter of the electricity was in grid regions dirtier than average. Virginia, Oregon, Ohio, and Iowa together represented a majority of the electricity demand the study covered.

The researchers used satellite imagery to build the list of facilities. They then used Environmental Protection Agency data to assign emissions to each one according to which power plants fed its regional grid.

"Data centers are growing incredibly fast, driven largely by AI, and decisions about where to build them and how to power them are being made right now," lead author Gianluca Guidi, a postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Biostatistics, said in a statement.

Guidi said the figures are a snapshot. "The data center boom hasn't slowed down since our study period ended in April 2025," he said. "That means the electricity use and carbon footprint we're reporting could already be substantially larger now in 2026."

The study has limits. There was not enough available data to examine every hyperscale data center in the U.S., and the data did not let the researchers separate general-purpose computing from AI-specialized operations at each facility. The study also did not directly measure health effects, which corresponding author Francesca Dominici, director of the Harvard Data Science Initiative, said is a next step.

"Decisions about where data centers go and how they're powered are critical environmental and public health decisions, and they should be made with good data," Dominici said in a statement.

The findings come as data centers have become a political issue. Governors who ran on pausing new projects won in Texas, Ohio and Georgia in this month's midterm elections. Earlier this year, New York froze most new large-scale data center construction in July, and Texas directed regulators to pause approvals of pending projects in August.
