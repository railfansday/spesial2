Rebecca Connors, 49, is an independent, but today, she said, she voted “very Democratic”. Speaking outside her polling location during the after-work crush in Rochester, a small town in New Hampshire’s seacoast region, she said she voted blue to send a message of disapproval to Donald Trump, whose favorability is at a record low in the Granite State.

Trump was the first reason Connors gave for going out to vote, saying, “He’s wreaking havoc.” But she also cited the top issues driving many voters to the polls, like the cost of living. “I want the economy to come back down again. I want to be able to get gas for below $3.50,” said Connors. “We work hard every day, and yet, we can’t get ahead.”

She added that it was important for the democratic process to put some “checks and balances” on Trump, and she wanted elected officials who would question him, “as opposed to getting down on their hands and knees and kissing his feet.”

Connors was also concerned about her LGBTQ+ loved ones, who she fears are more vulnerable under Republican control, and about ICE, which she believes should be disbanded.

After this morning’s announcement that the Trump administration was sending Department of Justice monitors to all 50 states, Connors was even afraid to vote, citing the current political environment. “There’s no reason you should be doing that,” she said of the decision to send in monitors.

Greetings from the Austin Recreation Center in Austin, Texas, where Gina Hinojosa, the Democratic candidate for governor, greeted supporters clutching “Save our schools” signs to highlight cuts to public education. No Democrat has won a Texas gubernatorial election since Ann Richards in 1990 , but Hinojosa, running against the incumbent Republican governor Greg Abbott, insisted that “everything is different” this time. She told reporters: “For one, Greg Abbott had never seen a poll that he was losing ever before when he ran for governor. I don’t know, there’s been like 15? I mean, so many. This election is within the margin of error and it is because Greg Abbot has done so much damage to public schools. “It is because what he has done to public schools is one example of a governor doing the bidding of the moneyed interests and not the people of Texas. Datacentres are another example. There are people all across the state very angry at this governor who no longer fears the voters and voters are fighting back in this election.” Asked how critical the Latino vote will be in this campaign, Hinojosa, 52, a civil rights and labour lawyer, replied: “It’s huge. Look, we know that when Beto [O’Rourke] ran in 2018 and came so close as a Democrat to winning [a Senate race], he got 64% of the Latino vote. When I get that I win because there’s more of us in the voting population than there were then. “Latino voters are angry about what we’re all angry about. They’re angry about schools. They’re angry about this high cost economy, which is harder to make it today than it was 12 years ago before Greg Abbott. They’re angry about the corruption and they are angry about being treated less Texan, less American than everybody else.That’s why you see Latinos turning out in big numbers.”

It’s almost 6pm ET on Tuesday 3 November, and the first round of polls in the US midterm elections are about to close. After months of campaigning, debate and analysis, Americans have been casting their ballots in these critical elections that will take the political temperature of the nation halfway through Donald Trump’s second term – and determine the trajectory of the remainder of his presidency.

Americans, who have already voted early in record numbers and are turning out in numbers significantly outstripping the totals from the 2022 midterms, will decide the balance of power in Congress and, therefore, Trump’s ability to advance his agenda for the next two years.

With the US president almost unprecedentedly unpopular, Democrats are aiming for what would be stunning victories in seats where, for decades, turning blue would have been unthinkable. Trump is not on the ballot, but this election is very much a referendum on the direction of the country under his Republican party.

Results will soon begin trickling in for what will be a defining moment for American democracy, so stick with us as we bring you all the latest news, analyses and commentary as it all unfolds.
