Off-Grid Messaging with MeshCore: How to Text When the Cell Towers Go Dark

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A beginner's guide to MeshCore LoRa mesh radios: how they work, which hardware to buy, and how to fix the classic problem where you can hear the mesh but it can't hear you.

I have a weakness for radios that nobody needs permission to use. In a previous life, I braved 5°F winter days scanning public Wi-Fi all over downtown Helsinki, warmed by nothing but spiked glögg. Wi-Fi taught me that a little unlicensed spectrum and a lot of clever engineering can change the world. Ham radio taught me that the best communications gear is the kind that still works when everything else has given up. So when I found a $40 radio that could text people miles away, with no towers, no SIM card and no license, I had a new hobby before I'd finished reading the product page.

It also exposed a blind spot in my emergency kit. Water: check. Forty pounds of freeze-dried lasagna: check. Hand-crank flashlight that doubles as a phone charger: check. And under Communications, a phone. A phone that is, in practice, a very expensive flashlight once the nearest cell tower loses power.

Off-grid mesh networking fills that gap: cheap long-range radios that form their own text network, with no carrier in sight. I spent the past couple of months on the Bay Area's MeshCore network with a handful of radios, one SDR dongle, and more patience than I knew I had. For ten frustrating days I could hear the whole mesh, and it couldn't hear me. This guide covers what I wish I'd known on day one: why people do this, how MeshCore works, what to buy, and how to fix the problem almost every beginner hits.

Modern communication is a long chain of things that need electricity and a business model: towers, backhaul fiber, data centers, and the goodwill of your carrier. Break any link and your $1,200 slab of glass is reduced to playing the offline games you never installed.

After Hurricane Helene in September 2024, parts of western North Carolina lost power and cell service for days, in places for weeks. In April 2025, the Iberian blackout took mobile service down across Spain and Portugal within hours, once tower backup batteries ran dry.

In the Bay Area, I don't have to imagine it. We have earthquake faults under our feet and fire seasons that bring planned PG&E power shutoffs. On October 27, 2019, high winds, a forest fire and a PG&E shutoff took out my power, internet and cable TV all at once. The Mercury News wrote about it, and I ended up building a cardboard-and-tinfoil antenna to watch Sunday football over the air.

Mesh radios don't care. They run for days on a small battery, indefinitely on a palm-sized solar panel, and relay messages through each other instead of through a tower.

First, a naming hazard. LoRa (lowercase a, "Long Range") is a radio modulation owned by Semtech. It has nothing to do with LoRA, the AI fine-tuning trick. I spend a lot of time around AI people, and I can confirm that search engines find this hilarious.

LoRa uses chirp spread spectrum: each symbol is a tone sweeping across a slice of spectrum. Because the receiver knows the shape of the chirp, it can dig a signal out from below the noise floor. That's how a radio putting out about as much power as a phone's Wi-Fi reaches miles instead of yards.

The trade-off is bandwidth: a few hundred bits to a few kilobits per second. Enough for a text or a GPS position, never enough for a photo of your dog. LoRa runs in unlicensed ISM bands: 915 MHz in the Americas and Australia/New Zealand, 868 MHz in Europe and the UK. Sub-gigahertz signals pass through trees, walls and San Francisco fog far better than Wi-Fi. Karl the Fog is not the problem. The hills are.

A cell network is a star: lose the tower and everyone in range goes silent. A mesh has no center. Radios pass messages along, hop by hop, until they arrive.

Cellular and Wi-Fi depend on someone else's infrastructure. Ham radio doesn't, but it takes an exam, real skill, and (in most countries) a ban on encryption. LoRa mesh is what I'd hand to my non-radio-nerd family: no license, encrypted, days on a battery, and it looks like a messaging app.

Both big projects run on the same cheap LoRa boards. They're different firmware with opposite ideas about who should relay.

The honest verdict: for a group that moves around together and wants something that works out of the box, pick Meshtastic. If your city has MeshCore repeaters, or you want to help build permanent regional coverage, pick MeshCore.

Around the Bay, the second case is winning.

That's what a mesh looks like when it delivers reliably enough for real conversation. The Bay Area MeshCore crowd has put hundreds of repeaters on rooftops, hilltops and the occasional tree. One of my test messages was heard by 66 of them, across a span of 238 miles. The two networks can't talk to each other, so the right choice is whichever one your neighbors run. Mine run MeshCore.

MeshCore's design fits on a napkin. There are three kinds of node:

Every node has a cryptographic key pair, and the public key is its identity: no accounts, servers or phone numbers. Nodes introduce themselves with an advert, a signed packet carrying their public key, a name, and optionally a location. Repeaters advert every 12 hours. Companions advert only when you tell them to, so press that button when you're new, or nobody will know you exist.

A flood packet gets repeated by anyone who hasn't already; a direct one only by the next repeater on its path. Repeaters forward your words but can't read them.

Alice in Berkeley texts Bob in San Jose. Both have advertised, so each has the other's public key.

If the route breaks (say, a repeater loses power), Alice's radio retries three times and floods the last attempt to find a new route.

Group channels always flood, because "everyone in #test" has no single destination. A channel is just a shared key. Public uses a well-known key, and a #hashtag channel's key is derived from its name, so anyone who guesses the name can read along. Treat both as billboards.

Two honest caveats. The cryptography is lean (tiny authentication tags, no forward secrecy): fine for a camping trip, not for a nation-state threat model. And with only 256 possible 1-byte repeater IDs, busy regions get collisions, which is why the Bay Area mesh is moving to 2-byte IDs.

Check official support before you buy. My first rig was an M5Stack Cardputer ADV, a credit-card-sized computer with a keyboard, plus the snap-on Cap LoRa-1262 radio with GPS. Meshtastic supports it officially. MeshCore didn't, so I ended up writing my own MeshCore firmware for it (it's on M5Burner as Bruce's MeshCore, if you have one). Fun hobby, not a beginner's path. Before buying anything, find your board in the MeshCore web flasher. If it isn't listed, you're on your own.

Decide how you want to type. Bluetooth companions are small boards paired to the MeshCore phone app: cheap, simple, and the app is good. But your phone becomes part of the system, and in a real outage you'll want its battery for other things. Standalone devices have their own screen and keyboard and need no phone at all. That's what I'd want in a go-bag.

Battery life depends on the chip. Radios built on Nordic's nRF52 chips sip power, often running a week or more on a small battery. ESP32 radios are more capable but hungrier: with the screen off, my Cardputer draws 120–150 mA, so its 1,500 mAh battery lasts 10–12 hours, not days. For something that sits in a bag listening, nRF52 wins.

GPS costs more than battery life. On my radio, the running GPS receiver raised the LoRa noise floor by about 4 dB, making it measurably deafer. Keep GPS off unless you actually need your position.

Newest isn't always best. I tested a brand-new radio built on Semtech's fourth-generation LR2021 chip (most radios use the second-generation SX1262). On a cheap SDR dongle it showed two problems: it sent the start of each packet about 25 dB too quietly, and it sat 23 kHz off frequency. The first turned out to be a firmware bug, which I've since patched in my own build. The second happens because the board has no TCXO (a temperature-compensated crystal), which matters on MeshCore's narrow 62.5 kHz channels. Prefer boards with a TCXO, and let other people beta-test the shiny new silicon.

Get an external antenna connector. You'll want to upgrade the antenna (see section 7), so look for SMA or RP-SMA, not a tiny internal antenna.

Buy two radios. It sounds greedy, but a second, known-good radio is the best diagnostic tool you'll ever own. It's how I proved my problem wasn't my hardware.

If I were starting over, I'd get a LilyGO T-Deck Plus with the community-built WadaMesh firmware, which is much nicer to use than the official T-Deck build. It looks like a BlackBerry that took up survivalism: thumb keyboard, trackball, 2.8-inch touchscreen, GPS, a 2,000 mAh battery and an SX1262 radio with a TCXO. No phone required. It was also my known-good reference through every test in this article.

Buy the right band for your region: 915 MHz for the Americas and ANZ, 868 MHz for Europe and the UK. Buying the wrong one is the hobby's most common rite of passage.

Notice the pattern: almost every entry says "upgrade the antenna." It's usually the single biggest improvement you can make, and the first fix for the lopsided-link problem in section 7. Check that the connector (SMA, RP-SMA or N-type) matches your device, or order an adapter too.

Pro tip: never power on a LoRa radio without an antenna attached. Transmitting into nothing can fry the radio chip, a lesson almost everyone learns once.

MeshCore lives or dies by repeaters, so check there's a network before you spend a cent.

A few radios, like the Wio Tracker L1 Pro, arrive with MeshCore installed; most ship with Meshtastic. Either way, flash the latest release. No soldering and no compiler, just a USB cable and Chrome. Open flasher.meshcore.io, pick your board, and pick a role: Companion for your pocket radio, Repeater for the roof. Bluetooth companions then pair with the MeshCore phone app.

Set your region preset (this is the legal part, so don't skip it), press advert so others can find you, and send your first message. Mine was "Hi" on Public. Nobody replied to this clueless newbie. As it turned out, nobody had heard him.

This is the most common beginner problem in MeshCore. It cost me ten days.

Your radio fills up with repeaters and messages. But anything you send disappears: channel messages never come back as repeats, DMs never show as delivered, and nobody replies. It feels like a broken transmitter. It almost never is.

The upshot: being 4–6 dB short of the nearest repeater is enough to lose most of your sends. That's less than the difference between your desk and your window.

Like any engineer, I blamed my gear and spent a week swapping settings, firmware and suspicions about every component on the board. Two things finally cracked it.

First, a known-good reference. I bought a T-Deck Plus, and it failed from my house too. Then I used a cheap RTL-SDR dongle as a crude power meter, with both radios taking turns on the same marked spot. My Cardputer was never weaker than the T-Deck.

Second, a hill. From a hillside, the same radio heard about 30 repeaters instead of one. Every message got through, and it scored 84–87% on the Bay Area health check, which counts how many of the region's listening stations hear your message. The T-Deck scored 82% from the same spot.

Same radio, same firmware, same antenna. The bug was my house.

One company makes the magic chirp. Nearly every LoRa radio runs Semtech silicon or a licensed copy. That's a supply risk, not a kill switch: the firmware is open source, and nobody can switch your radio off remotely. A movement against centralized infrastructure that depends on one chip supplier. Irony noted.

License-free isn't rule-free. ISM bands have power limits, and unlicensed users get no protection from interference. Your "I'm okay" has no more right to the airwaves than your neighbor's weather station.

Encrypted doesn't mean invisible. Public and #hashtag channels are readable by anyone, and adverts can carry your location. Leave location sharing off, especially if your bug-out cabin is a secret.

Repeaters are volunteers. Coverage depends on other people's rooftops and batteries. Treat the mesh as one layer of your comms plan: two is one, and one is none.

Reticulum isn't a mesh app but a whole cryptographic network stack that runs over LoRa, Wi-Fi, packet radio, the internet, or anything else that moves bytes. It takes config files and patience, and around the Bay I could barely find anyone on it. And no, it can't bridge MeshCore and Meshtastic; today that takes a gateway, a Raspberry Pi with one radio from each tribe.

MeshCore won't replace your phone. It's slow, text-only, and it works only because a few hundred neighbors put radios on their roofs. But that's rather the point: it's a network built by the people who use it, out of cheap radios and spare rooftops, and it keeps working when the expensive one doesn't. And when you can't get through, the answer is almost never in your settings. It's in the sky: go higher, see farther, and join the people already up there.

My T-Deck now lives in my go-bag, next to the lasagna. For the price of a takeout dinner, you can put one in yours that will, on the worst day of the year, still get the message through:

"We're fine. Bring water. And the lasagna."
