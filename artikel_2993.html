Micron or Nvidia: If I Could Only Buy and Hold One of These Artificial Intelligence (AI) Chip Stocks for the Next 5 Years, It Would Be This One

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Micron and Nvidia both have compelling tailwinds as investments in artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure accelerate.

The artificial intelligence (AI) revolution has created a generational opportunity for semiconductor companies. As AI hyperscalers like Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Meta Platforms continue investing in data centers, demand for advanced processors, memory chips, and networking equipment is accelerating.

Two companies are benefiting enormously from this trend: Nvidia (NVDA -1.26%) and Micron Technology (MU -5.78%). Nvidia dominates the market for graphics processing units (GPUs), while Micron supplies the memory required to power accelerated computing systems.

While both companies are experiencing remarkable growth, their long-term investment theses are fundamentally different. Micron is capitalizing on an unprecedented shortage of high bandwidth memory (HBM), while Nvidia is quietly transforming into a diversified AI infrastructure and software company.

If I could buy only one of these stocks and hold it through 2030, my choice would be Nvidia. Here's why.

Micron's HBM products are essential components for AI accelerators, allowing GPU clusters to process enormous amounts of data quickly and efficiently. As Nvidia, Advanced Micro Devices, and other semiconductor companies design more powerful chips, demand for HBM will continue to surge. Meanwhile, Micron's DRAM and NAND memory businesses are benefiting from growing demand across data centers, smartphones, computers, and enterprise storage systems.

During its fiscal fourth quarter of 2026 (ended Sept. 3), Micron generated $54.2 billion in revenue, compared with just $11.3 billion in the same period last year. Net income reached $37.7 billion, while operating cash flow climbed to nearly $44 billion. For the full fiscal year, Micron's revenue increased to approximately $133.2 billion, up 256% year over year.

These figures underscore how dramatically AI is changing the memory market. Importantly, Micron is also expanding production of its HBM4 and HBM4E products, which are designed to support more demanding workloads across agentic AI, robotics, autonomous systems, and other next-generation applications.

Even so, there is a potential problem with Micron's investment thesis. The memory industry is historically cyclical. When demand exceeds available supply, prices rise, allowing manufacturers to generate unusually high profits. In turn, manufacturers invest aggressively in additional production capacity. Eventually, that new capacity catches up with demand, causing prices and profit margins to normalize.

By 2030, Micron could be a substantially larger company while also facing more competition, greater production capacity, and less favorable pricing. This is why I think the company's growth trajectory warrants some caution.

Nvidia's investment thesis is considerably broader than Micron's. During its fiscal second quarter (ended July 26), the company generated $96.2 billion in revenue -- representing 106% year-over-year growth. Its data center business accounted for approximately $89 billion, reflecting ongoing demand for AI compute. However, focusing solely on Nvidia's GPUs misses how the company is expanding.

Consider its partnership with Marvell Technology (MRVL -12.72%). In March, Nvidia announced a $2 billion investment in Marvell as part of a strategic collaboration involving custom AI processors, networking technology, and silicon photonics. Through its NVLink Fusion platform, AI developers can integrate specialized chips with Nvidia's broader computing architecture, including CPUs, networking equipment, and rack-scale infrastructure.

This is important because the hyperscalers are developing their own custom processors rather than relying exclusively on general-purpose GPUs. Instead of treating that trend as a competitive threat, Nvidia is positioning itself to participate in the custom-chip ecosystem.

The company is also expanding into telecommunications. Nvidia's $1 billion strategic investment in Nokia (NOK -3.04%) supports the development of AI-powered radio access networks, commonly called AI-RAN. These systems aim to transform traditional telecommunications infrastructure into distributed platforms that support AI applications on edge devices.

Nvidia's ambitions extend beyond hardware. Its CUDA software ecosystem is a major competitive advantage, while platforms such as the Nemotron open AI models are expanding its presence in areas traditionally dominated by frontier model developers like Anthropic and Google DeepMind.

All this means that Nvidia is no longer just selling processors that power AI. The company is increasingly supplying the networking, software, infrastructure, and models that make AI useful.

To me, Nvidia is a more diversified investment opportunity relative to Micron. Micron's profitability remains heavily influenced by the current supply-and-demand imbalance in the memory market.

Sure, Nvidia faces competition and cyclical risks, too. But its growing portfolio of software, networking, custom chips, telecommunications, and open-source models provides several opportunities to generate revenue outside of the core GPU franchise.

On a deeper level, these businesses reinforce one another. A customer deploying Nvidia's GPUs is likely to also purchase its networking equipment, adopt its software tools, and eventually build applications using its AI models. That creates an integrated ecosystem that competitors will struggle to replicate at scale.

While Micron can still deliver impressive upside as long as the memory shortage persists, Nvidia doesn't need a single semiconductor market to remain supply-constrained to succeed in the long run.
