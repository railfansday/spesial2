Oscar Chances for Elizabeth Holmes Documentary 'You Can See Everything’

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

The biggest star of Oscar season may be a woman who can’t even attend.

This past month, one name has been circling around Hollywood more than any other: Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes. The documentary You Can See Everything— which follows Holmes during the 34 days leading up to her 11-year prison sentence for fraud (now reduced to 10 years)—has become a must-see sensation and a box-office hit. Directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim, the three-hour film starts out as an intimate portrait of Holmes as she spends her days with her husband Billy Evans and their two children, and then transforms into a much wilder investigation as Fielder tries to make sense of Holmes and Evans’s relationship and intentions.

[Editor’s Note: Spoilers from You Can See Everything ahead.]

We’ve already covered the most shocking revelations from the A24 film, but another big twist could be coming: You Can See Everything is very likely to become the first documentary ever nominated for best picture.

For most of Oscar history, documentaries have been sequestered to the best documentary category. In 98 years, a doc has never been nominated for best picture, even after the category expanded from 5 to 10 nominees in 2010.

The film that got closest to a best-picture nomination was the 1994 documentary Hoop Dreams. Critic Roger Ebert had deemed the documentary the best movie of the year and pushed for its inclusion in the best picture lineup. But not only did Hoop Dreams miss that category, it didn’t even get nominated for best documentary. This led to an outcry, and in his 1995 essay “Anatomy of a Snub,” Ebert took stock of the five documentary nominees and decreed Hoop Dreams better than all of them. “What is obvious is that the committee that chose this year’s Oscar nominees were fond of documentaries about things that have already happened. They do not seem receptive to cinema verite,” he wrote.

The uproar over Hoop Dreams’s omission led to major changes in how the documentary nominees were voted on, and was also the starting point for the creation of a documentary branch within the Academy, which was established in 2001.

The genre has come a long way since then, with several films becoming major moments within the zeitgeist, like 2002’s Bowling for Columbine, 2006’s An Inconvenient Truth (which won for documentary feature and original song), and more recently, films like Free Solo and My Octopus Teacher.

Nathan Fielder and Elizabeth Holmes, You Can See Everything.

You Can See Everything’s toughest race may be in the documentary category itself. The documentary branch, which selects the nominees, has gained a reputation for snubbing popular documentaries, including the Mr. Rogers film Won’t You Be My Neighbor? (2018) and the Jon Batiste doc American Symphony (2023).

Celebrity-centered docs have had an especially hard time gaining recognition, including Will Ferrell’s Will & Harper, I Am: Celine Dion, Elton John: Never Too Late, Super/Man: The Christopher Reeve Story, and Mariska Hargitay’s My Mom Jayne.

Will this branch view You Can See Everything as too popular or too celebrity-centered, with Fielder, who is known for starring in his TV series The Rehearsal and Nathan for You, as not only one of the filmmakers but also a participant in the film? The shortlists, which come out on December 15, will be the film’s first test in the doc category.

You Can See Everything could also make history if it’s nominated in more than three categories. It’s still rare for a documentary to get nominated outside of the doc category, but editing, cinematography, and directing feel like possible nods for the film. Only two docs have even been nominated in three categories before: At the 1971 Oscars, Woodstock won best documentary and was also nominated for editing and sound, and 2021’s Flee was nominated for best documentary feature, animated feature, and international feature.

And what about the possibility of Amanda Seyfried, who makes a surprise appearance in the film, landing a nomination for supporting actress? You Can See Everything blurs the lines between doc and narrative storytelling when Seyfried steps back into her The Dropout role, playing Holmes in Fielder’s reenactments of scenes that he was unable to film for the documentary. A source tells Vanity Fair that A24 is still deciding if she’ll be submitted for supporting actress, but some awards prognosticator sites already have her popping up as a potential nominee.

An individual in a documentary has never been nominated in an acting category because, of course, there aren’t usually actors playing other characters in a nonfiction film. The closest comparison from Oscar history would be Maria Bakalova, who landed a supporting actress nomination in 2021 for her performance in Borat Subsequent Moviefilm, a mockumentary sequel in which she played a fictional character opposite real people like Rudy Giuliani and Mike Pence.

You Can See Everything will have challenges at other awards shows on its way to the Oscars. The Golden Globes’ rules currently state that only “feature-length, scripted motion pictures” are eligible for the drama and comedy motion picture categories and that “live-action or animated documentaries and docudramas with over 25% of documentary footage or audio are considered documentaries and, therefore, are not eligible for consideration as motion pictures.” The Actor Awards (formerly known as the SAG Awards) has also never included a documentary, and the rules explicitly state “documentaries are not eligible.”

The Directors Guild of America recently confirmed to Variety that Fielder and Oppenheim are only eligible to compete in the outstanding directorial achievement in documentary category and not in the group’s most high-profile prize, outstanding directorial achievement in theatrical feature film. Their rules state that “re-releases, animated films and documentaries are not eligible” for the theatrical feature film award, which has aligned with the winner of the best-director Oscar for the majority of its existence, including the past six years straight.

Any of these voting bodies could bend their rules for this genre-bending film, or they may not. We’re in uncharted waters here. But if a documentary finally cracks best picture after 98 years, it will be fitting that it’s one as unique as this—and that its main character won’t be walking the red carpet.
