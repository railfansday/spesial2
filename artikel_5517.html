10 marathon training mistakes that raise your injury risk

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Marathon training mistakes such as ramping up mileage too fast or skipping strength work can set up overuse injuries, and these 10 show what to watch for

For many runners, the injury that ends a marathon build does not happen on race day. It develops over weeks of training. Many of the problems that sideline marathoners are overuse injuries, such as stress reactions, shin splints and tendon pain. These injuries build when training load rises faster than bone, tendon and muscle can adapt to it.

That makes the risk less about any single run and more about how a training plan is carried out. The same schedule can strengthen one runner and break down another. The difference often comes down to pace, recovery, equipment and timing.

The body adapts to running through a cycle of stress and repair. A hard workout or a long run creates small amounts of strain in the tissues that absorb each footstrike. Those tissues rebuild stronger in the hours and days after the session, while the runner rests. When stress keeps arriving before repair is finished, the balance tips the other way. Fatigue accumulates, form starts to slip and small aches can turn into injuries that keep a runner off the road for weeks.

The marathon training mistakes below each disrupt that cycle in a different way. Some involve volume, such as adding weekly miles too fast or stretching the long run too quickly. Others involve intensity, including easy runs done at a hard effort and too many demanding workouts packed into one week. Several concern recovery, from skipped rest days to missing step-back weeks. The rest cover strength training, the condition and fit of running shoes and late changes to gear or terrain. They also cover warning signs of pain and the decision to start a marathon training plan without enough of a running base.

These 10 are a selection of common errors, not a ranked or complete list. Each one stands on its own. Readers can go straight to the habit that matches their own training. Each slide explains how its mistake upsets the balance between stress and repair. Together they show that running injury prevention depends more on planning than on any single fix.

This article offers general training information, not medical advice. Overuse injuries in runners can look alike in their early stages. Persistent or worsening pain warrants an evaluation by a sports medicine physician or physical therapist rather than more miles.

Raising weekly mileage faster than the body can adapt sets up overuse injuries. Bone, tendon and muscle all need time to strengthen in response to new training load, and a sudden jump in volume leaves them less of it.

Running is repetitive. Every stride sends force through the feet, shins, knees and hips, and a marathon training week repeats that force across many thousands of steps. The body responds by remodeling tissue to cope with the stress. Aerobic fitness tends to improve faster than bone and tendon adapt.

That mismatch can mislead runners. A runner can feel fit enough for more miles while the skeleton and connective tissue are still catching up. When weekly volume climbs faster than those tissues can rebuild, small amounts of damage pile up faster than they are repaired.

The results are familiar overuse injuries in runners. A stress reaction is an early stage of bone stress injury, in which bone breaks down faster than it rebuilds. Without rest, it can progress to a stress fracture.

Medial tibial stress syndrome, commonly called shin splints, causes pain along the inner edge of the shinbone. Tendinopathy often affects the Achilles tendon or the patellar tendon just below the kneecap. It develops when a tendon is loaded repeatedly beyond what it can tolerate.

Many marathon training plans guard against this by building mileage in small steps. They often schedule periodic lower-mileage weeks, which let the body absorb recent work before volume rises again.

A widely cited guideline is the 10 percent rule. It holds that weekly mileage should rise by no more than 10 percent from one week to the next. It is a rule of thumb, not a proven threshold. It offers a simple brake, but it does not account for a runner's history, age or the intensity of the added miles.

Terrain also changes how much stress a week places on the legs. A week that adds distance on hilly routes asks more of the body than the mileage total alone suggests.

Easy runs should feel easy. In a marathon training plan they are meant to be run at a conversational effort. Pushing them harder piles on fatigue without delivering a matching gain in fitness.

Easy running does specific work. Low-intensity mileage builds the aerobic base a marathon depends on. It encourages the body to develop more capillaries and mitochondria in the working muscles. Those changes help muscles use oxygen and fuel more efficiently over long distances. They respond to time spent running at a comfortable effort, not to how hard each run feels.

Easy runs also let a runner accumulate volume while absorbing less stress per mile. In many marathon plans, they make up much of the weekly mileage. That design only works if the easy days stay easy.

A runner who turns every easy run into a moderate or hard effort changes the balance of the whole week. Each run leaves more residual fatigue behind. That leftover fatigue carries into the next day and the day after.

Tired legs have a harder time holding good running form. As fatigue sets in, stride mechanics tend to become less efficient. More of the impact then lands on muscles, tendons and bones that are already worn down. That repeated strain on tired tissue raises the risk of overuse injuries in runners.

A common gauge is the talk test. At a true easy effort, a runner should be able to speak in full sentences without gasping. If conversation shrinks to a few words at a time, the pace has drifted out of easy territory. Perceived effort and heart rate can serve the same purpose. Both reflect conditions that pace alone ignores.

Conditions change how a given pace feels. Heat, humidity, hills and wind all raise the effort required to hold the same speed. On those days, an easy run may need to be noticeably slower than usual to stay easy.

Social pressure can push the pace up too. Group runs, a faster training partner or the urge to match a number on a GPS watch can nudge an easy day into a workout. Running by feel on easy days, rather than chasing a target pace, keeps the purpose of the run intact.

The payoff of holding back shows up elsewhere in the plan. Runners who keep easy days relaxed arrive at their harder sessions with fresher legs. That freshness lets those workouts do the job they were designed to do.

Rest days and step-back weeks are where marathon training pays off, because the body repairs and strengthens tissue during recovery rather than during the run itself. Cutting them out leaves muscles, tendons and bones absorbing new stress before they have adapted to the last round.

Each run leaves the legs with repair work to finish afterward. Muscle fibers mend, tendons strengthen and bone remodels by breaking down and rebuilding to handle the load it has been given. Those changes take time. They happen between sessions, not during them.

A rest day gives that process room to work. Many marathon training plans include at least one rest day each week. Some fill that slot with low-impact cross-training such as cycling or swimming. That keeps aerobic work going while sparing the legs from the repeated impact of running. Others call for complete rest. Either approach serves the same purpose — a break from the specific load that running places on the legs.

The step-back week works on a longer cycle. After a few weeks of building, a plan drops total mileage for a week before resuming its climb. Many plans schedule one every three or four weeks. The lighter week lets accumulated fatigue clear. It also gives slower-adapting tissues such as tendons and bone a chance to catch up with the muscles and cardiovascular system, which respond to training faster.

Runners often skip these breaks out of worry that a lighter week will cost fitness. Fitness built over weeks of consistent training does not disappear in a few days of reduced volume. The final taper before race day relies on the same principle. It trims mileage in the last weeks so the runner reaches the start line recovered rather than worn down.

Turning a rest day into an extra run, or running a step-back week at full volume, removes the window in which adaptation happens. The runner keeps adding stress to tissue that has not finished responding to the previous load. Overuse injuries can develop this way across a training block, often without a single obvious moment when something went wrong.

Recovery also depends on what happens off the road. Adequate sleep and enough food to match training demands both support the body's repair process. A scheduled rest day does its job best when those basics are also in place.

Skipping strength training leaves the calves, glutes, hips and core less prepared for the repetitive load of marathon training. Strength work for these muscles supports the load that running places on the Achilles tendon, knee and shin. It does not guarantee injury prevention.

Running is a series of single-leg landings. Each stride puts the body's weight, plus the force of impact, onto one leg at a time. Over a long run, that pattern repeats thousands of times. The muscles around the ankle, knee and hip help absorb and control that force. When those muscles tire or lack capacity, more of the work shifts to tendons, joints and bone.

The calves carry a large share of that load. The calf muscles attach to the heel through the Achilles tendon. They help control how the foot and lower leg handle each landing and each push-off. Stronger calves give the Achilles tendon and the shin more support during those phases of the stride. Weaker calves offer less of that help as weekly mileage climbs.

The hips and glutes play a different role. They help keep the pelvis level and guide the thigh as the foot strikes the ground. If the hip muscles cannot hold that alignment, the knee can drift inward on landing. That changes how force travels through the knee and lower leg. Hip strength is one factor in how much load the knee takes on during running.

The core connects those pieces. The muscles of the trunk help hold posture steady late in a long effort, when the legs are tired.

A strength routine for runners usually draws on a short list of basic movements. Calf raises, done on two legs or one, target the lower leg. Squats, lunges and step-ups work the quads, glutes and hips together. Single-leg exercises such as split squats challenge balance as well as strength. Glute bridges and side-lying leg raises isolate the hip muscles. Planks and side planks cover the core.

Strength gains build over weeks rather than days. That makes the start of a training block a practical time to add this work. Strength sessions fit best on easy days, away from the long run and the hardest workouts. That placement keeps them from leaving the legs tired for the key sessions in a marathon training plan.

Running shoes lose cushioning and structure as the miles add up, and a pair that no longer absorbs impact or fits the foot properly can pass more stress to the body. Checking wear and fit regularly keeps that variable under control.

The midsole is the foam layer between the outsole and the upper. It does most of the work of softening each footstrike. That foam compresses a little with every step. Over many runs it loses some of its ability to rebound. The shoe then becomes firmer and less stable than it was when new. The change is gradual, which makes it easy to miss from one run to the next.

No single mileage number applies to every pair. Replacement guidance varies by shoe and by manufacturer. Foam type, shoe design, body weight, stride and the surfaces a runner covers all affect how quickly a shoe wears down. A lightweight racing flat and a heavily cushioned daily trainer are built for different jobs. They do not age at the same rate.

Physical signs give a practical check in place of a fixed number. A midsole that looks creased or compressed, particularly under the heel or forefoot, suggests the foam has broken down. Tread worn smooth or worn unevenly on one side of the outsole is another signal. A heel counter that no longer holds the foot firmly in place points to lost structure. Some runners log the mileage on each pair in a training app. That record makes it easier to notice whether new aches line up with an aging shoe.

Fit is the second part of the check. A shoe that is too short can push the toes into the front of the toe box on every stride. Over a long run or a steep downhill, that repeated contact can cause blisters and bruised or blackened toenails. A shoe that is too narrow can pinch the forefoot. One that is too loose lets the foot slide around and adds friction.

Feet tend to swell over the course of a long run. A shoe that feels snug in the store can feel tight in the later miles as a result. Feet also swell gradually through the day. Trying on shoes in the afternoon or evening gives a truer sense of fit. Wearing the same socks used for training helps too. The toes should have room to spread and move without hitting the front of the shoe.

Changing to new shoes, a new running surface or unfamiliar gear in the final weeks before a marathon alters how the body absorbs each stride. Race-day kit is best tested on long runs during training, not introduced on the start line.

Every running shoe spreads load a little differently. Midsole firmness, heel-to-toe drop and the shape of the upper all affect where stress lands in the foot and lower leg. A runner who has logged months in one model has trained the calves, Achilles tendon and arches to that pattern. A switch to a lower-drop or stiffer shoe shifts some of that work to other tissues. Those tissues then have little time to adjust before the race.

Carbon-plated racing shoes are a common example. Many runners buy a pair specifically for race day. The stiff plate and thick foam stack can feel very different from a daily trainer. Wearing them for the first time over 26.2 miles means meeting a new loading pattern at the point of greatest fatigue.

Running surfaces work in a similar way. Concrete, asphalt, packed dirt, a treadmill belt and a rubberized track each respond differently underfoot. A runner who has trained mostly on soft trails may find a road marathon harder on the legs than expected. Course profile adds another variable. Long downhill stretches place heavy braking demands on the quadriceps. Flat training routes do not prepare the legs for that work.

Smaller items matter too. New socks, shorts, a sports bra or a hydration vest can rub in places that never chafed before. A blister or raw patch late in a race can lead a runner to alter their stride to protect the sore spot.

The standard fix is rehearsal. Runners commonly break in race shoes over several long runs and at least one session at goal marathon pace. Wearing the full race outfit on a long run shows whether anything rubs, slips or bounces. Training on the same kind of surface as the race lets the legs adapt to the conditions they will face. That includes hills if the course has them.

The aim is to match training to race conditions. A late change does not mean a specific injury will follow. Rehearsal removes a variable the body has not yet adapted to.

Pain that is sharp, localized or worsening from run to run is a different signal from ordinary training soreness. Persistent pain like this is a reason to pause and get a professional assessment rather than push on with the marathon training plan.

Muscle soreness is a normal part of building toward a marathon. It tends to feel diffuse and achy across a large muscle group such as the quadriceps or calves. It usually shows up a day or two after a hard or unfamiliar session. It then fades as the muscles recover. It often eases once a runner warms up.

Warning pain behaves differently. It is often concentrated in one spot that a runner can point to with a single finger. It may feel sharp rather than dull. It can appear earlier in each run, linger after the run ends or hurt during everyday walking. Pain that changes how a person runs — a limp, a shortened stride or a shift in foot strike — is another sign that something beyond normal fatigue is involved.

Bone pain needs particular attention. Localized pain over a bone that persists, especially in the shin, foot or hip, can be a sign of a stress reaction or a stress fracture. Both are overuse injuries of bone. Continuing to run on them can allow the damage to progress.

Tendons send their own signals. Pain around the Achilles tendon or just below the kneecap that keeps coming back is worth having examined rather than managed by guesswork.

A sports medicine physician can examine the area and order imaging if it is needed. Early stress fractures often do not show up on a standard X $TWTR-ray. An MRI can detect bone stress that an X-ray misses. A physical therapist can assess the movement, strength and loading patterns that may be contributing to the problem. Either clinician can advise on what training, if any, is safe to continue. Runners should not try to diagnose themselves from symptoms alone.

Keeping a simple log of where the pain sits, when it starts during a run and what makes it worse gives a clinician useful information. A break taken early in a training block also leaves more weeks to rebuild than one forced in the final weeks before race day.

Packing intervals, tempo runs and a long run into back-to-back days leaves too little recovery between demanding sessions. Marathon $MPC training plans typically limit the number of hard workouts in a week and separate them with easy days to give the body time to absorb each one.

Each type of key session stresses the body in its own way. Intervals are repeated bouts of fast running with jogs or rests in between. Tempo runs call for a sustained, comfortably hard effort held over an extended stretch. The long run builds endurance through sheer time on your feet. All three place more load on muscles, tendons and bones than an easy run of a similar length.

A typical week in a marathon training plan arranges these workouts so hard days rarely sit side by side. One common pattern puts a speed or tempo session midweek and the long run on the weekend. Easy runs or rest days fill the space around them. The exact layout differs from plan to plan. The principle of alternating hard and easy days shows up in many widely used programs.

Trouble starts when extra quality work creeps into the week. A missed interval session gets made up the next day, just before a scheduled tempo run. A casual group run turns into a race. A hill session gets added on top of the workouts already on the schedule. Each choice can look minor on its own. Together they shrink the gap between the efforts that matter most.

That shrinking gap means the next key session begins on legs that have not recovered from the last one. Target $TGT paces become harder to reach. That lowers the value of the workout itself. Overuse injuries in runners become more likely when heavy sessions pile up with little room between them.

Hard effort also counts outside of running. A tune-up race is a hard session, even if it is labeled as practice. An intense spin class or a heavy cross-training day adds to overall fatigue for the week. Counting these alongside running workouts gives a clearer picture of how demanding a week actually is.

A missed workout can simply be dropped rather than squeezed into the following days. Moving it makes sense only when it can still be surrounded by easy running. Keeping the plan's spacing intact protects the recovery that each key session depends on.

The long run is meant to build endurance at a controlled, comfortable effort, and its distance should grow in modest steps. Running it too fast or stretching it too far too soon turns the week's key aerobic session into a source of excess strain.

The purpose of the long run is time on your feet. Hours of steady running train the heart, lungs and muscles to sustain effort across the full 26.2-mile marathon distance. They also condition bones, tendons and connective tissue to handle repeated impact for longer stretches. That work happens at an easy to moderate effort. It does not require pushing the pace.

Problems start when every long run becomes a race. A long run at or faster than goal marathon pace leaves the body far more depleted than the same distance run comfortably. Recovery takes longer. The days that follow carry leftover fatigue into other sessions. Late in a fast long run, tired muscles absorb less of each footstrike. More of the load then shifts onto joints and tendons.

Some marathon training plans do include goal-pace segments inside a long run. Those are planned, limited portions of the run. They are not the whole distance at speed.

Distance is the second risk. Jumping from a moderate long run to a much longer one in a single week asks tissues to handle a sharp rise in sustained loading. Many plans add distance to the long run gradually. Some alternate a longer outing with a shorter one the following week. That pattern gives the body time to absorb each step up.

There is no single correct peak. Many marathon plans top out at around 20 miles for the longest run. Others use shorter peaks and rely on the rest of the training week to prepare runners for race day. The right ceiling depends on the plan, the runner's experience and how the other sessions are built.

A long run that leaves a runner worn out for several days is a sign the pace or distance may be too ambitious. Slowing down and following the plan's progression keeps the long run doing the job it was designed for.

Beginning a marathon training plan with little running history raises injury risk because the body has less time to adapt to the workload ahead. A runner without an established base, or without enough weeks before race day, has to compress the build into a steeper climb.

A running base is the consistent, comfortable running a person has already done before formal marathon training starts. It is the starting point a plan assumes. Marathon $MPC plans are generally written with the expectation that the runner can already handle regular running. When that assumption does not hold, the opening weeks can represent a much bigger jump than the plan's authors intended.

Bone, tendon and connective tissue adapt more slowly than aerobic fitness. A new runner's heart and lungs may respond within weeks, which can make the training feel manageable. The structures that absorb the impact of each stride need longer to catch up. That gap between how a runner feels and what their tissues can tolerate is where overuse injuries can develop.

Lead time is the second part of readiness. Marathon plans commonly run 16 to 20 weeks, though the length varies from plan to plan. Signing up for a race with fewer weeks than a plan calls for leaves two options. One is starting partway into the schedule. The other is squeezing the full progression into less time. Both mean bigger increases from week to week than the plan was designed around.

No single base mileage is required of every runner. Plans differ in what they expect, and so do runners' backgrounds. Someone returning after a break from years of regular running is in a different position from someone who has never run consistently. A useful check is to read a plan's stated starting point and compare it with recent, steady running rather than with past fitness.

Runners who fall short of that starting point have several options. They can spend several weeks building regular easy running before the marathon plan begins. They can pick a beginner-oriented plan with a gentler opening. They can also choose a race later on the calendar, which gives the build more room. A shorter race such as a half marathon can serve as an intermediate goal before the full 26.2 miles.
