AEREDIUM bets parallel execution can raise blockchain speed limits

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Most blockchains process transactions one at a time. AEREDIUM is testing whether running engines in parallel across regions can lift that limit.

This article has been reviewed by the Quartz editorial team. The content may be licensed for reuse.

Blockchain developers have long struggled to process high transaction volumes without slowing down network agreement. As digital payment systems expand, developers are exploring parallel processing to increase capacity across separate locations. According to researchers at the Bank for International Settlements, the trade-off among a blockchain's transaction capacity, the concentration of its stakeholders, and its security is known as Buterin's scalability trilemma. Because traditional blockchains process transactions sequentially, high demand often triggers severe congestion and rising user fees during peak market activity.

Increasing speed on a traditional blockchain is far harder than upgrading standard corporate servers. In conventional computing, engineers simply add faster processors inside a single data center to handle heavier workloads. Yet distributed ledgers require independent computers across distant regions to verify every transfer and agree on a single shared history.

To address these constraints, Layer 1 blockchain AEREDIUM is testing a horizontal scaling model that runs multiple execution engines simultaneously. Rather than relying on a single processing environment, the architecture distributes transaction execution across separate geographic regions. During a Nov. 22 demonstration on its public test network, AEREDIUM reported deploying two execution engines roughly 4,000 kilometers apart. According to the company, each engine handled approximately 200,000 transactions per second during the trial.

Over a 10-second window, AEREDIUM reported processing 3 million individually signed transfers from 1,200 funded accounts, averaging roughly 300,000 transactions per second. The company said each transaction entered through the network's standard interface, completed execution and finalization, and incurred the applicable network fee. At its peak single second, the network finalized 41 full blocks of 10,000 transactions each, reaching 410,000 transactions per second. “We have now demonstrated 410,000 transactions in a single second across 41 full blocks, with every block recorded and available for public inspection,” said Albert Dadon, founder and CEO of AEREDIUM.

Instead of traditional energy-intensive mining or token-staking consensus, AEREDIUM uses an approach it calls Proof of Attestation. Under this design, attested software validates transactions inside isolated hardware enclaves to achieve single-block finality without waiting for multiple confirmation blocks. According to the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a trusted execution environment is “an area or enclave protected by a system processor.” AEREDIUM states that combining hardware-backed verification with parallel engines allows capacity to grow as new engines are added.

Still, controlled benchmarks do not automatically guarantee identical performance under continuous commercial traffic. Peak bursts of 410,000 transactions per second demonstrate short-term capacity rather than sustained output over months of complex smart-contract activity. Relying on hardware-enforced enclaves also introduces specific dependencies on processor integrity, firmware security, and reliable attestation mechanisms. Independent evaluations of larger, multi-region deployments will be necessary to confirm how well coordination holds up under heavy congestion or unexpected network outages.

Looking ahead, high-throughput parallel architectures could eventually support institutional settlement, cross-border payments, and automated trading platforms. In a June 2026 economic report, the Bank for International Settlements warned that as blockchain networks proliferate, “the benefits of scalability and efficiency are often undermined by the absence of cohesion among these frameworks.” For AEREDIUM, the long-term objective is expanding network capacity incrementally by adding regional execution engines rather than capping throughput at a fixed limit.

Ultimately, the broader significance of horizontal blockchain execution lies in shifting how developers think about network limits. Rather than treating transaction speed as a rigid ceiling, distributed execution explores whether capacity can expand alongside infrastructure demand. “Our architecture is designed to increase capacity as more execution engines are added, rather than operate toward a fixed transaction ceiling,” Dadon said. Whether parallel execution can preserve security and decentralization at global scale remains the defining test for next-generation financial networks.
