15 popular energy drinks ranked by caffeine, from 140 to 300 milligrams

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

From GFuel's 140 milligrams to Bang's 300, see how 15 popular energy drinks stack up on caffeine per serving.

A long day, a late shift, or an all-nighter can leave anyone wondering how much caffeine it will take to stay sharp. Coffee carries most mornings, yet some workdays outlast the pot. Plenty of people then head for the cooler at a gas station, where rows of cans compete for attention. The choice has consequences for the body. Too much caffeine can set off heart palpitations, raise blood pressure, disrupt sleep, stir up anxiety, and bring on nausea, along with other unwelcome effects. Anyone reaching for a can to finish a shift deserves to know the dose inside before cracking it open.

Energy drinks can pack three to five times the caffeine found in the beverages people sip every day. An average cup of coffee contains 96 milligrams. Black tea delivers half that, with 48 milligrams in an 8-ounce serving, and a typical cola supplies 33 milligrams in the same amount. Coffee, tea, and cola give a handy yardstick for judging any can on a store shelf. The U.S. government suggests adults cap their intake at 400 milligrams a day. A morning cup of coffee and a strong afternoon can may eat through most of that allowance before dinner, and a second can could push past it.

The Takeout ordered 15 popular energy drinks by the caffeine in one serving, starting with the mildest and climbing to the most potent. Each entry lists its caffeine in milligrams, though serving sizes vary across the lineup from 12 to 16 ounces, and not every entry states a size. The publication offers no testing method or data sources for its figures, and it gives no explanation for how it sequences drinks that hold an identical amount. Discontinued high-caffeine products fall outside the list. The result covers what a shopper can buy today. The author urges readers to check the caffeine on any can bought at a gas station and to stop after one.

GFuel carries 140 milligrams of caffeine per serving, the smallest dose among the 15 drinks here and 44 milligrams more than an average cup of coffee. A second mug erases that edge. Two cups of coffee deliver 192 milligrams, beating GFuel by 52. The author comes away unimpressed by the brand's caffeine count, and the arithmetic backs that verdict. Someone who already drains two mugs before noon gains no extra lift from switching to this drink.

GFuel aims its marketing at athletes and at gamers who play through the night. Its serving falls 160 milligrams short of the 300-milligram drinks at the opposite end of this list. A gamer hunting for the strongest can would need more than twice GFuel's caffeine to match them. Athletes and gamers both want to stay sharp over long stretches, and GFuel courts them with a dose closer to coffee than to the list's heaviest hitters. GFuel's appeal to late-night players has to rest on something besides sheer kick.

GFuel's mild formula leaves the most room in a daily budget of any drink here. One serving claims 35% of the federal daily ceiling for adults. Two servings would total 280 milligrams and still fit beneath that limit with a margin left over. A third would cross it. Even the gentlest drink on this list can push an all-night player past the advised intake once servings stack up.

GFuel's catalog reaches past canned drinks into energy powders, hydration packets, and other products. The brand announced a "2.0" formula in 2025 with new ingredients and more antioxidants. GFuel teamed with PewDiePie that year on lingonberry-flavored beverages. Each of those moves widens the menu. Powders and packets give buyers fresh formats, and the lingonberry line hands them a new flavor to sample. The brand's growth story rests on variety more than on stimulant strength.

Red Bull puts 151 milligrams of caffeine into a 16-ounce serving, a modest dose for a brand with an outsized place in American life. The list's author argues that no other energy drink label, Monster and its peers included, can match Red Bull's cultural relevance. Many people try Red Bull before any other energy drink, and the brand serves as their introduction to the whole category.

Red Bull's caffeine count trails a 16-ounce cup of coffee, the author notes. A shopper who orders coffee in that size walks away with a bigger jolt than one who grabs the can. The author warns that Red Bull won't come near the effect of Bang, the drink that closes the list. The slogan promises wings, yet the figures put this beverage at the gentle end of the lineup.

Sugar helps the original formula feel stronger than its label implies. Red Bull's classic version contains some sugar, and the author says the sweetness can amplify the "high" drinkers expect from a caffeinated drink. Part of the lift people credit to caffeine may come from the sugar riding along with it.

Speed gives Red Bull another edge over coffee. The author points out that a person can gulp a Red Bull in less time than it takes to finish a cup of coffee. Quicker drinking shortens the path from first sip to caffeine in the bloodstream. A drinker can feel the can's smaller amount sooner than the larger one waiting in a 16-ounce coffee.

Monster puts 160 milligrams of caffeine into each serving, a moderate dose attached to one of the longest corporate histories in this lineup. Some argue Monster stands as the most popular or most recognizable name among these 15, or at least rivals one other brand on the list for that distinction.

Monster's lineage traces to Los Angeles in 1935, where a family business sold fresh juice. Some records list the company, Hansen Beverages, as Hansen's Fruit and Vegetable Juices. Its products spread across the region, and the company branched into shelf-stable juices and sodas as the operation grew.

Stiff competition drove the original company into bankruptcy in 1988. A buyer acquired the Hansen's Soda brand, and its new owners steered the business toward energy drinks. Their first entry, Hansen's Energy, reached store shelves in 1997. The formula needed refinement, and Monster followed in 2002, five years after its predecessor's debut.

Nine years elapsed between the bankruptcy and the first energy can. A full 67 years separate the family's juice venture from Monster's arrival. The list's author points to this winding path in calling Monster one of the oldest brands on the list, even though the drink itself dates to 2002. Monster's age, measured by ancestry, comes from decades of juice and soda sales that preceded any caffeine. The company needed a bankruptcy, a sale, and two energy launches before it arrived at the 160-milligram formula sold today. Monster's staying power owes more to a company willing to reinvent itself than to the potency of its caffeine.
