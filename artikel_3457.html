‘Songs in the Key of Life’ at 50: Stevie Wonder Brings a Miracle to Madison Square Garden

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

“The universe is in alignment,” Stevie Wonder said, sitting at his keyboard at Madison Square Garden Thursday night, a beret emblazoned with a Yankees logo on his head. “But on this planet, we are not.”

For the length of his show, however, that lament wasn’t quite true, at least inside the arena. On the first U.S. stop of a brief 50th-anniversary tour, Wonder and a vast, multi-generational, virtuosic crew of musicians — including many of his children — recreated the entirety of 1976’s Songs in the Key of Life, an album that was always a realm unto itself, and pulled the audience as one into it. As Wonder dug into the songs, his voice uncannily strong at age 76, it was impossible not to feel renewed awe in the face of the album’s emotional and musical scope, its density of ideas, its unrelenting brilliance. Wonder’s sunglasses in the first set had a bejeweled starfield in the lenses, as if, in place of conventional vision, he’d opened a portal into the cosmic beyond. That felt about right.

Wonder’s stated aim, from the title on down, was to create a work that encompassed life, the universe, and everything. There are songs both bleak and uplifting about being Black in America, being human on Earth, being a parent and a child, and, of course, being a 205-year-old guy on Saturn chilling among “rainbow moonbeams and orange snow.” Then there’s the proggier-than-prog jazz-fusion instrumental, “Contusion,” which he set his musicians loose upon at MSG, and another vocal-free track, the pastoral, transcendent, jazz-chord-laden “Easy Goin’ Evening (My Mama’s Call),” performed onstage with Wonder’s own chromatic harmonica joined by a second harmonica player, Frederic Yonet, and melodica played by musical director Greg Phillinganes.

Wonder snuck more chords into individual songs than some rock bands managed in their entire careers, and the performances were a reminder that even the album’s most famous songs have musical surprises embedded in them — the melody and sentiment of “Isn’t She Lovely” can obscure its complexity, with its funked-out synth-bass, aching harmonica, and those airtight, breakneck signature drum fills. “Sir Duke” was impossibly buoyant, propelled by some of the greatest horn riffs ever written and the strutting lines of Wonder’s longtime bass player Nathan Watts, who also played on the original recordings. Watts somehow dug even deeper on the next song, “I Wish,” as did drummer Stanley Randolph, achieving oneness with two percussionists, Senfaub Stoney and Aaron Draper. The album’s more distant corners shone as well: the acid-funk of “All Day Sucker,” the rhythmic explosions of “Black Man,” or “Ebony Eyes,” which featured a battle between talkbox guitar and saxophone. Even after five decades, the album’s resurrection had little to do with nostalgia; Wonder was so present in the music that he wiped away tears during the opener, “Love’s in Need of Love Today.” After that song, he shouted out the power of democracy, mentioning New York City’s “new mayor.” And on the always wrenching “Village Ghetto Land,” with its brutal juxtaposition of “Eleanor Rigby”-like strings (played on synth by Wonder on the album, and by an actual string section in concert) against visceral descriptions of the horrors of ghetto poverty, he added an interjection: “still in 2026!”

Phillinganes, who went on to work with Michael Jackson and many others, played on the original album as an 18-year-old, and has been helping Wonder remake Songs in the Key of Life onstage since 2014. He brings the same scholarly intensity to the recreation that the late Brian Wilson’s band gave to Pet Sounds, and these performances deserve a live album and, in an ideal world, the Sphere’s more precise sound system — some of the lovingly crafted details are inevitably obscured in echoey arenas. In his latter-day live shows, Wonder has a fondness for covers, medleys, call-and-response bits, and loose jams, all of which are mostly held at bay by the album-recreation format. Early in the show, he did depart from the track listing long enough to run through welcome segments of “Overjoyed” and “Uptight (Everything Is Alright),” combined with quirkier bits: the chorus of “Born in the U.S.A.” (by one of his fellow “We Are the World” vocalists), and a fairly lengthy take on “Love Me Do.” (It later emerged that both Paul McCartney and Bruce Springsteen were in the house, in a luxury box alongside Spike Lee and Dave Chappelle.)

In the post-album encore, with a curfew looming, Wonder managed to deliver segments of “My Cherie Amour,” “Do I Do,” and “Living for the City” before unleashing a more full-fledged version of “Superstition” that broke past the venue’s strict 11 p.m. stop time. “I love you so much,” Wonder told the crowd, multiple times, and it felt like more than stage banter. “Take care of each other.”
