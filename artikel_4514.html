The Tyranny of Romantic Possibilities

James Gibson's influential notion of perceptual affordances refers to the meaningful possibilities for action that the environment offers us. A chair affords sitting, a path affords walking, and a door affords entry. I suggest that this concept can also illuminate the romantic realm. Just as the physical environment presents opportunities for action, the romantic environment presents opportunities for attachment, intimacy, desire, and betrayal. Understanding romantic affordances can help us better understand modern love.

At the heart of Gibson's ecological approach (1979) is the claim that we directly perceive possibilities for action rather than constructing them through complex inferences. Affordances are neither purely objective nor purely subjective; they emerge from the relationship between a person and the environment. Importantly, they do not disappear simply because we fail to notice them. A chair still affords sitting even when no one is sitting on it. Affordances are enduring possibilities waiting to be realized.

This perspective also highlights human freedom. Affordances offer possibilities, not necessities. They present opportunities, while leaving us free to decide which ones to pursue (Ben-Ze'ev, 1981).

"When your heart is on fire, you must realize, smoke gets in your eyes." —The Platters

The romantic environment is rich in affordances. A smile may afford intimacy. A glance may afford hope. An attractive stranger may afford the possibility of a new relationship. While Gibson's affordances involve possibilities for action, romantic affordances also involve possibilities for emotional and relational engagement.

A possibility is not the same as a reality. We are often attracted not to who a person is, but to what they might become in our lives. Sometimes we fall in love with a future rather than with a person. Unlike physical affordances, romantic affordances are notoriously ambiguous. We usually know whether a chair can support our weight. We rarely know whether another person's affection is genuine, fleeting, or merely imagined. Yet this uncertainty is often precisely what makes romantic possibilities so seductive.

Consider the familiar strategy of playing hard to get. By appearing simultaneously available and unavailable, interested and uninterested, people create competing affordances. A related assumption is that "whoever makes the first move loses." In both cases, people hesitate to appear overly eager. Ambiguity heightens desire because the outcome remains uncertain.

People are naturally drawn to what remains unresolved. The incomplete, uncertain, and partly unattainable often generates more emotional intensity than what is secure and stable. Romantic ambivalence thrives on such uncertainty. Robert Greene's (2001) portrait of the master seducer rests on this principle. Mixed signals, delayed gratification, and partial fulfillment keep multiple romantic possibilities alive at once. The attraction often lies less in what has been achieved than in what still seems possible (Ben-Ze'ev, 2024; and here).

"You can't hurry love, oh, you just have to wait. Love don't come easy, it's a game of give and take." —The Supremes

"My lover brings me tranquillity. Not during our lovemaking, which is so exciting, but as an enduring sense of inner peace." —A married woman

Modern life has dramatically expanded the number of romantic affordances surrounding us. Dating apps, social media, and increasing social mobility constantly expose us to potential alternatives. At first glance, this freedom seems desirable. Yet, as Barry Schwartz (2009) argued, an abundance of choice can become a burden. When attractive alternatives are always visible, satisfaction becomes harder to achieve. Instead of appreciating what is good enough, we keep searching for something better.

This abundance also fuels romantic ambivalence. The awareness of alternatives can undermine commitment to existing partners. Research suggests that attraction to attractive alternatives increases uncertainty about one's current relationship and diminishes both personal and relational well-being. The irony is striking: More romantic opportunities may generate greater excitement, but not greater happiness (Zoppolat et al., 2024).

Paradoxically, in a world overflowing with romantic possibilities, calmness and patience become increasingly valuable qualities of profound love (Ben-Ze'ev, 2019). A survey conducted across 116 countries and territories found that 72% of adults preferred a calm life to an exciting one, whereas only 16% preferred the opposite. The appeal of calmness extends far beyond romance.

When possibilities are abundant, the ability to devote oneself to a single relationship becomes especially valuable. Calmness and patience are therefore more than pleasant emotional states. They are ways of resisting the tyranny of endless alternatives. Such calmness is not passive. As Shunryu Suzuki (1970) observed, "Calmness of mind does not mean you should stop your activity. Real calmness should be found in the activity itself."

"True love is not a strong, fiery, impetuous passion. It is, on the contrary, an element calm and deep." —Ellen G. White

Modern romance confronts us with a paradox: freedom can become a burden. The more possibilities we perceive, the harder it may be to appreciate and cultivate the relationship we actually have. Romantic affordances create possibilities. Ambiguity makes them more alluring and their abundance makes commitment more difficult. Mature love requires choosing among them.

Indeed, one of the greatest challenges of contemporary love may not be finding romantic opportunities but resisting them. Never before have so many alternatives been so visible and so accessible. Yet no life, and no love, can flourish by pursuing every possibility. The deepest relationships are built not by maximizing options but by limiting them. A meaningful life and a profound love depend less on keeping doors open than on deciding which door to walk through. In modern romance, the greatest threat may not be the absence of opportunities, but the inability to stop pursuing them.
