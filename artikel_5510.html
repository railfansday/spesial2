Why yellow pad math still beats the spreadsheet in builder deals

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Scott Cox explains how yellow pad math tests the relationships behind pro forma lines to surface inconsistencies, risk, and upside. The approach favors directional accuracy over spreadsheet precision to identify what a deal truly depends on.  AI Summary

[In the third of seven excerpts from ‘Less Wrong, More Right,’ Scott Cox argues that models can hide what matters by weighting every input equally. Simple back-of-the-envelope comparisons can identify what must be true for a homebuilder deal to work and where opportunity may be underpriced.]

One of the most impressive people I got to observe early in my career was a vice chairman of the bank, widely considered the smartest person in the place. What made him remarkable had nothing to do with depth in any particular market. He would be handed a credit write-up or a new project package – often a deal in a market he did not know, on a product type he had not financed, for a borrower he had never met — and he would flip pages for two or three minutes. Then he would tell you what the deal’s success or failure was actually depending on.

I never saw him build anything. He did a few simple calculations on the relationships between the numbers, and the relationships told him where to look. He was not recognizing the borrower and he was not recognizing the market. He was recognizing a pattern that lacked internal consistency – or one that could only hold if something were true that nobody in the package had said.

What he was doing is more nuanced than it sounds. Every line on a financial statement or a proforma is an abstract representation of a thing, and usually a physical thing. Revenue is not a number; it is a specific count of houses closing in specific months. Work in process is not a number; it is houses standing in the ground at various stages of completion. Every category on the balance sheet, the income statement, and the proforma is a stand-in for something you could walk up to and touch.

The people who are good at this convert the line back into the thing before they reason about it. And once you are looking at things rather than numbers, you start to notice combinations that cannot coexist. You should not see this number and that number together. That is where the work happens — not in any single figure, but in the pairs.

Notice what that produces. When you check a model cell by cell, what you find is an error: a broken formula, a bad input, something with an answer at the end of it. When the relationship between two numbers is off, it could be an error, but it could also be indicative of something unusual, good or bad. An anomaly. Not necessarily wrong. Curious. Something that has to be explained – and often reveals either truly unique aspects of the deal, or assumptions that lean toward the heroic.

Which is the argument for doing simple calculations before anyone shows you the model. A large cash flow invites you to read all of it, and reading all of it is how you study the trees and miss the forest. Look at the major relationships first. Find the anomalies. Then go looking for the answers. A massive spreadsheet is close to the worst instrument for finding what a deal depends on – not because it is wrong, but because it presents everything at the same weight. Complex models obscure as much as they illuminate. Your simple calculations, done on a pad, cannot have a broken cell.

I call it yellow pad math. The cash flow is precise to the dollar, but it rests on the same handful of projections the pad does – it has simply dressed them up. That is precision without accuracy: every figure carried to the dollar, resting on estimates the complex model obscures. The pad is imprecise and makes no attempt to hide it, which is why it can be more accurate about what you actually know. Most of the important decisions we make are imprecise anyway. What matters is being generally accurate. Would you rather be directionally correct, or precisely wrong?

I want to be clear about what this method is for, because it’s easy to read it as purely defensive — a way to find problems and avoid bad deals. That’s half of it. The other half is finding opportunities that the same logic says are larger than anyone in the room is treating them as.

I was once in a meeting where a builder I knew was presenting to a potential capital partner. They were doing infill deals in rough but improving areas – they believed in the concept but were being somewhat tentative about it. I started asking about nearby apartments. Were there any? How were they performing? What were the rents? They gave me the numbers. I asked how that compared to their projected house payments. Close, they said – payments just a bit more.

I stopped and said to the capital partner: we should buy every piece of ground that fits that criteria. If people are willing to rent a Class A apartment in a sketchy area, they’re seeing something there — because they have options. They’re not there because it’s cheap. Which means they’re built-in buyers for townhouses if the payment is competitive with the rent. And that relationship – apartment rent roughly equal to an ownership payment in an improving area – is almost certainly temporary. Apartment developers have an inherent land cost advantage because of density. As the area improves, land prices will reflect that. The window where you can buy ground at a price that makes the payment/rent comparison work will close. The math said move now and move aggressively.

That conclusion came from two numbers and one inference. Not a model or market study. A question, a number, an inference about what the number implied, and the willingness to follow it further than the people in the room were comfortable going.
