Lakers' biggest win of the year? The risky offseason move to get Walker Kessler

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

When the Lakers sent two unprotected firsts and two additional swaps to the Jazz for Walker Kessler this offseason, the overwhelming consensus was that Los Angeles had hemorrhaged a significant chunk of its future flexibility for a questionable fit at best.

But when these two teams tip off against each other on Wednesday night — at least one of those worries will have already been assuaged. Kessler is an outstanding fit for the Lakers, who have won nine straight and own the best record in the NBA following a thrilling overtime win against the Hornets on Tuesday. (The Jazz, meanwhile, have the league's third-worst record.)

"Unbelievable, honestly," Luka Dončić said last month of his new teammate Kessler, who is averaging 14.5 points, 10.9 rebounds and 2.2 blocks per game. "We know what we're getting; I'm not surprised by how he's playing. He knows how to play this game, he played for a long time. I'm not surprised, but the fact that he's doing it, just proving a lot of people wrong, is just unbelievable."

(Mallory Bielecki/Yahoo Sports Illustration)

After the departure of LeBron James and the re-emergence of Dončić as a centripetal force, the Lakers needed a stabilizer. Not necessarily a running mate — Austin Reaves had already proven to be a high-octane complementary piece — but a dependable back-line enforcer. The Deandre Ayton/Jaxson Hayes tandem flamed out as quickly as it started in L.A., and the days of Anthony Davis were long gone. Dončić reportedly pushed hard for Kessler, who played only five games last season in Utah after shoulder surgery.

The results speak for themselves. With Kessler on the floor, the Lakers are allowing just 111.0 points per 100 possessions, which would rank just outside the top-five in defensive efficiency. With Kessler manning the middle, the Lakers have very loudly reshaped their defensive approach. They're fifth in "compact" shell possessions, per Synergy tracking data, which refers to the surface area connecting all five defenders on the floor. Last season, the Lakers ranked 22nd in that category.

Constricting the restricted area certainly comes with trade-offs, too. Contenders like Oklahoma City and San Antonio were notorious for similar styles in recent years, being comfortable with giving up 3s in the corners and essentially anywhere else on the perimeter. And to that point, the Lakers are dead last in opponent corner attempts and 28th in overall opponent perimeter attempts. (The non-Kesslers also do a decent job of contesting despite the amount of ground they have to cover at times, with Kessler's lateral movement helping a ton in this regard.)

What the Lakers have done is dare teams to shoot and laugh at the ones that even think about attacking the rim. Only four players have fared better than Kessler with shots within six feet: Donovan Clingan, Jalen Smith, Chet Holmgren and Victor Wembanyama. Opponents shoot nearly 13% worse when Kessler is on the floor and spend far less time venturing into his restricted area office. (He's in the 99th percentile in rim points saved.)

And don't make the mistake of thinking Kessler's presence is limited to the paint alone. He is once again posting ridiculous steal and block rates at his position, is capable of stepping outside to contest and bother shots and continues to be active in passing lanes. Pairing a talent like that with Matisse Thybulle, who's basically been the best defensive playmaker in basketball for a few seasons now, gives the Lakers the necessary juice to be the league's best in opponent turnover rate.

"You're talking about one of the best rim protectors in the NBA," JJ Redick said last week. "And that doesn't always show up in blocked shots or steals. He's doing a fantastic job for us."

At the other end, Kessler's value comes with the understanding that the Lakers aren't asking him to step outside of his comfort zone. Sure, Dončić as arguably the best player in the world helps a ton, using his gravity and elite on-ball talent to draw defenders out and create space. But Kessler knows where to find impact. He's quick enough to zoom around the perimeter, screen for his guards and duck under slower bigs to find pockets of space around the rim. And the Lakers know how to use his mobility (although it comes at the expense of their defensive rebounding at times). Kessler has accumulated nearly triple the number of possessions as a cutter or in transition (48) as he has as a roll man (17). He understands the assignment; he's in the 92nd percentile in points per shot attempt, albeit at a low usage rate.

"He's just healthy," one scout told Yahoo Sports. "He's a high-IQ big, active and young. Youth and health are the biggest things working well for Walker and that's helping their defense as a whole. [On offense] he's intelligent. He knows how to play his role; screen, roll, catch lobs and putbacks, and finish. He's not doing anything outside of his skill set."

Two areas you'd like to see improvement in are his touch/strength and finishing rate. Kessler is in the 98th percentile in rim attempts, but just 75th in conversion, as well as 34th percentile in ball security. Dončić's gravity dictates a plethora of paint touches, which Kessler leads the league in, but more aggression should help in that department.

Detractors will point to the Lakers' strength of schedule (five of their wins in the current nine-game run have been against teams with losing records), but bah humbug. They are taking care of business, and have helped Kessler settle in quickly. And, from Utah's vantage point, the Lakers' big acquisition this summer is enjoying the much greener SoCal grass.
