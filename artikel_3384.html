10 Saddest Deaths in 'Sunrise on the Reaping,' Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Spoiler Alert: This list contains spoilers for The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping.The Hunger Games has never been shy about putting its characters through absolute misery, and Sunrise on the Reaping is no exception. Set during the 50th year of the Games—its Second Quarter Quell—the latest chapter in Suzanne Collins' dystopian saga follows a young Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada) as he's thrown into an arena designed to be twice as deadly as the usual Games, with 48 tributes fighting for survival.

But as brutal as the competition itself is, it's the people Haymitch loses along the way that make this story truly one of the most devastating additions to the franchise. From friendships forged in the arena to the loved ones left behind in District 12, Sunrise on the Reaping is filled with so many deaths that have left a mark on the young tribute as well as audiences alike. So, here are some of the most poignant ones.

Panache Barker (Jhaleil Swaby) is one of the District 1 Career tributes who enters the Quarter Quell with a clear advantage over many of the other participants. Having already trained since young, he quickly established himself as a dangerous threat, with his arrogance, brute strength, and aggression already creating conflict before the games even began. But Panache's confidence ultimately proves to be his downfall in the arena, as Maysilee Donner (Mckenna Grace) uses her poisoned darts to stealthily kill him, right as he's about to deliver a fatal attack on Haymitch.

Having built himself up as the ruthless antagonist of the games, Panache's sudden takedown isn't particularly heartbreaking on its own. In fact, it initially feels like a moment of relief and dark irony, since he was, after all, constantly brutalizing the other tributes—and seemingly enjoying it. However, despite his actions, there remains an underlying tragedy as Panache is simply just another teenager who was manipulated by the Capitol into becoming a cruel killer. A mere cog in the endless machine.

As another District 1 Career, Silka Sharp (Laura Marcus) was undoubtedly ruthless and skilled—so much so that she became one of the last tributes standing in the arena alongside Haymitch. Their final confrontation is a tense battle that leaves Haymitch badly wounded, and Silka seemingly having the upper hand. But when she corners him towards the area's edge, Haymitch uses the hidden force field to his advantage, taunting her to throw her axe and have the weapon rebound to fatally strike her.

Similar to Panache, Silka's death isn't necessarily upsetting, as it's staged as a pure, triumphant "outsmarting the villain" climax. That's what happens when she's consistently positioned as a lethal powerhouse who's actively gunning to spill blood and win. But she, too, is a tragic product of the Capitol's cruel system. Unfortunately, the movie omits an important book moment of vulnerability that showcased this (yes, we're talking about the chocolate scene).

Wellie (Rada Rae) is one of the Newcomers from District 6, and as one of the youngest competitors in the arena, it's understandable why Haymitch was quick to care for her before the games began. We see this as he offers Wellie his lunch during one of the tribute's meal sessions. Unfortunately, we don't get much from her after that, as her death occurs off-screen with Haymitch finding her body after Silka rings Wellie's bicycle bell as a means to lure him into an ambush.

While the discovery is obviously grim and heartbreaking, audiences who haven't read the book have very little time to get attached to Wellie before she dies. In fact, the film effectively glosses over the important bond forged between Haymitch and Wellie in the game. They even soften her actual death—which was arguably the most gruesome and brutal one of all. Nevertheless, her death still remains an emotional gut punch as she symbolizes the tragic loss of innocents.

Entering the Second Quarter Quell alongside his other District 12 tributes, Wyatt Callow (Ben Wang) quickly becomes a sympathetic figure. Despite the initial hostility surrounding his family's role as oddsmakers—people who took bets on the Hunger Games—Wyatt earns the trust of his fellow tributes and proves that he's willing to protect those who are more vulnerable than himself. Of course, we see this right at the start of the games, during the chaotic Cornucopia bloodbath, as he steps in front of Silka's axe-throw to shield young Lou Lou (Iona Bell).

In this case, his death is truly upsetting because it comes from an act of kindness and sacrifice rather than selfishness or ambition. Wyatt isn't trying to win at someone else's expense. He simply wants to protect Lou Lou, a girl who has already been subjected to the Capitol's cruel manipulation. His willingness to put himself in harm's way makes him an easy character to root for, and his death only reinforces how little room the Games leave for such compassion.

Lou Lou is a tragic figure from the moment she is introduced, as she's essentially sent in as a mere body-double since the Capitol had to cover up the accidental death of Louella McCoy (Molly McCann). Seemingly drugged (or at least conditioned) into playing a role she doesn't fully understand, the girl is thrust into the Games without any control over her own circumstances. Despite surviving the initial bloodbath, she meets her end when she inhales poisonous pollen from the deadly plants scattered throughout the arena.

Naturally, what makes Lou Lou's death so tragic is the sheer indignity of her existence in the Games. For one, her fate was immediately set as she was sent in as a sacrificial pawn—one who doesn't even get to die under her real name. The books at least give hints about her past, but they also reinforce the disturbing lengths the Capitol is willing to go to in order to maintain the illusion of its spectacle. Lou Lou is one of the many victims of a system that sees children as disposable, and her fate is a reminder that the cruelty of the Games extends far beyond the arena's physical dangers.

The tragedy of Louella McCoy is how her death happens before she even gets the chance to enter the arena. During the tribute parade, the horses pulling the chariots are spooked by objects thrown onto the track, causing them to panic and crash. Haymitch is thrown off, while Louella is sadly left trapped in the District 12 chariot, which flips over and drags her across the path. It's brutal to watch and utterly shocking, especially since it sets the stage for Haymitch to boil with anger and openly defy President Snow (Ralph Fiennes).

Incredibly young and never even given the opportunity to fight for her own life, Louella is killed by the very spectacle that the Capitol insists is meant to honor them. If anything, it exposes just how careless and cruel the system (and its people) truly are, particularly since they flippantly replace her with another girl the next day. By erasing her loss, the Capitol once again strips a child of their dignity, just for the sake of maintaining the illusion of control.

Despite her sharp-tongued nature, Maysilee Donner grows to become one of Haymitch's closest allies as they fight to protect each other across the area. But as the number of tributes begins to dwindle, the two consciously decide to go their separate ways as neither wants to watch the other die (or be responsible for it). Moments later, a cannon fires, and they rush back towards each other, only for a flock of bird mutts to fatally attack Maysilee, with Haymitch scrambling to protect her in his arms.

Of course, the moment is agonizing to watch as Maysilee isn't just another tribute Haymitch loses along the way. She's a slice of home and a true friend who shares his determination to survive without surrendering completely to the brutality of the Capitol. Yes, there are significant changes from the book that shape her death as collateral punishment for Haymitch rather than a direct consequence of her own acts of rebellion. However, the stellar performance between Zada and Grace makes up for this omission, with their chemistry carrying the emotional weight of a relationship built on genuine care and humanity—even as they're in a competition designed to strip its participants of precisely that.

As the son of the legendary victor, Beetee (Kelvin Harrison Jr.), Ampert Latier (Percy Daggs IV) is a clever and resourceful young boy who helps recruit Haymitch to sabotage the Games as a means to undermine the Capitol's control. Their plan succeeds in disrupting the system, but the Capitol retaliates by unleashing vicious squirrel mutts on Ampert. Haymitch finds him being attacked and desperately tries to intervene. Sadly, the creatures horrifically overwhelm the young tribute, leaving Haymitch unable to save him.

Ampert's death is particularly tragic because he symbolizes the devastating risks of daring to challenge an authoritarian regime. Like some others, the young boy's fate of death was already sealed thanks to his father's prior acts of rebellion. As a result, his courage and willingness to risk everything for a chance at freedom only makes his loss all the more shattering—especially since it happens in such a brutal manner (one that's even worse and more gruesome in the books).

After unexpectedly surviving the Second Quarter Quell and returning to District 12 as a victor, Haymitch is confronted with the consequences of his defiance: his home is engulfed in flames where both his mother (Kara Tointon) and little brother, Sid (Smylie Bradwell), were trapped inside. Of course, it's clear that President Snow has made good on his threats, with Haymitch having nothing to do but watch as his entire family is taken from him. To make matters worse, the tragedy becomes even more painful when they reveal that mother and son were found holding onto each other, resulting in them being buried in the same casket.

There's something especially cruel about this punishment, as Haymitch's family are true innocents. Neither knew of his plans, and yet they were forced to pay the price. In this sense, Snow isn't merely trying to silence a rebellious victor. He's making an example of Haymitch and ensuring that his victory comes with a lifetime of suffering. The loss of Ma and Sid also strips Haymitch of the very support system he desperately needs after the Games, leaving him isolated and increasingly broken. It's a deeply personal act of vengeance, and one that helps explain why Haymitch eventually retreats into deep despair.

Just when you think Haymitch has lost so much, he's met with another devastating blow. After the funeral of his family and fellow tributes, he finally reunites with his long-love, Lenore Dove Baird (Whitney Peak), who has finally been released from captivity. And for a fleeting moment, their reunion offers a glimmer of hope as she happily pulls out a bag of gumdrops she found on the rocks, thinking them to be a gift. Haymitch, in turn, feeds one to her—only for him to quickly realize that it was a trap planted by Snow. Once again, he's left helpless in his desperation and can do nothing but watch someone he loves die in his arms.

Her death is distressing not just because it's Lenore Dove, but because of what she represents to Haymitch. Throughout the film, she's one of the few people who genuinely understands his anger at the Capitol and even encourages him to believe things can change. She isn't simply a romantic interest, but a person whose conviction and rebellious spirit help shape the person Haymitch becomes. Even as she dies, Lenore Dove makes him promise to keep fighting against the oppressive system, turning her final moments into one last act of defiance. And knowing what Haymitch's emotional journey looks like in the future makes this loss all the more heartbreaking as audiences realize that it's his memories of the people he loved that have kept him going.
