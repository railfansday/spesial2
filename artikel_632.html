BBC’s Antisemitism Crisis Represents an Unprecedented Chapter for the World’s Most Trusted News Organization

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

On Thursday, government adviser Lord John Mann announced he is launching an investigation into alleged antisemitism at the broadcaster. It is an unprecedented chapter for the corporation, long held up as one of the most venerable and reliable media institutions in the world.

For those who have been paying attention, however, the move is not surprising following a litany of public missteps dating as far back as 2021, when BBC News misreported an incident in which a group of Jewish students were attacked on a bus in Central London and then doubled down on it, despite eventually acknowledging the reporting lacked accuracy and impartiality. Later, the broadcaster steadfastly refused to reprimand Gary Lineker, one of its most high-profile sports anchors, as he repeatedly breached its much-touted social media guidelines, which were instituted precisely because of Lineker’s increasingly political Twitter activity. Under the guidelines, high-profile presenters hosting flagship programs are expected to be impartial while the show is on air and for a two-week window before and after. At the time, he called the guidelines “very sensible.”

It was only when last year Lineker reposted an Instagram story about “Zionists” alongside a rat emoji that the BBC finally cut him loose. Even then, rather than a punitive measure, his exit was presented to the public as a mutually agreed decision for the host to leave after the final “Match of the Day” episode aired the following week. The BBC’s press release went on to call him a “defining voice in football coverage” and “thank him for the contribution he has made.”

Critics compared the BBC’s apparently favorable treatment of Lineker to that of former BBC Radio 5 Live broadcaster Danny Baker, who was sacked immediately in 2019 after tweeting an image of a chimpanzee with the caption “Royal baby leaves hospital” just days after Meghan, Duchess of Sussex, gave birth, with the BBC calling it a “serious error of judgment.”

The following month saw yet another incident when the BBC broadcast a little-known rapper’s rant about a former (apparently Jewish) boss, described alternately as a “fucking Zionist” and “bald-headed cunt,” before the crowd was engaged in a chant of “Death to the IDF.” It later emerged that producers had assessed the rapper as “high risk” and yet decided to broadcast his set live.

Even as he embarked on his rant, which BBC chair Samir Shah later acknowledged consisted of “unconscionable antisemitic views,” no one at the broadcaster thought to cut the Glastonbury feed. Shah apologized for the oversight, and the BBC’s director of music, Lorna Clarke, briefly “stepped back” from her duties, although it appears little other action was taken (she has long since returned to her post).

Then, last month, it was revealed that the BBC had reached an out-of-court settlement with one of its top factual producers, Lucie Kon, after she brought a legal claim alleging antisemitic discrimination. Kon, an Emmy and Oscar winner who exec produced “Mr Nobody Against Putin,” had worked for the corporation for 25 years but said that after Oct. 7, when Hamas murdered 1,200 people in Israel and kidnapped 250 more, the atmosphere toward her changed.

The BBC rejected those claims but agreed to pay Kon £400,000 ($520,000) to settle out of court. For health reasons, Kon accepted. By comparison, the average award handed down by courts for race discrimination in the U.K. is £29,532, according to the most recent statistics.

At the same time, the BBC’s reporting on Israel has been an ongoing source of contention. There was the Gaza documentary which failed to tell viewers the narrator was the son of a senior Hamas official, the report speculating Israel had bombed a hospital in Gaza which had actually been hit by a Hamas rocket misfiring and the news story in which an anchor misrepresented a Reuters story, saying the IDF was “targeting … medical teams as well as Arabic speakers” when in fact the medical teams and Arabic speakers were part of the IDF, there to assess patients on the ground.

A leaked internal report last year — the same one that led to BBC boss Tim Davie resigning when it revealed the BBC had doctored one of President Trump’s speeches — noted that BBC Arabic, a part of the corporation’s World Service, had made 215 corrections and clarifications in its reporting of the Israel-Gaza war since 2023, amounting to two a week. At the time, a BBC spokesperson told Variety: “BBC News Arabic strives for the highest standards of journalism across its services.”

(Incidentally, the BBC World Service currently operates in 45 languages, including Arabic, Amharic, English, Hausa, Kirundi, Marathi, Tigrinya, Ukrainian, Urdu and Yoruba. It does not include Hebrew.)

So how did the BBC find itself embroiled in yet another crisis?

Part of the problem stems from the fact that the corporation effectively marks its own homework. While British newspapers are regulated by an independent body, IPSO, the BBC operates the Executive Complaints Unit to investigate grievances about itself.

But there is also a sense that the BBC, even under its new director general Matt Brittin, is not interested in engaging seriously with the issue. Each time there is a public blunder, the BBC will roll out the same playbook: issue a cut-and-paste apology (usually with reluctance), defend its reporting and make a gesture to suggest it has learned a lesson. But the sheer frequency of incidents — only a handful of which even spin out into the public realm — suggests nothing is being learned.

Earlier this month, the corporation’s deputy director-general, Rhodri Talfan Davies, was asked during an on-the-record Q&A session with journalists whether he would support an internal inquiry into antisemitism at the BBC. His response was to deny there was a “deep-rooted issue” or any “institutional discrimination” at the broadcaster. Notably, he didn’t answer the second part of the journalist’s question, namely, if Talfan Davies didn’t think there was antisemitism at the BBC, “Why do you think many of your Jewish employees think there is?”

Even the corporation’s checkbox response to Lord Mann’s announcement on Thursday gives the impression, whether intended or not, that the threat of an investigation into its internal conduct is of little consequence. When approached for comment, a BBC publicist told Variety by email, “We’d also like to direct you to the below statement, that we’ve previously issued,” before copy-and-pasting it underneath.

In his speech announcing the investigation, Lord Mann said he is “not interested in targeting individuals. That would entirely miss the point. I am targeting the institution.” The biggest clue as to the depth of the BBC’s antisemitism problem will be whether the institution even cares.
