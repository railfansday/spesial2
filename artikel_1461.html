The BAF (also known as mammalian SWI/SNF) chromatin remodelling complexes are essential regulators of nucleosome positioning and transcription factor binding1,2. Loss-of-function (LOF) mutations in genes encoding components of the BAF complexes are among the most frequent across cancer types3. However, a unifying mechanism that explains their widespread disruption remains unclear. Here we disrupted BAF function through pharmacological inhibition and ARID1A deletion across various mammalian cell types and tissues and observed a conserved chromatin response: increased accessibility at CTCF-binding sites. ARID1A LOF alone was sufficient to drive these changes, which demonstrates that canonical BAF (cBAF) disruption is the key driver of CTCF gain. Genomic profiling showed increased CTCF and SMC3 occupancy, which reveals an antagonistic relationship between cBAF and CTCF–cohesin complexes. Furthermore, cBAF loss restructured 3D genome organization, which led to a strengthening of topologically associating domain boundaries and subdivision of large domains into smaller insulated structures. Long-range enhancer–promoter loops were replaced by shorter, promoter-proximal loops anchored at CTCF-enriched sites. To determine how CTCF function is enabled following cBAF loss, we profiled CTCF-interacting cofactors and found that CTCF binds directly to fully assembled PBAF. After cBAF depletion, PBAF was recruited to sites of increased CTCF occupancy, which demonstrates that there is an antagonistic switch between SWI/SNF subcomplexes. In vivo, conditional Arid1a deletion in primary mouse cells resulted in the expansion of a highly proliferative population marked by elevated accessibility at CTCF sites. This phenotype was fully reversed by either Ctcf or Pbrm1 deletion, which establishes that cBAF and the activity of CTCF and PBAF have contrasting roles.

The BAF complex remodels chromatin by repositioning or evicting nucleosomes, which enables transcription, transcription factor (TF) binding and DNA replication1,2,3,4. Genes encoding BAF subunits are among the most frequently mutated in cancer, with LOF alterations present in around 20% of patients across nearly every tumour type3,5,6. Despite the widespread involvement of BAF complexes across cancers, a unifying mechanism of how BAF LOF contributes to transformation across diverse tissue types has not been clearly established.

In mammals, BAF complexes are highly diverse and feature numerous non-catalytic auxiliary subunits. So far, three main BAF complexes have been characterized in mammalian cells: the cBAF complex, the polybromo-BAF complex (PBAF) and the non-canonical BAF complex (ncBAF). Each of these has distinct and overlapping biochemical properties, chromatin targets and roles in disease7,8,9,10,11. The mechanisms by which mutations in BAF complexes promote oncogenesis include impaired chromatin accessibility at key enhancers and promoters and altered dynamics of interactions between transcriptional co-activators and TFs3,4,12,13. However, it remains unclear whether BAF LOF affects chromatin structure beyond nucleosome positioning and whether different BAF subunits have distinct or shared functions across tissues.

Beyond nucleosome positioning, epigenetic and transcriptional programming is controlled through higher-order architectural remodelling of the genome. However, it is unclear whether BAF complexes regulate 3D genome organization, despite growing evidence that other chromatin remodellers have a role in higher-order chromatin folding14,15,16. Although architectural proteins such as CTCF and cohesin have been extensively studied17,18,19,20, how BAF influences higher-order chromatin structures and regulates chromatin topology has not been fully explored. BAF complexes have a crucial role in nucleosome remodelling, and emerging links have been identified between other chromatin remodellers and higher-order genome organization21,22,23. On the basis of these findings, here we propose that disruptions in BAF function converge on a unifying mechanism of 3D genome reorganization. This mechanism could explain how distinct BAF LOF mutations drive oncogenesis across multiple tissue types.

Because BAF mutations predominantly cause LOF5,6 and ARID1A is the most frequently mutated BAF subunit with recurrent truncating alterations24, we modelled BAF dysfunction through ARID1A deletion (cBAF LOF) and the compound FHD-286, a SMARCA4 and SMARCA2 inhibitor24,25,26,27,28,29. We treated 15 human cell lines—including cancer cells from diverse tissues, human embryonic stem (hES) cells and non-cancerous immortalized lines—with FHD-286 for 24 h (Fig. 1a,b). All treated cell lines were BAF wild-type (WT) except SHSY5Y, which carries a SMARCA4 missense variant of unknown function (p.R973W)30. We also generated six isogenic lines using RIVA (lymphoma), Raji (lymphoma) and SUM159 (breast cancer) cell lines with heterozygous ARID1A (ARID1AHet) or homozygous ARID1A (ARID1AHom) deletions or knockdown via short hairpin RNA, as well as WT ARID1A.

Using ATAC–seq to assess chromatin accessibility, we identified regions where chromatin either opened or closed following BAF complex loss or inhibition. Analyses of sites with reduced chromatin accessibility (in closing peaks) did not identify TFs common across cell types. Instead, TFs in closing regions were specific to the cell type (for example, SOX5 in skin, HNF4A in liver and SPI1 in lymphoid and myeloid cell lines; Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Many of these TFs interact with BAF complexes (for example, JUN, BATF, NFKB2, ETS1, SPI1, POU5F1 (also known as OCT4), SOX2 and SOX10)21,24. This result supports the notion that BAF complexes modulate cell-context-specific TF activity3,31,32,33,34,35,36,37,38. By contrast, opening peaks were enriched for CTCF motifs in 20 out of the 21 cell lines (Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Figs. 2, 3 and 5). Opening CTCF peaks showed significantly lower cross-cell-type variability than closing TF peaks, which indicated that there is cell-context-independent CTCF regulation by BAF (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). Notably, the CTCF effect was not driven by a small number of loci. Instead, it was an intermediate but broad and coordinated shift across many sites. For example, in RIVA cells, a comparison between ARID1A knockdown and WT cells showed that CTCF motif peaks accounted for 47% of opening peaks and 5% of closing peaks in ARID1A-deficient cells (Cohen’s h = 1.30, Cramer’s V = 0.51; Extended Data Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 1).

These effects were conserved in mice. FHD-286-treated mouse cell lines and primary Arid1aHet B cells from Arid1a conditional knockout mice24 also showed the gain in CTCF-site accessibility (Fig. 1d,e). Overall, the strongest gains occurred in lymphoid and myeloid lines, which also show the highest baseline CTCF mRNA and protein expression across healthy tissues and cancers30,39,40 (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 1d). An orthogonal TF motif enrichment method, HOMER, confirmed CTCF enrichment across cell lines, with lymphoid and myeloid tissues ranking the highest (Supplementary Fig. 6a). We focused our subsequent experiments on lymphoid (RIVA) and breast cancer (SUM159) models (Fig. 1g,h).

To directly measure occupancy, we performed CTCF chromatin immunoprecipitation with sequencing (ChIP–seq) in ARID1AHet and WT cells. Chromatin regions that opened after ARID1A deletion showed increased CTCF binding in both lymphoma and breast cancer cells, with stronger gains in the lymphoid line (Fig. 2a,b). Because CTCF cooperates with cohesin at topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries18,41,42,43,44, we next profiled the core cohesin subunit SMC3. SMC3 occupancy increased globally, most prominently at sites with both enhanced accessibility and higher CTCF binding (Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Fig. 6b). Moreover, the majority of newly accessible CTCF sites overlapped with SMC3 peaks (Fig. 2c). mRNA levels of CTCF and subunits of cohesin and BAF complexes were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 6c), as were CTCF protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 6d). This finding indicates that the CTCF gain reflects increased chromatin binding rather than protein abundance.

To test whether CTCF itself drives the gain in accessibility, we acutely depleted CTCF by CRISPR in WT and ARID1AHet lymphoma B cells and performed ATAC–seq 48 h after transfection with CTCF-targeting Cas9 ribonucleoprotein. During this window of time, CTCF loss does not lead to effects on viability or proliferation45,46 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3). Principal component analysis (PCA) separated samples by genotype (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Consistent with previous observations24, ARID1A mutations caused loss of accessibility at NF-KB2, NF-KB1, REL and RUNX1 motifs (Fig. 2f). Moreover, CTCF depletion in the ARID1AHet background restored accessibility at CTCF sites to WT levels without affecting other TFs (Fig. 2g,h). These results establish these peaks as bona fide CTCF sites and rule out CTCF as a driver of the other TF-related changes. Accessibility gains correlated genome-wide with the expression of nearby genes, which therefore links them to transcriptional output (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Delta-delta analysis revealed that 93.5% of peaks (1,971 out of 2,108) that opened at CTCF sites in ARID1AHet cells reverted towards WT when CTCF was depleted (Fig. 2i, Extended Data Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 6e,f). To test whether ARID1A heterozygosity acts as a LOF alteration, we compared chromatin accessibility profiles of ARID1AHet cells to those in which ARID1A was completely knocked out. Differential accessibility profiles in ARID1AHet cells strongly overlapped with those of ARID1AHom SUM159 cells and of ARID1A-knockdown RIVA cells, in which full knockout is not viable (Extended Data Fig. 1g).

To determine whether the increased accessibility at CTCF motifs reflects altered nucleosome positioning at the motif itself, we inferred nucleosome positions using nucleoATAC47. The analysis identified a subset of CTCF sites with a nucleosome positioned directly over the CTCF motif in WT cells. This motif-centred nucleosome was displaced in ARID1AHet cells and restored in ARID1AHet cells with CTCF also depleted. This result couples nucleosome displacement after cBAF loss to CTCF occupancy (Extended Data Fig. 1h).

As stronger CTCF sites drive loop extrusion48, we ranked CTCF ChIP–seq peaks by baseline WT intensity and observed that stronger peaks gained greater accessibility in ARID1A-deficient cells than weaker ones (Extended Data Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 6g). These data suggest that ARID1A controls higher-order chromatin organization through CTCF and cohesin distribution.

To test whether the CTCF response occurs in non-transformed cells, we treated hES cells (MEL-1) with FHD-286 for 24 h. CTCF and SMC3 binding increased, although more weakly than in cancer lines (Supplementary Fig. 6h). This result probably reflects the dependence of pluripotent cells on SWI/SNF for stemness and differentiation that is triggered by prolonged pan-BAF inhibition. To uncouple the immediate chromatin response from differentiation, we engineered an ARID1A dTAG degron in hES cells and performed ATAC–seq, CTCF ChIP–seq and ARID1A ChIP–seq after rapid ARID1A depletion (Extended Data Fig. 2a). CTCF-site accessibility increased at both 6 h and 24 h, with strong enrichment already at 6 h (Extended Data Fig. 2b). ARID1A ChIP–seq confirmed loss of ARID1A occupancy (Extended Data Fig. 2c), and CTCF ChIP–seq showed a consistent, broadly distributed gain across more than 10,000 pre-existing CTCF peaks rather than de novo sites (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Furthermore, at the same regions that showed gain of CTCF, we observed a strong loss of ARID1A, which suggested that these loci were previously bound by ARID1A, which prevented CTCF binding (Extended Data Fig. 2c). To validate our findings in an independent setting, we performed a short FHD-286 treatment time course in RIVA cells. CTCF accessibility increased as early as 4 h after treatment, with a progressive increase at 12 h and 24 h, which indicated that CTCF responds rapidly to BAF inhibition. These results provide support for direct chromatin antagonism between BAF activity and CTCF occupancy (Extended Data Fig. 2e).

If CTCF functionally mediates the pathogenic consequences of cBAF loss, concurrent CTCF impairment should attenuate them. Consistent with this idea, patients with alterations in both ARID1A and CTCF showed significantly improved overall survival compared with those with an ARID1A alteration alone (Fig. 2j). To test whether this result reflects a deficit in the ARID1A-loss-driven proliferative advantage, we isolated primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) from embryos carrying conditional floxed alleles of Arid1a, Ctcf or both. Deletion was induced by Cre–GFP transduction followed by sorting of GFP-positive cells, and efficient recombination was confirmed by genotyping (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 12a). Arid1a-deficient MEFs proliferated faster than empty-vector controls, and concurrent Ctcf deletion restored proliferation to near-WT levels (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g). This finding indicates that the fitness advantage due to cBAF loss depends on CTCF.

If CTCF gain drives the proliferative phenotype of ARID1A loss, CTCF overexpression alone should phenocopy it. In RIVA lines overexpressing CTCF in ARID1A WT and heterozygous backgrounds, CTCF overexpression in WT cells accelerated growth to ARID1AHet levels (Fig. 2k). High CTCF expression was also associated with more aggressive disease and worse clinical outcomes in patient data (Extended Data Fig. 2h).

CTCF works with the cohesin complex to form TAD boundaries that confine genomic interactions in specific regions42,49. To directly assess the impact of ARID1A deficiency on chromatin 3D architecture and CTCF-associated loop formation at high resolution, we performed Micro-C50,51 on ARID1AHet and WT lymphoma cells. Consistent with the gain of cohesin and CTCF occupancy, ARID1AHet cells showed increased intra-TAD contacts that were strengthened more than weakened (Fig. 3a), and a global shift towards intra-TAD over inter-TAD interactions (Fig. 3b). Moreover, in line with the gain in CTCF binding, ARID1AHet cells showed TAD splitting at higher frequency than merge, loss or complex rearrangements (Fig. 3c), with new boundaries visible in formerly continuous TADs on contact maps (Fig. 3d). To test whether CTCF gain drives this splitting, we examined insulation scores at sites of differential CTCF and SMC3 binding. Regions gaining CTCF showed increased insulation (a lower insulation score indicates fewer cross-boundary contacts; Fig. 3e), and boundaries overlapping CTCF peaks had higher CTCF binding in ARID1AHet cells than in WT cells (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Thus, enhanced CTCF occupancy drives TAD splitting and boundary insulation after cBAF loss.

To test whether these structural changes are functional, we measured promoter connectivity in split TADs. Split TADs showed a higher proportion of promoters gaining connectivity than both WT counterparts and random unchanged TADs (Fig. 3g). This finding indicates that splitting reinforces local promoter interactions by limiting longer-range contacts. Indeed, genes in strengthened TADs had increased expression in ARID1AHet cells, whereas genes in weakened TADs were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 8b,c).

One of the main functions of TADs is to enable connection and coordination between genes contained in their borders. Because genes in TADs tend to manifest coordinated epigenetic marks necessary for coordinated expression levels52,53,54, we asked whether splitting disrupts this coordination. We performed RNA-seq and ChIP–seq for H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K4me1 and H3.3 marks in ARID1AHet and WT cells, then compared concordance in split, single (preserved boundaries with altered strength) and stable (preserved boundaries and strength) TADs. Split TADs showed greater discordance in chromatin marks and transcription than stable or single TADs (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g), which indicated that splitting establishes distinct chromatin environments. Thus, cBAF normally maintains regulatory coordination by preventing CTCF from subdividing shared domains. Finally, structural loops showed increased connectivity in ARID1AHet cells (Fig. 3h), a result consistent with enhanced cohesin stalling at gained CTCF sites. Together, these results show that cBAF loss reshapes 3D chromatin architecture through increased CTCF and cohesin activity (Fig. 3i).

To test whether BAF insulator antagonism is conserved beyond mammals, we examined Drosophila S2 cells, which use a diversified insulator repertoire, including Ctcf, Beaf and Su(Hw). CRISPR depletion of osa, the Drosophila ARID1A orthologue and a core subunit of the BAP complex, increased chromatin accessibility not only at Ctcf motifs but also at Beaf and Su(Hw) motifs (Extended Data Fig. 2i,j). Thus, SWI/SNF loss broadly opens architectural protein-binding sites across metazoans, which indicates that cBAF normally restrains engagement of multiple insulator classes.

To identify CTCF cofactors that might mediate 3D architectural changes after cBAF loss, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP–MS) for CTCF in lymphoid cells. CTCF co-precipitated ISWI subunits, consistent with its previously reported role in CTCF function21, and all PBAF-specific subunits (PBRM1, ARID2, PHF10 and BRD7) together with BAF-shared subunits (SMARCC1, SMARCC2, SMARCE1, SMARCB1, SMARCD1, SMARCD2, SMARCA4, SMARCA2 and BCL7A). By contrast, cBAF-specific and ncBAF-specific subunits were depleted relative to IgG (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 9a). Reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation experiments for BRD7 and PBRM1 recovered CTCF and the PBAF-specific subunit ARID2 but not the cBAF-specific subunit ARID1A (Fig. 4b). IP–MS for PBRM1 recovered all expected PBAF subunits and CTCF at levels comparable to PBAF core subunits (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 9b). These data reveal a previously unknown association between CTCF and all subunits of the PBAF complex.

To confirm that CTCF and PBAF interact directly, rather than being co-precipitated on the same DNA fragment, we performed additional CTCF IP and western blot experiments in the presence of endonuclease. The CTCF–PBAF interaction was preserved after DNase and RNase treatment (Extended Data Fig. 3a), and was detected in breast cancer (SUM159) and Burkitt lymphoma (Raji) cells (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). This result confirms the reproducibility of the relationship across cell types.

To assess whether CTCF associates with fully assembled PBAF, we performed glycerol gradient fractionation and profiled cBAF, PBAF, ISWI, CTCF and cohesin subunits in RIVA ARID1AHet cells and SUM159 ARID1AHom cells alongside their WT counterparts. PBAF components distributed to higher molecular mass fractions as expected. In both cell lines, CTCF co-migration with PBAF-containing fractions was increased in ARID1A-deficient cells compared with WT cells, with the strongest shift in SUM159 ARID1AHom cells (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Fig. 9c,d). ChIP–seq for PBRM1 and BRD7 showed increased binding in ARID1A-deficient cells, with PBRM1 gained broadly and BRD7 less robustly (Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). Because PBRM1 is the last subunit to be incorporated during PBAF assembly55, the broader PBRM1 gain is consistent with the preferential recruitment of fully assembled PBAF to gained CTCF sites after cBAF loss.

The preferential CTCF–PBAF interaction suggested that cBAF and PBAF have opposing roles at CTCF sites: cBAF restricts CTCF function, whereas PBAF promotes it. Because FHD-286 targets both complexes, we asked whether the FHD-286-mediated net chromatin effect primarily reflects cBAF inhibition. ATAC–seq profiles from FHD-286 treated and ARID1A-depleted cells showed strongly overlapping differential peaks and concordant directionality, which indicated that FHD-286 phenocopies cBAF loss (Extended Data Fig. 1g). Integration of FHD-286-induced accessibility changes with ARID1A and PBRM1 ChIP–seq in SUM159 cells revealed that peaks that lost accessibility were enriched for cBAF binding, whereas peaks that gained accessibility were enriched for PBAF binding (Extended Data Fig. 3f–h). This finding indicates that the FHD-286 phenotype is driven largely by cBAF inhibition rather than an equivalent blockade of all BAF assemblies.

To determine whether PBAF remains functionally engaged under FHD-286 treatment despite loss of cBAF, we examined ARID1A and PBRM1 occupancy by ChIP–seq after FHD-286 treatment in SUM159 cells. ARID1A occupancy decreased, whereas PBRM1-associated signals increased, which indicated that pharmacological-mediated BAF inhibition does not eliminate the PBAF program and instead enables a gain of PBAF-linked chromatin function (Fig. 4f). CTCF IP in FHD-286-treated RIVA and SUM159 cells showed preserved CTCF–PBAF association, which indicates that this interaction is not disrupted by the inhibitor (Extended Data Fig. 3i).

To dissect subcomplex-specific contributions, we performed ATAC–seq in cells treated for 24 h with the following inhibitors or degraders: BRD-K98645985, which inhibits ARID1A and ARID1B in cBAF9; FHD-609, which degrades BRD9 in ncBAF27; PBRM1-BD2-IN-8, which inhibits PBRM1 in PBAF56; and FHT006710, which degrades both BRD9 and BRD7 in ncBAF and PBAF. Degraders efficiently depleted their targets without altering ARID1A expression (Extended Data Fig. 3j), and dimensionality reduction separated samples by the complex targeted (Fig. 4g). PBAF inhibition decreased accessibility at CTCF sites, a result consistent with a CTCF-enabling role (Fig. 4h,i). By contrast, cBAF inhibition and ncBAF degradation increased CTCF motif enrichment (Fig. 4h,i), with inferred CTCF binding increased at peaks opened by PBRM1 inhibition (Extended Data Fig. 3k). Thus, PBAF enables CTCF binding, whereas cBAF and ncBAF antagonize it, a result consistent with previous findings that BRD9 loss increases CTCF binding in haematopoietic stem cells57.

To test whether the proliferation gain in ARID1AHet cells depends on PBAF, we generated heterozygous and homozygous deletions of the PBAF subunit ARID2 in the ARID1AHet background. ARID1AHetARID2Het and ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells proliferated slower than ARID1AHet cells in an ARID2 dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4j,k), which eliminated the proliferative advantage of ARID1A loss. This finding indicates that PBAF activity drives the accelerated proliferation of ARID1A mutant lymphoma cells, but does not exclude the fact that loss of other genes could produce similar proliferation effects.

To test whether CTCF, cohesin and PBAF gains colocalize at the same genomic sites, we developed an unbiased multimodal clustering model that integrates ATAC–seq, CTCF ChIP–seq and PBRM1 ChIP–seq data across ARID1AHet and WT cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). This model identified 16 chromatin-state clusters (Extended Data Fig. 4c).

Clusters G1–G8 (CTCF-gain clusters) showed increased CTCF binding in ARID1AHet cells. All CTCF-gain clusters except G3 and G4 also gained PBRM1 and SMC3 binding and expression of the nearest gene, with cluster G8 showing the largest changes (Extended Data Fig. 4d). All clusters gained BRD7 binding, an independent PBAF subunit not used for clustering, as well as the ATPase SMARCA4, consistent with active PBAF remodelling. The same regions gained H3.3 but not H3.1 marks (Extended Data Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 11), which indicated elevated nucleosome turnover at sites of CTCF, PBAF and cohesin gain.

After integration of the inhibitor and degrader ATAC–seq data, the top CTCF-gain clusters G1–G5 gained accessibility after cBAF or ncBAF disruption, mirroring the ARID1AHet versus WT pattern, and lost accessibility after PBAF inhibition (Extended Data Fig. 4e). This result further demonstrates that PBAF enables CTCF binding when cBAF is lost. Clusters G1–G5 also gained accessibility across a panel of multiple cell lines treated with BAF inhibitors (Extended Data Fig. 4f), which indicated that these are sites shared across tissues. Overall, 60–90% of sites in these clusters contained the M1 CTCF motif, which is associated with strong CTCF binding and insulator function58,59, compared with less than 50% in other clusters (Extended Data Fig. 4g). CTCF-gain clusters were also enriched for TAD boundaries, with the strongest gains in insulation at clusters G1–G5 (Extended Data Fig. 4h,i). Together, these data establish that cBAF loss drives TAD reorganization at sites that gain CTCF, PBAF and cohesin, with PBAF functionally replacing cBAF to enable CTCF-mediated insulation.

Given that PBAF preferentially localizes to promoter–proximal regions6,8, we speculated that cBAF normally maintains long-range enhancer–promoter loops that collapse into short-range promoter–proximal interactions at CTCF–PBAF sites after ARID1A loss. Analyses of Micro-C contact frequency by genomic distance showed that newly opened CTCF peaks in ARID1AHet cells have increased short-range interactions peaking around 500 kb and reduced long-range contacts above 1 Mb (Extended Data Fig. 5a), whereas unchanged CTCF sites showed no such shift. Opened SMC3 peaks showed the same distance shift (Extended Data Fig. 5b), a finding consistent with cohesin-mediated rewiring.

To determine how chromatin rewiring affects promoter function, we performed single-cell multiome profiling in ARID1AHet and WT cells and scored each gene by the correlation between expression and connectivity inferred from peak co-accessibility (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Genes with increased connectivity in ARID1AHet cells showed elevated transcriptional activity (Extended Data Fig. 5d–f). CTCF peaks that gained occupancy after ARID1A loss showed enhanced connectivity and more promoter–enhancer contacts (Extended Data Fig. 5g), and genes overlapping these peaks were upregulated (Extended Data Fig. 5h). PBRM1-bound regions gained in ARID1AHet cells showed the same pattern of increased connectivity, additional promoter–enhancer loops and elevated gene expression (Extended Data Fig. 5i,j), as did sites gained by the independent PBAF subunit BRD7 (Extended Data Fig. 5k,l). Thus, cBAF loss drives PBAF and CTCF to rewire promoter–enhancer contacts towards short-range promoter–proximal interactions that increase gene expression.

We have thus far shown that cBAF deficiency triggers a CTCF gain-of-function program with two hallmarks: enhanced intra-TAD insulation with domain subdivision and increased promoter–enhancer connectivity. To test which of these hallmarks depend on PBAF, we generated additional Micro-C profiles in ARID1AHet and ARID1AHetARID2Hom RIVA cells. The increased intra-TAD contacts (of single TADs) seen in ARID1AHet cells were attenuated in ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells, with log2(fold change) distributions centring near zero (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Furthermore, the intra-to-inter-TAD ratio, elevated in ARID1AHet cells, returned to WT levels in ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells (Extended Data Fig. 6b), which indicates that there is reduced insulation after PBAF loss in the ARID1A-deficient background.

After stratification of TADs by their ARID1AHet behaviour, ARID2 deletion partially but selectively rescued the strengthening class. That is, strengthened TADs lost contact enrichment in ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells, whereas weakening TADs were unchanged (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Genome-wide Micro-C signals at TAD boundaries, strengthened in ARID1AHet cells, were reduced towards WT levels in ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells (Extended Data Fig. 6d), and representative loci show attenuated boundary strengthening and TAD splitting after ARID2 deletion (Extended Data Fig. 6e).

We next tested whether the promoter–enhancer connectivity gain in ARID1AHet cells depends on PBAF. To that end, we performed additional single-cell multiome analyses that included ARID1AHetARID2Hom RIVA cells (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Connectivity in ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells shifted back towards WT levels (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b), and loops strengthened or gained in ARID1AHet were reduced after ARID2 deletion (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d,i), which indicated that PBAF contributes to the formation or stabilization of these new promoter–enhancer contacts. Expression of genes near gained CTCF or PBAF sites was partially restored towards WT levels in ARID1AHetARID2Hom cells (Extended Data Fig. 7e–h). This result links PBAF-dependent rewiring to the transcriptional consequences of cBAF loss.

As PBAF deficiency only partially rescued the ARID1AHet chromatin phenotype, we tested whether ISWI, a remodeller previously linked to CTCF function and recovered in our CTCF IP–MS data (Fig. 4a), is also involved. We deleted the ISWI catalytic subunit SMARCA5 (also known as SNF2H) in ARID1AHet RIVA cells and performed ATAC–seq. Loss of SMARCA5 strongly reduced accessibility at CTCF motifs in ARID1AHetSMARCA5Hom cells (Extended Data Fig. 8a), which indicated that ISWI is a major contributor to nucleosome remodelling at CTCF motifs after cBAF loss, with PBAF subsequently reinforcing CTCF-dependent insulation and loop architecture.

We next tested whether ISWI is required for the ARID1AHet proliferative phenotype. In independent CRISPR clones, ARID1AHetSMARCA5Hom cells proliferated significantly more slowly than ARID1AHet cells (Extended Data Fig. 8b), which eliminated the proliferative advantage of ARID1A loss. The reduced proliferation rate relative to WT cells is consistent with ISWI loss reducing CTCF accessibility below WT levels (Extended Data Fig. 8a). Thus, like PBAF (Fig. 4j,k), ISWI is required for the proliferative consequences of cBAF loss. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that loss of other essential genes could produce comparable proliferation phenotypes. Our data establish that ISWI activity is necessary, not that it is specifically required.

On the basis of these new data, we favour a model in which PBAF and ISWI contribute to CTCF gain-of-function in the setting of cBAF deficiency, consistent with the antagonistic relationship between cBAF and CTCF for chromatin architecture. At the same time, PBAF and ISWI may act through distinct mechanisms at distinct genomic loci or converge on the same sites. We therefore compared CTCF binding, PBAF binding and ISWI-dependent accessibility. Approximately half of ISWI-dependent CTCF sites were also associated with PBAF. Conversely, about half of shared CTCF and PBAF sites lost accessibility after SMARCA5 deletion (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Thus, PBAF and ISWI frequently converge on the same CTCF-linked regulatory elements, with smaller subsets preferentially dependent on one or the other.

Mutations in components of the BAF complex are particularly common in lymphoid malignancies, with ARID1A the most frequently mutated subunit60,61. More than 35% of diffuse large B cell lymphomas (DLBCLs) carry BAF mutations, and DLBCL arises from germinal centre B cells, which cycle between the highly proliferative dark zone (DZ), where they undergo rapid division and somatic hypermutation, and the light zone (LZ), where they are selected for affinity-matured antigen receptors62,63,64 (Fig. 5a). Each zone is defined by distinct chromatin and transcriptional states. Because the DZ-to-LZ transition involves extensive chromatin and architectural reorganization54,65,66,67, we asked whether ARID1A loss might disrupt this program through CTCF. ATAC–seq of WT germinal centre B cells showed that CTCF motif accessibility was significantly higher in DZ than LZ cells (Fig. 5b), which suggested that there is a CTCF-dependent component to the DZ state. Conditional Arid1a heterozygous deletion in mice can skew the germinal centre reaction towards the DZ24, and reanalyses of ATAC–seq data from these mice revealed that CTCF motifs were the most enriched in newly accessible regions (Fig. 5c). Together, these data link DZ expansion in Arid1a-deficient germinal centres to increased CTCF accessibility.

To test whether CTCF is required for the observed Arid1a-driven expansion of the DZ, we crossed a conditional Ctcf knockout mouse model (Ctcfflox/floxCg1Cre/Cre), which lacks Ctcf only in germinal centre B cells, with Arid1a-conditional knockout mice (Fig. 5d). Mice were immunized with the T cell-dependent antigen sheep red blood cells to trigger germinal centre formation and Cre recombination. Germinal centre B cells were sorted at day 10, the peak of the immune reaction. Arid1aHetCtcfHet double heterozygosity fully reversed the DZ expansion phenotype (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 12c). This rescue effect was reproduced with a second T cell-dependent antigen (NP-KLH), a result that confirms its robustness (Extended Data Fig. 9a,b and Supplementary Fig. 12b,c). Notably, double heterozygotes did exhibit restored overall germinal centre size, which is reduced in Arid1a-deficient mice through loss of PU.1 and NF-κB activity24. This result demonstrates that the ARID1A–CTCF axis selectively governs DZ expansion rather than germinal centre formation per se.

To resolve the observed rescue effects at the chromatin level, we performed ATAC–seq on sorted DZ and LZ germinal centre B cells from Arid1aHet, Arid1a WT and Arid1aHetCtcfHet mice. PCA separated samples by cell type along principal component 1 (PC1; 33.4% of variance) and by genotype along PC2, with Arid1aHetCtcfHet cells reverting towards WT (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). As observed in the lymphoma cell lines (Fig. 2f), Ctcf deletion had a larger effect on reducing accessibility at CTCF sites than Arid1a loss had on increasing it (Fig. 5e,f). Rescued sites were highly enriched for CTCF signatures, based on hypergeometric testing (Fig. 5g), which confirmed them as direct CTCF targets and enriched for a PBAF signature. This result corroborates our observations in cell lines that Arid1a loss redirects remodelling activity from cBAF to PBAF in vivo.

To track chromatin changes during tumour evolution, we performed an in vivo lymphomagenesis time-course experiment, combining Arid1a heterozygosity with BCL2 overexpression (VavP-Bcl2), a cooperating driver that produces a follicular lymphoma-like disease68,69. We chose this combination because ARID1A loss and BCL2 activation frequently co-occur in human lymphomas61. Bone marrow from Bcl2-Tg or Bcl2-TgArid1aHet donors was transplanted into recipient mice, which were euthanized at 2 months (pre-disease), 6 months (early disease) or at overt lymphoma onset. DZ cells were sorted from splenocytes for ATAC–seq at each stage (Extended Data Fig. 9e and Supplementary Fig. 12c).

CTCF was consistently enriched at regions that gained accessibility in Bcl2-TgArid1aHet mice relative to Bcl2-Tg controls, both at individual time points and in aggregate (Extended Data Fig. 9f,g). This result shows that the CTCF accessibility program is engaged early in cBAF-deficient tumour development. PCA separated healthy DZ cells by genotype, and projection of lymphoma samples onto the same PC space resulted in both Arid1a WT and Bcl2-TgArid1aHet lymphomas clustering with Arid1aHet healthy DZ cells rather than Arid1a WT cells (Extended Data Fig. 9e). This finding indicates that chromatin changes that arise during lymphomagenesis overlap with those already present in pre-malignant Arid1aHet cells. Motif enrichment confirmed stronger CTCF accessibility gains in Bcl2-TgArid1aHet lymphomas and weaker gains in Arid1a WT lymphomas (Extended Data Fig. 9f,g). Thus, CTCF accessibility gains mark the earliest chromatin events of cBAF-driven lymphomagenesis.

To resolve cell-fate effects that may be obscured during bulk profiling, we performed single-cell multiome analyses on germinal centre B cells from Arid1aHet, CtcfHet and WT mice (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 12c). Using published annotations24,70,71, we classified cells as DZ, DZ-to-LZ transition, LZ or recycling (back from LZ to DZ). Arid1a loss expanded the DZ compartment, whereas Ctcf loss expanded the LZ (Fig. 5h). After ordering cells along a germinal centre pseudotime, CTCF motif accessibility was elevated in Arid1aHet cells during the DZ phase and normalized to WT levels by late LZ and pre-memory stages, whereas CtcfHet cells showed the strongest accessibility reduction in DZ cells (Fig. 5i). Thus, ARID1A loss reinforces the DZ program through early CTCF accessibility gains, whereas Ctcf haploinsufficiency blocks DZ establishment, a finding consistent with a model in which excessive CTCF activity after cBAF loss locks cells in a proliferative DZ state.

To test in vivo the central premise of our model, that the Arid1a haploinsufficient phenotype requires PBAF activity, we performed a genetic rescue experiment. We generated a conditional Pbrm1 knockout on the Cg1cre background and crossed it with our Arid1a conditional line, which produced four groups: control (Cg1cre only), Pbrm1Het, Arid1aHet and double heterozygous (Pbrm1HetArid1aHet). After immunization to induce germinal centre formation and Cre-mediated recombination, we quantified DZ and LZ proportions by flow cytometry at the peak immune response. Arid1aHet mice showed the expected DZ-biased hyperproliferative phenotype, whereas Pbrm1HetArid1aHet double heterozygotes were fully restored to WT DZ and LZ proportions (Extended Data Fig. 9h and Supplementary Fig. 12b). This result mirrors the rescue observed with Ctcf heterozygosity and establishes in vivo that PBAF activity is required for the Arid1a LOF phenotype. These data are consistent with a model in which cBAF loss promotes PBAF gain permissive for elevated CTCF binding and altered cell fate.

Here we have shown that cBAF loss drives a conserved gain of CTCF and cohesin binding that restructures 3D genome architecture and reprograms transcription, which is enabled by an antagonistic PBAF switch. Our results therefore reveal a previously unrecognized interplay between SWI/SNF chromatin remodellers in controlling 3D genome structure (Fig. 3i). cBAF operates at enhancers to facilitate TF binding and sustain enhancer–promoter communication across long genomic distances3,4,7,31,72,73. When cBAF is depleted, these enhancer–promoter interactions weaken21,74,75,76, which potentially facilitates alternative remodellers to gain influence. We show that PBAF, which primarily functions at promoters and proximal regulatory elements10,11,77,78, gains increased access to chromatin to stabilize short-range promoter interactions and facilitates CTCF binding at TAD boundaries. Our biochemical data show that CTCF directly interacts with both PBAF and ISWI, which provides support for a model in which CTCF coordinates recruitment of these remodellers to effect distinct steps of chromatin opening, CTCF occupancy and domain insulation.

A puzzling aspect of this mechanism is that cBAF, the function of which is to create nucleosome-depleted regions, restricts CTCF binding, with CTCF sites becoming accessible only after cBAF loss. We propose that cBAF limits CTCF binding at a subset of loci by maintaining a nucleosome that is positioned directly over the CTCF motif, which then occludes the binding sequence even when flanking chromatin is remodelled (Extended Data Fig. 1h). When cBAF dosage is reduced, this constraint is relieved and CTCF can engage DNA. Loss of cBAF permits new CTCF binding events that can activate previously silenced genes. Beyond its architectural role, CTCF contains a transactivation domain that decondenses chromatin79 and binds near many active promoters80. Our findings demonstrate that CTCF occupancy gained after cBAF loss actively drives gene expression at newly accessible sites (Extended Data Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 11).

The enhanced cohesin (SMC3) loading following BAF loss is probably a direct consequence of increased CTCF binding, as CTCF directs cohesin binding to DNA loops41,43,67,81. Although SMARCA4 can interact with RAD21 (refs. 82,83), the responsible BAF complex was unclear. Our findings link this interaction to PBAF and CTCF recruitment. When cBAF is removed, cohesin accumulates at CTCF sites, which reinforces loop extrusion stalling and explains the 3D architectural changes we observed: more cohesin terminating loops at newly engaged CTCF sites strengthens insulation and increases intra-domain contact density. Increased CTCF and cohesin at more sites introduces additional loop anchors, which in turn shortens average loop lengths in TADs.

Although cBAF loss increased CTCF accessibility across most of the tissues we profiled, a subset of our cell types did not show significant changes, which suggests that there are cell-type-specific modifiers of this program. Consistent with such context dependence, two studies of T cells reported that PBAF loss, rather than cBAF loss, increased CTCF motif enrichment at newly accessible chromatin84,85. This result is in contrast to our model, in which PBAF stabilizes CTCF binding after cBAF loss. Together with our findings, this discrepancy highlights the context-dependent nature of chromatin remodelling in modifying the function of this antagonism. Context-specific TFs may compete with CTCF for remodeller activity. Additional cofactors that interact with BAF complexes could tune the balance between cBAF and PBAF engagement at CTCF sites. Furthermore, the proliferation rate and cell cycle state may set cohesin availability for CTCF-mediated looping, given the prominent role of cohesin in sister chromatid cohesion.

Raw sequencing reads were assessed using FastQC. Sequence adapters were removed with Trim Galore!, and the cleaned reads were aligned to either the mm10, hg38 or dm6 genome reference using BWA. Duplicate reads were identified with Picard94. Post-alignment, mitochondrial reads, duplicates, reads from blacklisted regions, secondary alignments, multimapping reads, reads with insert sizes greater than 2 kb, incorrectly oriented paired reads or orphan reads were removed. Peak calling was performed with MACS2 using a shift of −75 bp and an extension of 150 bp95. ATAC–seq quality control was done by evaluating fragment length distribution, FRIP scores, transcription start site (TSS) enrichment and high-quality autosomal reads with Picard, ataqv and MultiQC96,97. Consensus peaks were determined by overlapping regions covered in at least two samples. FeatureCounts was used to quantify reads in the peaks98. Data normalization for visualizations and unsupervised clustering was done using variance stabilizing transformation (vst)99. Differential peak accessibility was analysed using DESeq2 with a negative binomial model, filtering for an absolute fold change of 1.5 (log2[fold change] of 0.58) and an FDR-adjusted P value of 0.01. Peaks were annotated by identifying the most significantly changing peak within 5 kb of the TSS of each gene and assigning it to the promoter or potential enhancers of the gene, as defined by GREAT100. Genes were ranked by log2 fold change or the Wald statistic and analysed using GSEA with the MSigDB database and curated gene signatures from RNA-seq and literature101,102, filtering for adjusted P  values less than 0.05. Transcription factor regulatory potential was evaluated by comparing mammalian motifs in JASPAR to sequences of accessible peaks103,104. A multivariate model was used to assess the relationship between log2 fold changes in peak accessibility and motif presence, adjusting for GC content, that is, log2[fold change = β0 + β1 × x1 + β2 × x2 + … + βN × GC, where xi is a Boolean variable for the presence of a TF motif in a peak as a binary variable and GC for the GC-content of the peak. Effect sizes and P values were derived using ordinary least squares regression with robust standard errors. Furthermore, TF-binding sites, binding scores and differential binding were analysed using TOBIAS footprinting105. Pseudomedians and 95% CI were calculated using the wilcox.test() function in R (v.4.3.1). Read density heatmaps were generated from normalized bigwig files using deepTools (v.3.5.5). Harmony-corrected values were used with Signac’s FindMultiModalNeighbors to generate multimodal nearest neighbours, which were used for UMAP visualization. Cell-type labels were assigned using RNA expression and the Seurat TransferLabel function with reference datasets70. Gene signature module scores were calculated using the AddModuleScore function with a control value of 5. Slingshot (v.2.8.0) was used to generate pseudotime, based on the first and second Harmony-corrected PCA of expression, starting from Centroblasts. The density and difference in density of cells for WT and ARID1AHet cells along pseudotime were calculated, with significance assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. ChromVAR scores for the JASPAR2020 core vertebrate motif list were calculated using Signac’s AddMotif and RunChromVAR functions on the filtered, combined peak set. Gene signature or ChromVAR accessibility of specific motifs were plotted against pseudotime, divided into ten deciles. Expression or accessibility for each decile was compared between WT and the indicated genotype using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and the differences in splines were plotted and coloured according to decile significance. Signac LinkPeaks was used to identify putative promoter–enhancer loops using the default settings, including a maximum distance of 500 kb for all genes. The multiome processing code can be accessed at GitHub (https://github.com/crchin/multiome_process).

Raw sequencing reads were processed using the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline112 (v.3.12.0) with default parameters. In the pipeline, Salmon (v.1.10.1) was used in quasi-mapping mode for transcript-level quantification against the GRCh38 human reference transcriptome (GENCODE v.33 annotation). Transcript abundances were imported into R and summarized to gene-level counts using the tximport package. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v.1.38.3). Genes encoding PBAF-specific subunits, as well as CTCF and cohesin subunits, were selected for targeted analysis. Count data were normalized using variance stabilizing transformation for plotting.

Paired-end reads, obtained in biological duplicates for WT and ARID1AHet cells, were converted to valid pairs files and cool files following Dovetail’s Micro-C analysis protocol. The paired-end reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using BWA-MEM (v.0.7.18) with the following parameters: −5SP -T0. High-quality, valid pairs were retained using the parse module from the pairtools (v.0.3.0) implementing the following parameters: –min-mapq 40 –walks-policy 5unique –max-inter-align-gap 30. After the resulting pairsam file was sorted using pairtools sort, PCR duplicates were removed using the dedup module from pairtools with the following parameters: -mark-dups -output-stats. The pairtools split module was then used to generate a .bam and .pairs file. This process was performed individually on biological replicates and pooled replicates. Using the pairs file, two contact matrix file formats were generated: .cool and .hic. The .cool files were generated using cooler cload pairix (v.0.9.3) and KR-normalized using cooler zoomify –balance at 1 kb, 5 kb, 10 kb, 20 kb and 100 kb resolutions. The .hic files were generated using juicer tools (v.1.22.01) and KR-normalized at 10 kb and 20 kb resolutions. To ensure robust cross-condition comparisons and to remove systematic biases arising from sequencing depth, coverage heterogeneity and distance-dependence decay, we also jointly normalized replicate contact pairs for each genotype binned at 10 kb resolution using the fast loess procedure implemented in MultiHiCcompare (v.1.24.0). These fast-loess-normalized matrices were converted to .cool files. TADs were called using HiTAD (v.0.4.5) on 20 kb cool matrices and filtered to retain levels 0 and 1. Differential TAD domains were called using DiffDomain (v.1.0.0) on filtered HiTAD output. Insulation scores were obtained using the hicFindTADs tool from HiCExplorer (v.3.7.2) that were then mapped to boundaries from HiTAD TADs. Inter-TAD and intra-TAD connectivity across TAD boundaries was calculated using HiTAD output with the HiCExplorer hicInterIntraTAD function. Aggregate TAD plots were generated with the ATA function from the R package GENOVA (v.1.0.1) using 10 kb resolution loess-normalized matrices. GENOVA was also used to visualize contact matrices and differentials between WT and ARID1AHet data and to calculate relative contact probabilities. Loops were called using Mustache (v.1.0.1) on 5 kb KR-normalized .hic matrices per replicate. Pooled loops were obtained by the union of replicate loops. Loops were filtered using a P value threshold of 0.1 and a sparsity threshold of <0.6. To determine coordination of histone marks and gene expression in differential TAD domains, ChIP–seq mark summed densities were mapped to TAD domain regions, whereas gene expression was obtained from genes for which promoters overlapped with TAD domains. The coordination score was defined as previously described54. TAD boundary pile-ups were calculated using the union of TAD boundaries across the genotypes, and comparing the pile-ups to randomized shifted control regions using the shift option of coolpup.py.

The chromatin segmentation, quantification and clustering steps were completed as previously described24. In brief, a common peak set was generated from the input marks (ATAC–seq, CTCF ChIP–seq and PBRM1 ChIP–seq). Peaks were extended to a minimum size of 500 bp and a graph was generated in which each vertex is a peak and two vertices are connected only when the two peaks overlap by >50% of their size in both directions. The graph was split into connected components, and components were collapsed into regions by taking the maximal extent of the peaks in that component. Any unconnected peaks were added to this set by their original coordinates. The start and end coordinates of all the regions were concatenated and taken as a set of boundary coordinates, and the genome was segmented into disjoint regions between these boundaries to make up the candidate segmentation set. The original peaks across all the marks and replicates were overlapped with this candidate set. Any region with fewer than two overlaps were discarded. Any adjacent region with similar peak presence or absence values (as a Hamming distance of binary values less than 2) were combined into one. After the peaks were segmented into these regions, subread featureCounts98 was used to quantify the reads of every mark across all the datasets. Normalized log-FPKM scores were calculated for each file, and the replicates were collapsed in a dataset by taking the median per region, and the log2 fold change of the differential (ARID1AHet versus WT) was calculated by taking the log-FPKM difference between the conditions. The normalized score for the WT conditions and the log2 fold change scores were clustered using k-means clustering using the input feature set used during peak segmentation, and the rest were used as independent read-outs. Gene expression was associated to the regions by taking the nearest TSS to each region and taking the expression difference. CTCF motifs were annotated using the CTCF R package and the EMBL M1 and M2 motifs114,115. For Supplementary Fig. 11, the motif enrichment was calculated taking the peaks in each cluster and assigning them a dependent variable of 1, and any other peak outside of that region as 0, and running the multivariate motif enrichment as described in the ATAC–seq section. The TAD insulation scores were calculated by taking the insulation scores around 100 kb of each region from Micro-C results with a 10 kb bin size, and shifting each profile vertically so that the maximal insulation score of each peak is shifted to zero. Therefore the insulation score difference between ARID1AHet and WT data would be calculated as the delta of the trough at the centre of the region (if any) compared with the background connectivity for each condition, and the differences in the extents of the dips can be calculated between ARID1AHet and WT.
