Billionaires spent $378 million to sink Prop 40. Norway’s 134-year-old wealth tax offers a different model

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

When Norway instituted a wealth tax in 1892, the U.S. was in the throes of the Gilded Age. American industrialists like John D. Rockefeller and Andrew Carnegie rocketed to unprecedented levels of wealth, while staggering economic inequality plagued the country.

More than 130 years later, Norway still levies that tax. And once again, the U.S. is experiencing an extraordinary degree of wealth concentration—by some measures, even exceeding Gilded Age levels.

The parallel now sits at the center of a live American debate. On Nov. 3, California voters rejected a one-time tax on billionaires even as they and voters in two other states backed higher income taxes on the rich. In Congress, Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Ro Khanna are pushing an annual 5% federal tax on billionaire fortunes. In Norway, a government-appointed commission recommended this year that the country cut its rate.

The two countries are working from very different blueprints. Norway’s tax reaches the middle class at about 1%. The leading U.S. proposals would hit fewer than 1,000 people at 5%. That difference shapes how much a wealth tax would raise, who would leave, and whether it would survive in court.

Unlike the billionaire-centric levies that have dominated the U.S. debate, Norway’s wealth tax reaches a much broader slice of the population. For 2026, net wealth above NOK 1.9 million (about $197,000) is taxed at a combined local and national rate of 1%, with the threshold doubled for married couples. Above NOK 21.5 million (about $2.2 million), the rate rises to 1.1%.

Its contribution to government revenue is humbler than some advocates might expect. The tax generated about 0.6% of GDP and 1.5% of the country’s total tax revenue in 2024, according to Cristina Enache, an economist at the Tax Foundation. That modest yield is one reason economists caution against treating Norway as proof that a U.S. version would generate enormous sums.

The tax has proved durable nonetheless. Its revenue has climbed despite an exodus of millionaires, Reuters reported last year. In a poll taken before Norway’s September 2025 election, 39% of Norwegians wanted the tax kept or raised, 23% wanted it cut, and 28% wanted it abolished.

Norway, though, is rethinking the dose. In June, a government-appointed expert commission proposed cutting the rate to a single level between 0.25% and 0.75%.

Even in Norway, a wealth tax is not the main reason the country has built such a large public balance sheet. Its sovereign wealth fund, the largest in the world, is funded primarily by petroleum revenues, including taxes, licensing fees, and state ownership interests, not wealth-tax receipts. Norway’s broader prosperity also reflects strong institutions, natural-resource wealth, high labor-force participation, and good governance, which makes it a hard template for the U.S. to replicate.

Enache said Norway’s relatively low threshold means the tax can reach middle-class homeowners and savers, particularly those with substantial assets and little debt. A U.S. policy aimed exclusively at billionaires would be a fundamentally different scheme.

“If U.S. policymakers seek to raise revenue comparable to Norway’s wealth tax, they would likely need a much broader tax base reaching well beyond billionaires,” Enache said. A narrower tax, she said, would likely raise substantially less and be more vulnerable to avoidance, migration, and changes in taxpayer behavior.

Revenue estimates in the U.S. debate swing widely depending on the rate, the threshold, which assets are included, and how taxpayers respond. The Tax Foundation estimates that a 5% annual tax on net wealth above $1 billion would raise about $1.5 trillion over a decade, assuming roughly a third of the tax would be avoided and before accounting for higher compliance costs.

The Make Billionaires Pay Their Fair Share Act, which Sanders (I-Vt.) and Khanna (D-Calif.) introduced in March, would impose a similar 5% annual tax on Americans worth more than $1 billion, a group of 938 people by the sponsors’ count. They cite an estimate by economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman that it would raise $4.4 trillion over a decade, funding everything from $3,000 direct payments to households earning $150,000 or less to affordable housing, expanded health care, and childcare. Assuming more avoidance than Saez and Zucman do, the Tax Foundation trimmed that estimate to about $3.3 trillion, and the American Enterprise Institute has put the figure closer to $2.3 trillion.

Chester Spatt, a professor of finance at Carnegie Mellon University’s Tepper School of Business, is among those skeptical that such a steep tax would generate the revenue its proponents expect.

“It would indirectly represent a very heavy tax on investment income,” he said. “I suspect it won’t raise much revenue and instead lead to the development of devices that would be outside the reach of the tax.”

Part of the problem is measurement. Income taxes are generally easier to administer because they rely on annual filings (ahem, unless you’re one of the more than 125,000 high earners who stopped filing returns that the IRS flagged in 2024). A wealth tax would require the government to value assets such as private businesses, farmland, artwork, and intellectual property, many of which have no clear market price.

Norway offers a real-world test of how the rich respond. In 2022, the country raised its base wealth tax rate by 0.1 percentage point, to 0.95%, and added a 1.1% top bracket for net wealth above NOK 20 million. Enache said the increase was followed by an outflow of wealthy individuals to jurisdictions including Switzerland, which has its own wealth tax, albeit at lower rates. Data from the Norwegian think tank Civita, reported by Reuters, shows 261 residents with assets above NOK 10 million left in 2022 and 254 in 2023, more than double the typical pace.

Around 40% of those emigrants were business owners, according to research Enache cites from Princeton University’s Christine Blandhol. That research estimates the tax changes could reduce Norway’s long-run economic output by about 1.3%. Business owners who relocate may take investment and entrepreneurial activity with them, and governments can also lose income tax revenue from people who leave.

Enache said those behavioral responses can make the net revenue gains from a wealth tax much smaller than expected.

California offered a preview before a single dollar was collected. Proposition 40 would have imposed a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of the roughly 200 billionaires who lived in the state on Jan. 1. Opponents led by Google cofounder Sergey Brin poured about $378 million into defeating it.

Enache argued that the debate itself prompted wealthy residents to begin moving assets out of the state. Google cofounder Larry Page and Uber cofounder Travis Kalanick both moved out of California before the measure’s residency cutoff.

The measure failed even as other efforts to raise taxes on high earners succeeded. Washington voters kept the state’s new 9.9% tax on income above $1 million. Colorado voters replaced their flat income tax with graduated rates topping out at 8.4%, and Californians made the state’s top income tax rates permanent. The results underscore a distinction between taxing what people earn and taxing what they own.

Spatt sees California’s vote as a warning sign for broader wealth-tax efforts. Voters, he said, understood that the proposal could encourage high-wealth and high-income individuals to move their tax revenue elsewhere. Although a federal tax would make it harder to avoid the levy simply by moving to another state, he believes voters may also worry that a tax initially limited to billionaires could eventually expand.

Many taxpayers realize, he said, that “the billion-dollar threshold would likely be cut over time” and that the measure’s “‘one-time’ nature” was “likely a mirage.”

A federal wealth tax would also face an obstacle with no Norwegian equivalent: the U.S. Constitution. It doesn’t stop states from taxing wealth, but it requires “direct” federal taxes to be apportioned among the states by population, and the 16th Amendment carved out an exception only for taxes on income. Whether a federal wealth tax could clear that bar remains unsettled.

Interested parties watched the 2024 Supreme Court case Moore v. United States closely, because the taxpayers argued the law at issue taxed income they had never realized. But the court ruled on narrow grounds, expressly declining to address taxes on wealth or appreciation. Four justices, one short of a majority, signaled that they see realization as a constitutional requirement for taxing income.

Some tax lawyers read that as a warning. Victor Jaramillo, a member of the law firm Caplin & Drysdale and an adjunct professor of international tax controversies at Georgetown University Law Center, is one of them.

“The opinion does not seem to bode favorably on the chances of a wealth tax surviving constitutional scrutiny, in my opinion,” Jaramillo told Fortune.

Enache gave a more definitive assessment. “It seems likely that a U.S. wealth tax would be found unconstitutional,” she wrote. “That means it would raise $0.”

None of that has ended the argument. Some tax experts, including Peter Barnes, a former U.S. deputy international tax counsel and longtime General Electric tax lawyer, argue the U.S. should instead tax a much broader group of households at a far lower rate than the billionaire-only plans propose.

In Norway, the debate is running the other way, with a rate cut now on the table. In California, the health care workers’ union behind Prop 40 has vowed to return with another billionaire tax initiative in 2028.

For U.S. advocates, Norway’s staying power may be less of a blueprint than it looks. “Longevity should not be confused with success,” Enache told Fortune. “Norway’s wealth tax has survived largely because it is embedded in a broader political and fiscal system that has traditionally enjoyed strong public support.”
