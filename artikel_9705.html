We Built a Test to Catch Contact Forms That Lie. It Lied to Us Five Times First

My team builds a form backend, and the most common message we get is some version of "my form stopped working". Usually the form never worked. Someone generated the site, clicked Send once, saw a green "Message sent!" and moved on. In early October we got tired of guessing how often that happens and decided to count it on sites built with Lovable and Bolt.

On paper the plan was short. Open a live site, find the contact form, fill it in with test values nobody would type for real, press Send and watch what leaves the page. If a request goes out with our text in it, the form sends. If nothing goes out and the page still celebrates, the form is fake. Every request after the click was blocked in the browser, so no site owner got a test message from us.

We wrote the rules down before the first site was tested. The test went through 793 sites and found 119 contact forms. It took six runs to get to the final answer, which was 27 fakes out of the 105 forms with a clear result.

Six runs, because the test itself kept getting things wrong. These are the five times it fooled us, more or less in the order we noticed.

Version one counted any request after the click as "the form sent something". If you've ever opened the network tab on a modern landing page, you can guess what happened. Pressing Send also pings Google Analytics, Tag Manager, Clarity, Hotjar, PostHog or Vercel's insights, and Lovable sites call their own analytics endpoint too. A fake form with three trackers on the page passed as a working one.

So "sends" got a stricter meaning. A request only counts if it carries what we typed. We picked test values that stand out, an example.com address and a phone number starting with 555, which made them easy to find in a URL or a request body. Known analytics hosts were ignored on top of that.

During the pilot, one site cleared its fields after Send and that was it. No toast, no text, no request. It looked like a form that simply does nothing. It turned out the site had shown an alert(), and the browser automation had dismissed it before we could read it.

After that, whatever a dialog said was treated as part of the page.

After the full run, we went through the fakes by hand. For each one we opened the site's JavaScript and read the submit handler. Most were exactly what we expected, and the pattern became familiar quickly: wait a second, show a green toast, reset the fields. No fetch, no email service, no database.

But two forms we had confirmed as fake by reading their code were classed as "nothing happened". They showed "Message sent!" for two or three seconds and then hid it. Our test read the page once, five seconds after the click, by which point the toast was gone.

The fix was to sample the page every 250 milliseconds for five seconds and keep every new line of text we saw. In the same pass we found a form that sent people to mailto: by setting location.href. Navigations like that never show up as network requests, so we hooked them separately.

This one is my favourite. A site returned a "queued" message, but its code clearly sent requests. On a closer look, the handler had a honeypot, a hidden field that only bots fill in. If the field has a value, the form quietly shows a fake success and drops the message. Our test filled every text field it could find, including that hidden one, so the site correctly treated us as a bot.

So the test stopped filling fields that a person can't see or reach. That created the next problem.

After that change, three sites that had been sending started refusing our test values. We logged a diagnosis: the honeypot rule must be skipping ordinary fields named website or url. We narrowed the rule. Nothing changed.
