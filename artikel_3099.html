What The First 48 Hours After An AI Failure Says About Your Governance

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

​You log on to your computer for the workday and see an AI agent with access to your CRM send a message to your entire customer list. It’s not a phishing attempt. The agent is doing what it was authorized to do but with the wrong list attached and the wrong offer inserted. The clock starts ticking the moment the first customer replies.

Now ask yourself: What did the agent do, which of those actions are reversible, who can stop it mid-workflow and what has to be disclosed, to whom?

Every organization with a crisis plan knows what happens in the first 48 hours of a cybersecurity incident. There’s typically a named incident commander, a crisis team and a call tree that reaches legal and comms. But ask that same group what happens after an AI agent does something it shouldn’t have, and the next steps become more uncertain. There isn’t an industry equivalent to follow in this situation.

Agent logging wasn’t designed to reconstruct a decision chain the way security logging was, making it harder to answer, after the fact, what the agent did and why. This failure isn’t an edge case; it’s the default state most in which organizations already operate.

What I described is not a hypothetical risk sitting somewhere in the future. Grant Thornton’s 2026 AI Impact Survey found that nearly three in four organizations are giving agentic AI access to their data and processes, whether piloting, scaling or running autonomous AI. Just 20% have a tested AI incident response plan for when it fails. The gap between adoption and readiness is apparent, leaving most of the enterprise operating without a plan for the day an agent does something it wasn’t supposed to.

The same survey also revealed that 54% of COOs are concerned about regulatory and compliance uncertainty related to agentic AI, compared with just 20% of CIOs and CTOs. Those running operations and those deploying the technology are not aligned on where the risk is. When builders and operators don’t share the same risk sentiment, that readiness gap between them sits there, latent—until an incident forces the question. ​

A plan on paper isn’t the same as true readiness. Sygnia’s 2026 CISO survey found that 73% of security leaders say their organization would not be fully ready to execute their incident response plan under pressure, despite 99% having one on the books. Cybersecurity has decades of regulatory pressure behind it and still has this gap. AI incident readiness doesn’t have the same maturity to rely on, and the technology is moving faster than the governance. The gap between deploying agentic AI and being able to respond when it fails isn’t shrinking anytime soon, which further compounds the financial and reputational cost of unpreparedness.

The lack of readiness often comes down to structural misalignment. A new model deployment produces a demo. A governance framework produces a slide. An incident response plan for AI doesn’t produce value until the day you need it. That’s the trap tech leaders fall into.

Most AI governance conversations right now still focus on the front end: what agents are allowed to do, which use cases get approved and how permissions get scoped before deployment. This work is important but treats governance as a gate you build once and walk through. AI incident readiness is different since it’s based on the assumption that something will eventually get through the gate anyway and asks whether your organization can handle the fallout when it happens.

That’s a harder conversation to have, because it means admitting in advance that the AI you’re deploying will, at some point, act beyond your intention. Most leadership teams would rather debate the next use case than plan for this. But the organizations willing to are building something durable. They’re assigning decision rights before they’re needed, designing logs to be read by a stakeholder under pressure rather than an engineer debugging a model and establishing a disclosure threshold in advance.

None of this is exclusively a technology problem. The leadership decisions about who owns the moment when AI is wrong are equally relevant. And ownership isn’t a straight line. Responsibility is split across the user, the agent interface, the model provider and the system on which the agent acts. Engineers and technical teams are no longer the only ones on the hook. AI is touching more enterprise data with each deployment, which means operations, legal and marketing all have to be in the room when something goes wrong, not just IT.

Sygnia’s 2026 CISO survey also lists difficulty coordinating key stakeholders as a top challenge in executing incident response, cited by 90% of respondents. You can test that within a day. Pull five people in a room, including operations, legal and marketing, run the scenario, and time how long it takes to answer the questions from the top of this piece. If the room can’t answer within the hour, you’ve found the gap for the cost of an afternoon.
