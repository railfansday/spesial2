Dianna Russini wants us to pretend none of this ever happened

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

The cult classic Clue, the movie based on the board game, ends with three possible endings. Originally, each movie theatre that showed the film played one of the endings at random, so viewers didn't know which one they'd get.

One of those endings (the one proclaimed as the "real" ending for those of us who fell in love with the film in syndication) features Tim Curry as the butler Wadsworth revealing that he was indeed the evil Mr. Boddy all along. And that by wooing the individuals he was blackmailing to his mansion to kill their manipulators one by one, they saved him a lot of trouble.

Before Mr. Boddy leaves, Mr. Green (Michael McKean) indignantly tells him he won't get away with it. Mr. Boddy then calmly asks the stunned houseguests a simple question, "So why shouldn't we get away with it? We'll stack the bodies in the cellar, lock it, leave quietly one at a time, and forget that any of this ever happened."

Dianna Russini's big People magazine feature, the carefully crafted and manicured exclusive to soft-launch her return to public life and resume her sports media career, asks us to do the exact same thing.

Russini will re-emerge from her months-long hiatus this week after her apparent affair with New England Patriots head coach Mike Vrabel with a couple of new gigs: a weekly podcast and column with BetUS and one with longtime friend Jon "Stugotz" Weiner.

A return to NFL reporting and establishment media always seemed like a distant fantasy for Dianna Russini, given the magnitude of the Vrabel scandal. And to be quite honest, a pair of podcasts and a column with an offshore sportsbook is about as low-stakes as it gets in the grand scheme of things. Russini will be on the far fringes of an NFL media universe that she was once a key player in.

But the way that this has all unfolded has felt so fake that it's done serious damage to any hopes of achieving anything beyond that.

Russini reportedly hired infamous Hollywood publicist Melissa Nathan to help chart her comeback course. And if the goal was to produce something so lacking in any dose of reality that it is almost impossible to fathom, then mission accomplished.

The People feature presents Russini as an entirely sympathetic figure and a victim of circumstances placed upon her. It largely ignores any role she took in a very close, long-term relationship with one of her sources that was serious enough to lead to her resignation from The Athletic.

She talks about how "heartbreaking" the Vrabel saga was. That it was a "nightmare." If it sounds a lot like the purposely vague generalities that announcers use to rehabilitate players coming back from some kind of off-the-field trouble that they caused by mentioning how they are "battling through adversity," it's because that's literally the example Russini used to talk about how much support she has gotten from inside the NFL universe, urging her forward.

"I felt like players were texting and calling me to say, 'Get back to work, it's time,' " she says. "I think it's pretty poetic that here I was all these years telling the story about these players going through adversity, and then they became sort of the people that I was learning from during this."

But no comment drew as much attention as Dianna Russini choosing to go on the offensive, calling out the "lies" told throughout the story.

"The lies became so out of control," Russini claims. "This was a runaway train, and there was nothing I could do to stop it. Once we realized nothing was going to stop this, it became unhealthy for us to read it because it wasn't helping us heal. It was piling on and adding to what we were already dealing with."

A piece of journalism would provide some context for what those lies could have been. Who exactly was lying? About what? What exactly is Russini willing to admit to regarding what actually happened? Does she own any agency in this, at all?

Mike Vrabel wasn't exactly a beacon of accountability when he took to the podium in New England to talk about this, but at least he acknowledged that something happened. He even had his day of counseling on Day 3 of the NFL Draft, at least to give the impression that he was taking something seriously.

But it seems that any acknowledgment that wouldn't sweep this under the rug and help Dianna Russini's career has been forgotten.

Instead of taking any responsibility at all, Russini sounds as defiant as she did in her original resignation letter at The Athletic. It seems as if she is still living in denial that millions of people are aware of what appeared to be a long-term relationship with Mike Vrabel and why she's now working for an offshore sportsbook instead of the sports platform of The New York Times.

"One of the hard parts about this was that a lot of the work I was doing and the things that I've accomplished got lost," Russini says. "It quickly went from just one moment that people felt was true, and erased years of work."

It would be one thing if Russini talked about a fresh start or a clean slate. Instead, she sounds like she is trying to replay the Day 1 strategy: dismissing the Arizona resort photos as a "completely innocent interaction" and expecting the public to accept it without additional scrutiny.

People want to be forgiving. They want to open doors for second chances. They don't want to be taken for fools.

People isn't going to win any Pulitzers anytime soon, but the Dianna Russini feature is such a preposterous public relations fiasco that it deserves to be enshrined at Four Seasons Total Landscaping.

A lot of things can be true at the same time. Is it unfair that Russini's career was derailed while Vrabel's career with the Patriots was unperturbed? Of course. Was Russini the victim of actual abuse on social media and intrusions into her life and her family's lives that nobody would wish for anyone? Absolutely. Has Russini been unfairly singled out and scrutinized as a woman in sports media when the transactional nature of insiderdom features countless webs of complicated relationships? Yes.

But none of that excuses a complete lack of transparency and honesty that has accompanied this public rollout.

When her podcast debuts this week, Russini has one last chance to be forthcoming with her audience about what really happened this year before she begins this next chapter. Nobody is asking for her to go on Call Her Daddy and reveal all the sordid details at this point. But a simple statement of actual accountability, responsibility, and honesty will go much farther in repairing her public image than any highly manufactured People interview. If not for anyone else, for herself. Failing to do so will turn The Russini Report into nothing more than a village of trolls laughing at her expense.

Sure, there will be a collection of people who won't care about any of that and will tune in for the entertainment value. It's clear she has a lot of people in the industry and the football world still on her side. But her comeback could have been so much more if she had allowed herself to take some semblance of personal responsibility for the predicament she has found herself in. Instead, we're all supposed to pretend that none of this ever happened.
