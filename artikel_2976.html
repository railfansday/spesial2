How These 5 Aerospace Stocks Earn Money Beyond New Plane Sales

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Aftermarket aerospace companies offer recurring, long-term revenue streams, and they deserve to carry valuation premiums.

The aerospace market can be broadly split into two groups: original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and aftermarket companies. While the OEM companies are the most highly visible, the latter are often the most profitable and relatively more stable due to a stream of recurring revenue that can last decades in some cases. Here's a look at five companies with heavy aftermarket exposure, namely GE Aerospace (GE +1.43%), TransDigm Group (TDG +1.59%), Loar Holdings (LOAR -0.47%), AAR Corp (AIR -1.25%), and RTX (RTX +0.51%).

GE Aerospace's joint venture with Safran, CFM International, is the dominant player in the aircraft engine market, with its LEAP engine used on both the Boeing 737 MAX (the sole option) and on the Airbus A320neo family (RTX's Pratt & Whitney also provides an engine). These two narrow-body airplanes are the workhorses of the skies. GE Aerospace also has the GEnx engine on the Boeing 787 Dreamliner and the GE9X engine on the upcoming Boeing 777X.

It's an extremely powerful position, and as Boeing and Airbus aggressively ramp production to try to deliver on their multi-year backlogs, GE/CFM will grow their installed base of engines. That's great news for long-term profitability, because although new engines are usually sold at a loss, they can generate more than 40 years of lucrative aftermarket revenue as engines come in for "shop visits" and are overhauled.

As such, investors should focus on the long term with GE Aerospace. As CEO Larry Culp noted on the last earnings call, "With the LEAP installed base expected to more than double between 2025 and 2030, our team focused on increasing capacity for the LEAP fan assembly." That increase speaks to a long road of lucrative income ahead.

One of the characteristic features of aerospace parts is that they are strictly regulated and controlled by aviation bodies like the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA). That gives companies like highly engineered aerospace components manufacturer TransDigm an opportunity to hold significant pricing power in the aftermarket.

About 55% of its revenue comes from the aftermarket (commercial aerospace and defense). The company's acquisition-led strategy is to buy manufacturers of relatively small and niche proprietary parts (TransDigm has bought over 100 businesses since 1993) and then incorporate them into its overall operations.

It's a strategy that has resulted in startling profit margins for an industrial company and creates the cash flow to reinvest in acquisitions.

The TransDigm model is so successful that Loar is following a similar strategy. In fact, CEO Dirkson Charles and CFO Glenn D'Alessandro were both senior executives at McKechnie Aerospace, a company acquired by TransDigm in 2010.

Loar's playbook reads like a copy of TransDigm's, with a focus on highly engineered niche components in aerospace and defense, where management believes it holds market-leading positions. If it's half as successful as TransDigm, it would be a highly attractive option for investors.

Aftermarket services company AAR Corp provides parts distribution, maintenance, repair, and overhaul (MRO) services, as well as maintenance software, to a wide range of commercial airlines, defense customers, and OEM customers (including Airbus, GE Aerospace, and RTX).

It's a direct beneficiary of the pickup in air travel since the end of the pandemic, and of the fact that older airplanes continue to be flown more as Boeing and Airbus are still ramping up production. Moreover, its acquisition of a majority stake in MRO Holdings will expand its geographic reach and service offerings to its existing customer base.

I've previously discussed the improving prospects for RTX's defense business, Raytheon. Now it's time to focus on its commercial aerospace aftermarket business (RTX also has an extensive OEM business), It runs across Collins Aerospace, and more importantly, Pratt & Whitney aircraft engines, where in common with GE Aerospace's CFM International joint venture, it has a long tail of aftermarket revenue from its installed base of engines in the Airbus A320 neo family of aircraft.

All told, these five companies offer investors an excellent way to profit from an investing theme likely to stay relevant for many decades to come.
