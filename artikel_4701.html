Pursuit of Excellence Elevates Everything for Everyone

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

All the privileges we enjoy, whatever they may be, have never been, and never will be, shared equally. However, what is understood and shared equally are universal truths, such as the laws of physics, gravity, and even the constructs of meritocracy and excellence.

For example, to tune an instrument requires the application of excellence. Tuning an instrument can be achieved with the hearing of an individual and now also through the use of technology. Research into music training demonstrates that musical precision develops through structured learning, refinement, repetition, and neuroplastic adaptation (Merrett et al., 2013; Neves et al., 2022).

Irrespective of whether tuning is done by an individual or through technology, the purpose and outcome are the same: to ensure that the instrument is pitch-perfect. When this is achieved, it means that the pursuit and application of excellence have met the required standard of vibrational sound perfection.

Research on expertise consistently demonstrates that, irrespective of discipline or circumstance, high-level performance can be achieved only through the personal application of deliberate practice, determination, continual refinement, perseverance, feedback, resilience, and ongoing error correction until the required standard of excellence is attained. Once achieved, the same process must then be applied unrelentingly to maintain excellence (Ericsson, 2004, 2008).

The media has recently reported that the pursuit and application of excellence, in the area of symphonic orchestration, may be considered secondary and/or even unnecessary. My immediate response was, and remains: How is this even possible?

Symphony orchestration has been considered one of humanity's most highly regarded sophisticated presentations of coordinated musical excellence, often referred to as high art.

Neuroscience research has also shown that musical training produces measurable structural and functional neurological changes in the brain, which also helps to enhance auditory processing and support higher-order executive cognitive functions.

All of this also supports fine and gross-motor biomechanical and neuromuscular skill acquisition and motor learning development processes (Merrett et al., 2013; Neves et al., 2022; Schlaug et al., 2005; Zaatar et al., 2024).

If excellence is required to tune a single instrument, it becomes even more important when dozens or even hundreds of musicians must perform together. Such performance is not achieved by accident. Rather, it emerges from disciplined preparation, deliberate practice, feedback, determination, high-level focus, and the pursuit of increasingly complex and refined standards of intellectual and skill-based performance excellence (Ericsson, 2004, 2008).

The question, therefore, is not whether merit and excellence matter. The question is how any musical institute that depends (in absolutely immutable terms) on both individual and collective merit-based competence (to achieve and apply biomechanical and musical coordination, precision, and musical perfection) could consider removing the universality of excellence and meritocracy from its very purpose of existence (Ericsson, 1999, 2004, 2008).

Whether in music, science, engineering, medicine, education, sport, or any other field of human endeavor, excellence and merit remains a universal principle that helps individuals and societies improve outcomes, elevate standards, and drive progress that ultimately benefits everyone (Ericsson, 1999, 2004, 2008).

The pursuit of excellence and meritocracy is therefore not merely desirable; it is the mechanism through which successful outcomes become possible. Excellence is neither accidental nor arbitrary. Rather, it emerges from the pursuit and application of meritocracy, applied achievements, competence, unrelenting effort, continual refinement, and the disciplined pursuit of superior reproducible performance (Ericsson, 1999, 2004, 2008).

Excellence and meritocracy, therefore, is not an optional opinion. It is the universal principle through which advancement occurs and through which civilization continues to progress. The pursuit of excellence elevates everything for everyone.
