Everyone wants their own Starlink. It's not that easy

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A slowdown over Ukraine sent America’s allies shopping for satellites. The rockets are the hard part

Ukrainian soldiers set up a Starlink satellite communication terminal at a combat position in the Donetsk region of Ukraine (Photo by Dmytro Smolienko/Ukrinform) (Photo credit should read Dmytro Smolienko/ Ukrinform/Future Publishing via Getty Images)

For more than a week in October, Ukraine's fastest drones lost touch with the people flying them. The Starlink terminals they depended on stopped working above roughly 100 miles per hour, which grounded the interceptors Kyiv used to chase down Russian attack drones.

After public outcry, SpaceX chief Elon Musk said limits on the military service were set by the Pentagon and ultimately the president, and later he said Ukraine had broken Starlink's terms by using it for offensive strikes. Kyiv denied that, and President Donald Trump said he had asked Musk to lift the restrictions.

Normal speeds returned on Oct. 24, but there wasn't much time to rebuild trust. Earlier this month, Ukraine said new restrictions were interfering with its drone interceptions again, although this time SpaceX said there had been no change.

Poland, which pays for many of Ukraine's Starlink subscriptions, joined the public criticism in October. Its foreign minister, Radoslaw Sikorski, asked Musk whether he was playing politics with his products again.

The E.U.'s defense and space commissioner, Andrius Kubilius, warned that SpaceX risked losing the confidence of the governments paying for Ukraine's service. In Britain, the telecom giant BT put off the launch of a Starlink-based broadband product.

Europe, which had long been worried about a situation like this, had spent years on its own replacement for Starlink. IRIS², the E.U.'s 348-satellite network, moved into its build phase in August. Germany was taking a more military-focused approach, and announced it would spend €35 billion on military space by 2030, including a communications constellation.

France took a shortcut. A network called OneWeb with similar service to Starlink belonged to Eutelsat, a French satellite operator. The French state became Eutelsat’s largest shareholder, with Britain keeping a smaller stake. That bought Europe a working alternative, though a small one. OneWeb had roughly 650 satellites in orbit to Starlink’s 11,100.

Asian countries were pursuing national networks of their own. Japan committed $922 million to a satellite venture led by Rakuten. South Korea approved a domestic low-orbit network for 2035. Meanwhile, Taiwan pitched allies on a shared constellation.

Canada had further to go. All 27 satellites it launched since September 2025 rode SpaceX's Falcon 9, and Ottawa only this year handed its first grants to three homegrown rocket startups.

For once, the rocket science was the easy part. Europe, Japan, and India already built their own rockets and satellites. The hard part was launching often enough to be a real competitor to SpaceX.

Europe's Ariane 6 was held to six flights in 2026 after a turbopump fault, and Brussels projected that its government missions alone would need 14 a year by 2031. Falcon 9 flew 165 times in 2025.

The money was just as lopsided. SpaceX’s Starlink-led connectivity business took in $5.2 billion in revenue in the third quarter alone. At that pace it earned the entire IRIS² budget in about 10 months.

The scramble has a precedent. Governments across Europe, Asia and the Middle East had already committed billions to sovereign AI, building national data centers so they would not depend on Silicon Valley. Space is a chapter in the story.

In both, independence still ran through American suppliers. Sovereign data centers were filled with Nvidia $NVDA chips, and Europe had sent its own navigation satellites up on Falcon 9 while Ariane 6 ran late.

In both, renting was cheaper. Amazon $AMZN and Microsoft $MSFT had pitched governments on sovereign cloud services that cost less than building from scratch but left an American company in control. Starlink was the same bargain.

The comparison broke down in two places. No ally could make a chip to rival Nvidia’s, but several could build a rocket and a satellite. The second break was less comforting. Nvidia wanted every government as a customer, while SpaceX closed its Falcon 9 bookings for flights after late 2028 as it turned toward Starship and AI. Allies that wanted satellites in orbit after that would need their own rockets.

Prying allied technology loose from America, from chips to rockets, was going to take years, if it happened at all. The E.U.’s stopgap, a pooled government satellite service, was due to open to Ukraine in 2027, and IRIS² would not start launching until 2029. Kyiv could not wait that long. It was already working to renew its Starlink contract, which was set to expire in mid-2027.
