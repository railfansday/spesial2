Freelancer Quarterly Taxes: A Simple Calendar So You Never Miss a Payment

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Nobody warns you about the quietest freelance bill. There is no employer withholding tax from your pay, no payroll department smoothing it out, and no reminder in the mail until it is too late. Then April arrives, and a whole year's tax lands on your desk at once — plus, in many places, a penalty for not paying along the way.

Quarterly estimated taxes fix that. Instead of one painful bill, you pay four smaller ones through the year, on dates you can put on a calendar today. This article is that calendar: the four dates, the amount to set aside, and a routine that takes twenty minutes a quarter.

When you work for an employer, tax is taken out of every paycheck before you see it. When you freelance, nobody takes anything out — the full payment lands in your account, and the tax part is still yours to pay later. Tax authorities know this, so they ask self-employed people to pay in instalments through the year instead of all at once.

Skip the instalments and two things happen. First, the year-end bill is much bigger than it needs to be, because a whole year of tax arrives in one month. Second, many tax systems charge interest or a penalty for paying late — you end up paying more than the tax itself. Quarterly payments are not extra tax. They are the same tax, split into four manageable pieces, paid on time.

In the United States, federal estimated tax payments for the self-employed fall on four dates each year: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year. (Other countries have their own schedules — the UK's payments on account, for example, fall in January and July — so check your local rules and write your own four dates down.)

The dates are easy to miss because they are not the 1st of the month and they are not tied to anything you already do. So do this now: open your calendar, add a reminder one week before each date, and a second reminder on the date itself. In the sample, a freelancer who added all four dates in January never missed one all year; the year before, without the reminders, she missed two and paid a penalty on both.

The hardest part is not the date — it is knowing how much to pay. A simple starting point many freelancers use is to set aside 25% of every payment they receive, in a separate account, the day the money arrives. Not at the end of the month. The day it arrives.

The math is simple. In the sample, a freelancer earns $18,000 in a quarter. 25% of that is $4,500 — that is the quarterly payment, already sitting in the tax account, waiting for the date. If the real tax turns out to be less, the leftover is a bonus. If it turns out to be more, the gap is small and manageable, not a crisis. The exact percentage depends on your income, your deductions, and where you live — 25% is a starting point, not a rule — but any fixed percentage beats saving "whatever is left over," because what is left over is usually nothing.

Willpower is a bad tax strategy. If the tax money sits in the same account as your spending money, it will get spent — not because you are careless, but because it looks like money you have. The fix is boring and it works: open a second account, call it Tax, and move the set-aside there the moment a client pays you.

In the sample, the freelancer's rule is one transfer per payment, done in the same five minutes as sending the thank-you email. By the end of the first quarter, the tax account held $4,500 and the spending account held only what was actually hers to spend. She stopped doing mental math about what she could afford, because the tax money was simply not there to spend. The separate account is the whole system — everything else is a calendar reminder.

The quarterly payment is only as good as the numbers behind it. If you guess your income, you guess your tax — and guessing wrong in either direction costs you. Once a quarter, before the payment date, sit down for twenty minutes and do three things: add up what you actually earned, add up your deductible expenses, and check whether your set-aside still matches reality.

Deductible expenses matter here, because they lower the income the tax is calculated on — software, a home office share, phone and internet, equipment, courses. In the sample, the freelancer's quarterly review found $2,400 of expenses she had forgotten to log; recording them before the payment date meant her quarterly payment was based on real numbers, not a guess. An expense tracker that you update as you go makes the quarterly review a twenty-minute job instead of a weekend of digging through bank statements.

A slow quarter, a late-paying client, a surprise expense — something will go wrong in at least one quarter a year. The calendar survives it if you have a rule for it in advance.

If income drops, pay what you can on the date and note the shortfall; most systems let you catch up at the next date, and paying something on time is always better than paying nothing. If a client pays late and the money arrives after the date, pay the moment it lands. If you overpaid in an earlier quarter, the extra counts toward the next one — it is not lost. The goal is not a perfect quarter. The goal is four payments a year, roughly right, roughly on time, every year. In the sample, the freelancer missed one payment by three weeks when a client paid late; she paid the day the money arrived, the penalty was a few dollars, and the system kept working.

Four dates, one percentage, one separate account. That is the whole system — and it is the difference between tax season being a routine and tax season being a crisis.
