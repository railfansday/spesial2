If an AI agent pays for the wrong thing, there are few ways to get a refund

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Shoppers have started to approve each charge on an AI agent's own card. Every sign-off leaves a record that may be their best proof in a refund fight

Minh Connors / Bloomberg via Getty Images

J.P. Morgan Private Bank, the part of JPMorgan $JPM Chase that serves wealthy clients, told its clients on Nov. 11 not to give their login details to an AI agent. The warning came from a bank that doesn't currently let any outside agent into a client's account at all. Two days later, OpenAI began rolling out ChatGPT Wallet, which lets its AI agent pay without a shopper's login. The card network issues a one-time substitute number for each purchase and charges the shopper's real card behind it. Shoppers approve each purchase with Face ID or a fingerprint, and the agent never sees the real card number.

Shoppers have already shown they will let an agent spend their money. Within 40 days of Meta $META launching its Muse agent in early September, 10 million people in the U.S. had downloaded it. They used it for shopping more than for anything else, and their purchases averaged more than $200. Muse pays through Link, Stripe's wallet for agents, and the shopper okays each total in the Muse chat window before any money moves. Stripe says the card or bank account behind the purchase remains hidden from Muse.

ChatGPT Wallet's one-time numbers and Stripe's Link give shoppers a way to let an agent spend without handing over the bank login that banks are telling them to keep to themselves. Keeping the login safe doesn't settle everything, though. A store still decides for itself whether to let agents shop. And when an agent gets an order wrong, neither Visa $V nor Mastercard $MA has a dispute code written for an agent's mistake, which makes the shopper's record of each approval the strongest proof they have in a refund fight.

Every order agents put together sits on a screen in ChatGPT Wallet until it's approved. People can check what the agent picked, which store is selling it, what it all costs, which card will be charged, and any shipping fees or repeat billing. They can also turn the whole order down if anything looks wrong. When the wallet does pay, it uses a stand-in number, so the store never gets the real card either. For now, OpenAI limits the wallet to U.S. adults with ChatGPT Pro and an eligible American Express $AXP, Chase, Mastercard, or Visa credit card. Debit cards won't work.

Other companies let shoppers cap what an agent spends. Robinhood allows customers with its Gold Card give an outside AI agent its own virtual card, then either approve each purchase or set a monthly spending limit, according to Finovate, a fintech news site. OnePay, which announced a product called Agentic Banking in October, has eligible customers put money into a separate account for their agents, with a cap on each purchase and on each month. The agent never touches the customer's main account.

Those controls match what many shoppers say they want before an agent spends for them. A third of Americans surveyed by Global Payments, a payment technology company, want to approve every purchase before the money goes out. OpenAI's wallet makes that step mandatory, and Robinhood offers it as a choice. "Making purchases with a spending limit, in an approved category, and with a requirement to confirm the purchase are very different from giving an agent open-ended authority," Global Payments Chief Product Officer Cindy Turner explained.

A bank login hands an agent open-ended authority over the whole account, with no spending limit and no step to confirm a purchase. Madison Mills, a senior AI reporter at Axios, told CBS News that in many cases, customers had shared their bank logins with agents the bank hadn't verified. Agents holding those logins don't always ask first. In one case Axios found, an agent phoned a bank to lodge a complaint and never asked the account holder beforehand.

Customers could also end up paying for an agent's mistakes when they share a login. Regulation E, the federal rule that limits what a bank customer owes for a debit card charge or transfer they never approved, makes an exception for someone who hands over an "access device," the legal term for a card or credentials. The Consumer Bankers Association, the trade group for retail banks, warns that a login shared with an agent may count. If the agent then spends beyond what the customer allowed, the customer, not the bank, could be left covering the whole loss. An agent paying with a card number made just for it never holds the login, so that exception never comes into play.

Stores can still turn an agent away, however it pays. Within two weeks of Muse's launch, Amazon $AMZN began stopping its purchases with a popup that warned that "continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use." Amazon didn't block Muse over how it paid. The company said Muse doesn't tell stores it's an agent, keeps copies of customers' Amazon logins, and pulls data out of their accounts. A stand-in card number fixes none of those problems.

The RealReal, a luxury resale site, now makes it much harder for any AI agent, not only Muse, to sign in, fill a cart, or pay, according to Luke Friang, the company's chief technology officer. The company tightened its defenses after The Verge tested whether someone could use an agent to hold items indefinitely so that no one else could buy them. On The RealReal, an item goes on hold for 20 minutes once it lands in a cart, and nobody else can buy it during that time. The Verge's reporters told Muse to put the same listings back in its cart when the timer ran out, and for hours, no other shopper could buy those designer pieces. Friang said agents can still find listings and link to the product pages, but people have to buy the items themselves.

Walmart $WMT plans to let agents in, but only on its own terms. Under the rules Walmart announced on Oct. 21, an agent shops inside the customer's Walmart account and never sees the customer's card details, and it has no power to put a new way to pay on the account or swap out an existing one. With Walmart's planned Agent Profiles, the agent asks for approval on every purchase by default, and the customer can instead let it buy on its own up to a limit the customer sets. Walmart says it will block any agent that breaks its terms.

When an AI agent buys the wrong thing, whether people get their money back depends mostly on how the agent paid, according to Security Boulevard, a cybersecurity news site. A credit card gives them the most protection. Under Regulation Z, the federal rule for credit cards, cardholders can dispute a charge for goods that didn't arrive as agreed, as long as they write to the card issuer within 60 days of the first statement that shows it. When the issuer credits the account, the store usually ends up taking the loss. Visa and Mastercard sort these disputes into their existing "not as described" categories, and neither network's published list has a category for an agent's mistake. A debit card or bank transfer protects people far less, because a login handed to an agent can count as permission to spend under Regulation E. For the newest ways agents pay, the Consumer Bankers Association, the banks' trade group, says it's "at best, unclear" whether the old protections apply at all.

With the rules that unclear, a fight over an agent's purchase often comes down to what people can prove they told it to buy. Each ChatGPT Wallet approval leaves a record of exactly that. Visa CEO Ryan McInerney told investors on the company's Oct. 27 earnings call that Visa's payment product for agents records each customer's instructions and ties them to a biometric check, such as a fingerprint, so the agent buys only what it was asked to, and that Visa's usual dispute rules apply if something goes wrong. Security Boulevard says records like these can decide disputes in either direction. Some customers may change their minds about a purchase and tell their bank, "My AI bought that, not me," a false claim the payments company Worldpay already counts as fraud. If the store can show a confirmed approval for that purchase, it wins. The record protects customers, too, when they need to show their bank exactly what they told the agent to buy.

OpenAI plans to stop asking people to approve every purchase. Over time, the company says, people will be able to give an agent standing rules, such as a spending limit, a type of purchase, or a time period, and approve its buying ahead of time with a biometric check that records the rules they set.

People would no longer have to scan a fingerprint at every checkout. The record would change, too. Instead of listing every item people picked, it would hold only the rules they set, such as a $300 limit. If the agent then spent $900, the fight would turn on whether the customers could prove the limit they gave it and whether the store could show what the agent was allowed to spend. Without that record, it's their word against a log they can't see.
