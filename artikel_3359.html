8 Perfect Sci-Fi Books With No Bad Characters

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Great character writing is not strictly essential to all speculative fiction, but there's a good reason why it's the backbone of most of history's greatest science fiction books. Though worldbuilding, pacing, stakes, and themes are just as crucial for a piece of science fiction to be truly legendary—and, at best, downright perfect—great characters are also important, which is probably why several of the best sci-fi books ever are also among the sci-fi books with the most interesting characters.

There are many things that can make a sci-fi book character great, but the key element is that readers are able to deeply connect with them regardless of whether they're an alien, a cyborg, or a space-faring human. Throughout the years, eight particularly noteworthy sci-fi books that happen to not have a single bad character, on top of being absolutely faultless themselves.

The mere high-concept pitch for Tamsyn Muir's Gideon the Ninth is so deliciously absurd that it makes it instantly clear why this is such an irresistibly fun modern classic: lesbian necromancers exploring a haunted palace in space for just under 450 pages. It's an effortlessly genre-blending gem with elements of space horror, Gothic suspense, and dark fantasy, making it one of the most purely entertaining and original science fantasy novels the world has seen in years.

Another big part of why Gideon the Ninth is one of the best science fantasy books since 1990 is the fact that the whole ensemble of characters is every bit as memorable as the plot. Gideon herself narrates the book with an irreverent voice that feels like a breath of fresh air for this genre. Muir also makes sure to constantly pair characters that may seem disparate on the surface, but this only results in a constant stream of fun moments that make every character come alive perfectly.

Canadian writers' contributions to science fiction sometimes go underappreciated, which should absolutely not happen when praising the brilliance of Emily St. John Mandel's Station Eleven. Before it was turned into one of the most genuinely perfect miniseries of the last couple of decades, it was one of the most perfect post-apocalyptic novels of the 21st century, breaking away from the genre's usual tropes by celebrating the beauty of life and art rather than dwelling on the grimness of its dystopian premise.

Where a lot of post-apocalyptic fiction tends to flatten its characters into cheap stereotypes, Mandel is careful to never do that. Instead, Station Eleven is built on a large ensemble of characters who, though flawed, feel beautifully human and fully realized. No matter how small their role, every character is given a chance to show off their complexity and moral nuances, including a primary antagonist with so many deep layers that he never feels like any kind of villainous caricature.

When it comes to speculative fiction authors, few have ever been as groundbreaking or influential as Ursula K. Le Guin; and when it comes to the sci-fi works of such legends, few have ever been as revolutionary as The Dispossessed. The winner of all three of the Hugo, Nebula, and Locus Awards for Best Novel, this game-changing masterpiece strikes a faultless balance between utopian and dystopian sci-fi, exploring how no sociopolitical system is ever truly finished or beyond any need for change.

It's one of the best soft sci-fi books of all time, written with the utmost intelligence throughout—that intelligence extending to the phenomenal character writing. It would have been abundantly easy for any lesser writer to allow their characters to become mouthpieces for political or philosophical ideologies, but Le Guin never does that. Instead, her characters are vulnerable, psychologically complex, and deeply human. Shevek is one of Le Guin's most enrapturing protagonists, and the rest of the ensemble is just as important in elevating the book's thought-provoking themes.

The 20th century saw the publication of many a great book set on Mars, but remarkably few of them were better than Kim Stanley Robinson's Red Mars. This landmark in modern hard sci-fi writing is a must-read for anyone who loves the genre, setting a new standard for realistic Mars colonization narratives and sociologically complex world-building. It won the Nebula Award and launched the equally successful Mars trilogy; but as great as its sequels are, it's Red Mars that stands out as the best.

On top of the nearly unmatched scientific rigor, the jaw-dropping sense of scale, and the central philosophical question of whether humanity should prioritize terraforming Mars for humanity's benefit or preserving its natural life, you get some of the most human and believable character writing that hard sci-fi literature has ever seen. Every character seems to embody a different attitude toward the core ethical question that fuels the narrative's sense of momentum, but those attitudes feel deeply ingrained into their arcs rather than making them feel like cardboard-cutout archetypes.

Leviathan Wakes and the Expanse series that it sparked were born as a pitch for a massively multiplayer online role-playing game, which then evolved into a tabletop RPG campaign. The journey culminated when Daniel Abraham and Ty Franck, writing under the pen name of James S. A. Corey, finally put pen to paper and turned the concept into Leviathan Wakes, which ended up being one of the greatest space opera books of all time. Grounded, tonally nuanced, and full of geopolitical complexity, it's a perfect masterpiece through and through.

It should come as no surprise that a book which was born out of an RPG campaign has characters with very distinct skills and personalities, clear motivations, and ensemble chemistry that feels remarkably organic. The rest of the Expanse series also has excellent character writing, but Leviathan Wakes perhaps deserves the most credit for being the one to introduce this incredibly memorable cast. As perfect narrative foils for each other, Jim and Joe are the best dual protagonists that a hard sci-fi space opera could possibly ask for.

There are many who would still praise Frank Herbert's Dune as the single greatest and most influential sci-fi novel of the 20th century. Regardless of superlatives, there's practically no denying that as far as space opera books go, it doesn't get much more perfect than this. Thematically layered and narratively momentous, this cautionary tale against the dangers of charismatic leaders and religious fanaticism has only kept getting better with age.

It's one of the most essential sci-fi books to read before kicking the bucket, and that's in no small measure thanks to how timeless and memorable the character writing is. Paul is such a complex and layered protagonist that many people back in the '60s (and today) misunderstood him and saw him as a tragic hero rather than a cautionary figure. Then you get villains like Baron Harkonnen and Feyd-Rautha, and secondary characters like Chani and the Lady Jessica, all amounting to one of the most iconic ensemble casts in the history of sci-fi literature.

If Dan Simmons' Hyperion is one of the most amazing sci-fi books ever written—and it is— it's in no small measure thanks to the character writing. However, by no means is that the only strength that this masterpiece has going for it. Borrowing the structure of Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales, Simmons gave birth to not only the origin of the incredible Hyperion Cantos series, but also to one of the most ambitious and masterfully genre-blending sci-fi novels ever written.

Indeed, Hyperion is an absolute masterclass in virtually every way that matters in sci-fi writing, which inevitably brings the conversation back to its many fascinating characters. Because each pilgrim in this story gets roughly a novella-length backstory dedicated to their psychology and motivations, the characters become the book's entire plot. No one here is a clear-cut hero, which generates friction between characters that likewise results in the book's most engrossing and unforgettable moments of narrative momentum.

Who, if not Ursula K. Le Guin, could possibly be said to be responsible for not only one, but two of the sci-fi books with the most perfect character writing in the genre's history? When it comes to asking what the most perfect sci-fi novel Le Guin ever wrote could be, it's not actually much of a debate at all: Fans tend to agree that it has to be The Left Hand of Darkness. One of the best sci-fi books written since Dune, The Left Hand of Darkness won the Hugo and Nebula Awards thanks to how it completely revolutionized how sci-fi authors wrote first contact stories.

The novel mainly relies on two of the greatest and most fascinating characters that sci-fi literature has ever produced: Genly, a well-meaning but subtly prejudiced Starfleet captain whose journey of learning how to be humble and open-minded is the emotional heart of The Left Hand of Darkness; and Estraven, a disgraced prime minister whose dynamic with Genly is so enrapturing that it's reason enough for any sci-fi fan to immediately pick up this absolutely perfect novel. Add to that an ensemble of great side characters as the cherry on top, and you get one of the best sci-fi books in history.
