3 Perfect Fantasy Books To Start and Finish This Weekend (November 14-15)

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Reading is fundamental, and no genre is more beloved in literature than fantasy. Indeed, nearly everyone loves a great fantasy tale, no matter the approach the author takes. Heroic knights going on quests to save the realm, magic coming into our world to wreak havoc, dark lands where danger lurks in every corner — you name it, and fantasy has it.

However, taking on a new book is a massive commitment, especially because many fantasy sagas go well past the 500-page threshold to properly tell their story. If you're short on time and want a story that you can start and finish in a weekend, then look no further than the gems on this list. These fantasy books are ideal for a weekend read, delivering complete and riveting stories that you can pick up on a Saturday afternoon and put down before Sunday ends.

Few books in fantasy history are as foundational, groundbreaking, or influential as J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, or There and Back Again. The story is well-known by now: homely hobbit Bilbo Baggins joins the wizard Gandalf and the dwarves from Thorin's Company to venture out of his comfortable shire and journey across Middle-earth on a quest to retake the dwarves' land and treasure from the dragon Smaug.

Today, The Hobbit might be perhaps eclipsed by the wild and universal acclaim of its sequel, The Lord of the Rings. Still, this tale is dripping with cozy charm and adventure that makes it a spectacular piece of fiction, one that everyone should read before kicking the bucket. Tolkien's worldbuilding is fascinating but accessible, and Bilbo is one of literature's most spirited and engaging heroes. Put him in a classic hero's journey about maturity and the dangers of greed, and you have a delightful work of fantasy that has rightfully stood the test of time as one of literature's greatest treasures. Even better, The Hobbit is usually about 300 pages long, making it ideal for a weekend read.

Now for a novel that is the literary equivalent of short and sweet. Susanna Clarke's Piranesi tells the story of the titular character, who lives in a massive structure known only as the House. Every day, Piranesi documents his existence in the seemingly infinite structure, occupied by only two other living people. Piranesi meets twice a week with The Other, who is investigating a "Great and Secret Knowledge" hidden somewhere within the House.

The beauty of Piranesi lies in its creativity and emotional intelligence. Presented as the character's daily journal entries, the book is tremendously intimate and immersive, with Clarke taking us fully into Piranesi's head and helping us understand the reality of his situation alongside him. There is a beautiful, surreal quality to the book that is elusive but never off-putting, with the House becoming one of the most singular and changing settings in modern literature. One of the most amazing books of the 2020s, Piranesi is a true jewel, a study on the meaning of reality, what and how we perceive it, and what we ultimately choose to forget. And at just 272 pages, it's a breeze to read through.

Finally, we end with one of the newest classics from fantasy maestro Brandon Sanderson. A part of the Cosmere, the book is set on the planet Lumar, which has twelve moons, each one raining different spores that create seas of unique colors. Because the spores become explosive upon contact with water, sailing is difficult. The protagonist, Tress, lives on a lonely rock in the Emerald Sea but must venture into the Midnight Sea to rescue her friend from a Sorceress.

Leave it to Sanderson to deliver an enchanting, standalone tale out of nowhere. Tress of the Emerald Sea is The Princess Bride with a twist, a clever, delightful subversion of the damsel-in-distress trope that never feels forced or contrived. Tress is a classic fantasy protagonist with such a refreshing twist that she becomes wholly unique: she's selfless, introspective, and resourceful, making her feel so real, like a friend whose adventure you're relating to. Similarly, the story follows a traditional fairy tale structure but injects so much wit and anachronisms that one can't help to sail along with it. Yes, it's 472 pages long, but you'll barely notice them — that's just how great this book is.
