Developing Rasheer Fleming requires more than hoping he develops

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Every organization has its challenges, shortcomings, and opportunities. Whether it's roster management, cap sheet navigation, internal development, or simply figuring out how to get out of its own way, you can generally identify something an organization could be doing better. As I look at the Phoenix Suns this season, my hope is that the development of young talent doesn't become one of those organizational shortcomings.

When you examine some of the challenges that have manifested themselves on the basketball court, size and rebounding are the obvious areas in which this team needs to improve. In theory, however, the organization has positioned itself to address those deficiencies. They have young players with the physical attributes to make an impact in those areas, yet that impact hasn't materialized to the degree we'd hoped, and the opportunities afforded to some of them have been few and far between.

I'm referencing second-year players Khaman Maluach and Rasheer Fleming. While Maluach has received opportunities to start this season, we've seen very little of Fleming, who appears to have already been leapfrogged on the depth chart by rookie Koa Peat. Peat has played 36 minutes this season. Fleming? He's at 35.

That tells me one of two things. Either Peat is clearly the better option, or Fleming simply isn't ready. And if Fleming isn't ready, I find myself wondering why.

We saw flashes of Fleming's potential toward the end of last season. He demonstrated the ability to knock down the three-pointer and played impressive defense when called upon. There were enough encouraging moments to generate some genuine enthusiasm about what he could become following an offseason of development. In Las Vegas this past summer, he went for 12.0 points in 26.7 minutes on 47/42/50 splits, grabbing 6.5 rebounds along the way.

I understand the overarching argument surrounding the players ahead of him on the depth chart. Miles Bridges, Dillon Brooks, and Ryan Dunn all occupy minutes that could otherwise be available to Fleming. There are only so many minutes to distribute over the course of a basketball game, and Jordan Ott has a responsibility to put his team in the best possible position to win.

Which brings me to my next question. If Fleming is somebody the Phoenix Suns believe in and want to continue developing, why isn't he spending more time with their G League affiliate, logging as many meaningful minutes as possible and building his confidence? Isn't that what the G League is for, after all?

Sure, you want him available to the NBA squad if and when his services are required. But young players need repetitions, opportunities to make mistakes, and the freedom to play through those mistakes without wondering whether their next opportunity will come three games from now. They need to experience success, build confidence, and apply what they're learning in an actual competitive environment.

And considering the Suns' G League affiliate is located in Tempe, there isn't exactly a significant geographic obstacle to the organization facilitating that development. It's a resource practically sitting in their backyard. Every reasonable opportunity to get Fleming meaningful minutes down there should be explored, allowing him to grow, flourish, and develop the confidence necessary to contribute at the NBA level.

I'm not suggesting that Fleming is entitled to minutes with the Phoenix Suns simply because he's young and possesses an intriguing physical profile. Those minutes need to be earned. What I am questioning is whether the organization is providing him with enough opportunities to develop the skills necessary to earn them.

So is this an issue with the player's development, the organization's ability to develop talent, or some combination of the two? I don't pretend to have that answer from the outside looking in. But for an organization that has invested in young talent and needs that talent to eventually become meaningful contributors, it's a question worth asking.

This isn't necessarily a statement of concern. It's more of an attempt to understand the organization's developmental direction for the young forward. Patience obviously needs to be exercised, as we've seen all too well how impatience has ultimately worked against the Phoenix Suns organization.

Ty Jerome is now lighting it up with the Memphis Grizzlies. Derrick Jones Jr. is a starter for the Los Angeles Clippers. Mikal Bridges has a championship, and Jalen Smith has established himself as a solid rotational player for the Chicago Bulls. All are examples of young talent that once resided in Phoenix and went on to experience varying degrees of success elsewhere.

The organizational history here should serve as a reminder that internal development requires both patience and purpose. You have to give young players time to become something, but you also have to provide them with the opportunities necessary to become it.

The Suns have very little margin for error when it comes to developing the young talent they've acquired, especially given their limited financial flexibility and draft capital. They need Fleming to become something, and hoping he does is considerably different from actively facilitating that growth. Development requires more than patience. It requires a purposeful approach that creates opportunities for progression. The organization invested in Fleming's potential. Now it needs to invest in the process of turning that potential into production.
