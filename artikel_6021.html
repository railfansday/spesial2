JetBlue Airbus A321neo Still Grounded At LAX After Late-Night Tail Strike Caught On Camera

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

A two-year-old JetBlue Airbus A321neo remains grounded at Los Angeles International Airport (LAX) after striking its tail during a late-night go-around on November 22. The aircraft had pitched up to climb away when the underside of its rear fuselage scraped the runway, sending sparks flying on a live broadcast from Airline Videos.

Originating from Boston (BOS), tail number N2193J was attempting to land on Runway 25L at 11:44 PM local time when the crew initiated the ill-fated go-around. Following the contact, the pilots climbed out safely, repositioned for a second approach to Runway 25L, and landed without further incident roughly 16 minutes later at 12:01 AM.

While the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) logged a preliminary incident report noting the go-around tail strike, the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) has not yet listed a formal investigation while the extent of the physical damage is assessed. The plane involved is a 2-year-old Airbus A321-271NX named 'StateMint Piece,' which was delivered to JetBlue in December 2024. Its scheduled return leg as Flight B6524 to New York-JFK (JFK) at 2:30 PM today was canceled.

The last tail strike by a JetBlue-operated aircraft occurred in February at San Francisco International Airport (SFO). That A321neo, tail N2188J, was also flying into the West Coast from Boston. Operating as Flight B62233, it struck its tail on Runway 28L at SFO during landing. According to the NTSB final report via AeroCorner, the jet bounced upon touchdown, and the first officer pulled the sidestick fully aft while applying go-around power, causing substantial damage to the lower fuselage.

The Aviation Safety Network reports on another tail strike by a plane flown by JetBlue in 2022. That Airbus A320, tail N760JB, was damaged during takeoff out of Hayden, Colorado, due to confusion with local general aviation traffic and pilot error. While attempting to coordinate with a Beechcraft B300 King Air that was inbound, the crew performed a rapid departure and rotated 24 knots under speed while also striking the tail of the jet.

Fortunately, in all three instances of recent tail strike mishaps for JetBlue air crews, no injuries were reported aboard any of the planes. While formal findings are still pending from Sunday night's incident, the video showing a shower of sparks during the late-night go-around means that there will be repairs to paint, scraped-off rivets, and potentially failed fuselage panels. • Lower fuselage protrusions, like heated drain masts and VHF/transponder antennas, may have also been sheared off or crushed.

Following the accident at SFO, tail N2188J suffered what the NTSB described as substantial damage. Post-flight inspections documented significant scraping and abrasion across the lower aft skin, along with damage to a pitot tube. As the jet bounced twice before the first officer pulled full aft sidestick, the impact required major repairs to restore the lower fuselage skin and support structure before the aircraft could return to service.

Similarly, tail N760JB saw substantial abrasion damage across multiple fuselage frames, according to NTSB post-mishap inspections. Notably, the crew did not initially realize they had struck the tail until air traffic control alerted them at 31,000 feet (9,448.8 meters), prompting an immediate diversion to Denver. Since the tail-strike damage may have compromised pressurized sections of the rear fuselage, the aircraft cannot safely fly at high altitude and must be repaired on-site or flown at low altitude to a maintenance base.

The A321neo is now a highly common sight.

For tail N2193J, the process is well underway to find the damage and correct it so that the plane can return to service safely. Immediately after landing, maintenance crews would have pulled the Cockpit Voice Recorder and Flight Data Recorder. The FAA and NTSB will analyze exact pitch angles, airspeed, and stick inputs at the critical phase of flight when the mishap occurred.

If the damage is considered to be superficial within the allowable limits for skin abrasion set by Airbus, mechanics at LAX can install an aerodynamic 'scab patch,' or doubler. If the inspection finds substantial damage, then engineers will need to be involved to develop a custom repair scheme.

Should that be necessary, the plane will fly under 10,000 feet (3,048 meters) per a 'ferry permit' to make its way to a proper maintenance, repair, and overhaul depot that can handle the scope of work. As for the pilots, the typical procedure is focused on identifying safety failures and retraining rather than punishment. That is provided that the accident was not a result of misconduct.
