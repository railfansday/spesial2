Some Australian fish are handling heat that should push their limits

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Two prized estuary fish showed no stress in simulated marine heatwaves, and the tolerance seems to belong to the whole species.

A marine heatwave is supposed to be bad news for fish. Yet some estuaries on Australia’s east coast already reach about 98°F (37°C) in summer.

That’s hotter than the harshest projections for the end of this century, and the fish there keep feeding, growing, and getting caught.

Researchers compared three fish species across hot and cool estuaries, then tested two of those species under simulated heatwaves.

The lab fish showed no sign of stress, whether they came from a hot estuary or a cool one.

If that holds, the fisheries built on these species may fare better in a warmer ocean than studies of coral reef fish had suggested.

Angus Mitchell and Ivan Nagelkerken of Adelaide University led the work with colleagues at the University of Technology Sydney, the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology, and James Cook University.

The species were yellowfin bream, sand whiting, and tarwhine.

The results suggest that tolerance may be shared across populations, rather than limited to fish from hotter estuaries.

“They’re what people target to take home, so understanding whether they can cope with warming estuaries matters directly for the fisheries that coastal communities depend on,” Mitchell said.

The team netted 563 fish from 20 sites in 15 estuaries in January and March 2024, along roughly 620 miles (1,000 kilometers) of coast.

Some sites were shallow lagoons where the water heats and cools fast.

The hottest sites in January peaked between 98°F and 100°F (36.5°C to 37.8°C), while the cooler sites topped out near 86°F (30°C).

All three species prefer water between about 68°F and 84°F (20°C and 29°C). So fish at the hottest sites were at times living well above that range.

In muscle samples from 294 of the fish, the team measured total protein and two markers of heat stress.

Those were the capacity to neutralize the reactive molecules that heat produces, and the damage those molecules do to fats in cell membranes.

On all three, fish from the hot sites and the cool sites were about the same, with one exception described below.

For the lab trial, the researchers collected young yellowfin bream and sand whiting from two spots north of Sydney.

Narrabeen Lagoon sometimes closes off from the sea. Its daily temperature range is three to four times wider than at Careel Bay, an open bay about 7 miles (11 kilometers) away.

At the Sydney Institute of Marine Science, each fish got its own tank, 120 tanks in all.

Some stayed at a baseline temperature. Others got a moderate heatwave, 3.6°F (2°C) above baseline for 14 days.

The rest got an extreme one, 7.2°F (4°C) above baseline for seven days. Both heatwaves delivered the same total heat.

On day five, a camera recorded how much each fish swam, hid, and ate, and how close a slowly lowered ruler could get before the fish fled.

The team scored 12 measures, from growth and liver size to feeding, boldness, and the three cellular tests.

On every one, fish in both heatwaves looked the same as fish at baseline.

It didn’t matter whether they came from the lagoon or the bay, and the extreme event was no harder on them than the moderate one.

About 6% of the fish died during the trial, spread evenly across the treatments.

A separate comparison revealed a difference in growth between fish from the two collection sites.

Sand whiting from the lagoon grew faster in length than whiting from the bay at their baseline temperatures, 79°F against 75°F (26°C against 24°C), though not under either heatwave.

One reason for the calm result, the authors wrote, is that the trial never got that hot.

The hottest tank, at 86°F (30°C), was still cooler than the water these fish meet in the warmest estuaries in January.

Wild sand whiting from hotter sites were heavier for their length than those from cooler sites. This pattern appeared among the 351 sand whiting collected in the field survey.

That suggests better body condition, though it does not establish faster growth.

Yellowfin bream and tarwhine showed no such pattern.

Tarwhine from the cool sites did carry more membrane damage than those from the hot sites, but with only 44 fish, the authors treated that with caution.

“We’re already seeing tropical species push further south as waters warm, reshuffling which fish support local fisheries,” Nagelkerken said.

“Our results suggest estuarine species with broad thermal tolerance may hold their ground, but that won’t extend to species with narrower limits such as coral reef fishes.”

The lab fish came from one hot site and one cool site per species. The result may not hold for populations at the far ends of each species’ range.

The field survey can show that fish from different estuaries look alike.

It can’t say whether they were born tolerant or became tolerant by living through hot summers.

And the study never measured the temperature at which these fish stop functioning, so the study does not establish their upper thermal limit.

Nagelkerken said the results offer hope, though many other species are more sensitive to climate change.

“Many estuaries are increasingly subject to other types of human pressures, such as pollution and overfishing,” he said.

“Hence, we need to manage our estuaries well to sustain the fisheries species that are set to be more resilient to a future climate.”

Separating inherited tolerance from acquired tolerance would take fish from different estuaries raised under identical conditions, or genetic tests for local adaptation.

The authors also want tracking tags on wild fish, to match each animal’s temperature history to how it copes.

Until more populations are sampled across the full range, the finding stands for three species, in one summer, on one coastline.

The full study was published in the journal Global Change Biology.
