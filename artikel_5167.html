Bitcoin Mining Difficulty Falls 9.13%, Giving Active Miners Some Breathing Room

Bitcoin’s automatic difficulty adjustment has made the next block easier for miners to find. At block 975,744 on November 14, the network reduced difficulty by approximately 9.13%, bringing the measure to 134.82 trillion.

That offers some relief to operators whose machines remain online. It also needs to be read correctly: the network has changed the amount of computational work expected for a successful block, not the electricity bill on a particular mining site or the price a miner receives when selling Bitcoin.

Mempool’s network data records the new difficulty at 134,823,837,323,272.7, compared with 148,364,561,003,867.4 previously. The adjustment block’s recorded time was 05:02:50 UTC on November 14. October 29’s adjustment had increased difficulty, so the latest move reverses part of that earlier tightening.

Bitcoin’s developer documentation explains the mechanism: the network retargets every 2,016 blocks, using the elapsed time across the preceding period to steer block production back toward its intended pace. Slower production leads to an easier target; faster production leads to a harder one.

The setting is therefore backward-looking. It summarises block production over an interval rather than identifying a specific company, outage or price move responsible for a change in mining activity.

Difficulty and expected success per hash move inversely. Dividing the previous difficulty by the new one gives approximately 1.1004. In other words, the same amount of hashing now has roughly a 10% higher expected success rate than before, assuming the relevant reward and other conditions remain unchanged.

That is a calculation from the published difficulty values, not a promise that every miner will produce 10% more coins in its next reporting period. Mining outcomes vary, pool payout methods differ and individual machines experience downtime. The improvement describes expected work efficiency at the protocol level.

A fleet upgrade is another way to change the calculation. NewsBTC’s earlier PowerCompute production report illustrates how operators also manage equipment, financing and power use alongside network conditions.

Consider a simplified example with fees and uptime held constant. If difficulty falls about 9.13% while Bitcoin’s dollar price also falls about 9.13%, the higher expected Bitcoin output per hash is broadly offset by the lower value of each coin. The percentage effects work through a ratio, rather than adding neatly together.

Real operations are more complicated. Electricity contracts, cooling, maintenance and financing can change the break-even point. Transaction fees also vary, affecting the reward available for the work performed. A miner with efficient equipment and cheap power can face very different economics from another running older machines.

That explains why an easier network does not automatically stop industry restructuring. As NewsBTC’s coverage of Hut 8’s AI-campus plans shows, companies may compare mining against other uses of their power and capital.

The Bitcoin Core implementation applies retargeting at defined block intervals. Between those points, the difficulty setting persists even while the amount of active mining equipment changes.

If improved economics encourage machines to return, blocks may arrive faster and put upward pressure on the following adjustment. If more capacity leaves, the opposite can happen. Estimates for that next change will shift as new blocks arrive; they should not be treated as completed adjustments.

The confirmed development is narrower and more useful: the November 14 cut reduces expected work per block for the current period. Whether that becomes lasting financial relief depends on the prices and costs miners face while using it.
