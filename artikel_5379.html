64% of shoppers will wait for Thanksgiving weekend as 2026 holiday plans shift

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Shoppers plan to wait for Thanksgiving weekend, buy online less and give more experiences, but their budgets may tell a different story.

Holiday shoppers are on track to spend more this year than last, but most of them will not take home much more. CNBC reports that Visa $V projects holiday sales growth of only 1.1% once price increases are excluded, a slower pace than last season's 1.3%. Much of the extra money will go to higher prices, with consumer prices in September up 3.7% from a year earlier. People are paying more and buying less. The squeeze shows in how they feel as well as in what they pay, and the University of Michigan's consumer sentiment index stood at 48 in October, according to the Chicago Sun-Times.

Shoppers who feel this pinched still spend on the holidays, but they change when they buy, where they buy, and what they give. Some hold out for a sale. Others weigh the convenience of ordering from a couch against the comfort of seeing a product before they pay. Still others reconsider what a present should be. These choices are plans made under pressure, so they show what people hope to do as much as what their budgets will allow. This season is defined by the distance between what shoppers intend and what they actually buy. Retailers are setting their calendars, prices, and promotions with that distance in mind.

The National Retail Federation set out to identify how consumers planned to shop and give gifts this winter holiday season, looking at gifts of experiences, the appeal of Thanksgiving weekend, and the place of online shopping. Retail spending in November and December is projected to reach $1 trillion, up 4.2%, according to National Retail Federation figures reported by the Chicago Sun-Times. Most of that gain reflects higher prices rather than more goods, and each of the shoppers' plans is a way to cope with those prices. Shoppers chase discounts, choose between a screen and a store, or reach for something other than a boxed present. Each plan also depends on money that shoppers may or may not have when the time comes to spend it. The question for every one of them is whether it holds once shoppers reach the register.

Most holiday shoppers now treat Thanksgiving weekend as the real start of the season and plan to hold their purchases until then. The National Retail Federation reports that 64% of shoppers expect to hold off until that weekend, compared with 51% five years ago. Waiting has gone from a close split to the plan most shoppers follow. Shoppers wait for the discounts, not for the tradition, and 56% name sales and discounts as a reason they choose a retailer. With prices high, shoppers are trying to time their buying to the lowest prices of the year.

Retailers have started selling those deals weeks before the weekend arrives. Ulta $ULTA began its "early Black Friday" deals on Oct. 25, the Chicago Sun-Times reports, about a month ahead of the weekend most shoppers are saving for. An early start lets stores reach shoppers while they are still willing to spend. "While the consumer is willing to spend now, at some point they may not be," said Rob Karr, CEO of the Illinois Retail Merchants Association. Stores set much of the season's timing, and shoppers who wait for Black Friday are waiting for a date that retailers have already spread across several weeks.

Ordering gifts online is still the leading holiday plan, though it holds a smaller lead with shoppers than it once did. Just 53% of shoppers plan to buy online, according to the National Retail Federation. That is a bare majority, and no other way of shopping is more popular. Among shoppers who don't plan to buy online, the most common reason is a wish to see items before buying them, which 38% of them cite. For many people, a trip to the store this season is a way to avoid paying for the wrong thing and then dealing with a return.

Across U.S. retail as a whole, the online portion has grown instead. E-commerce made up 17.1% of U.S. retail sales in the second quarter of 2026, according to FRED, up from 16.3% a year earlier. Stores still account for most retail sales, but the online portion got larger over that year rather than smaller. A smaller share of shoppers can intend to order holiday gifts online in the same year that online purchases make up a bigger part of all U.S. retail sales.

Waiting for the weekend works only for shoppers who still have money when it comes. In a Deloitte survey of Chicago-area shoppers, about a quarter had already spent their whole holiday budget by the close of October, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. Those shoppers reach Thanksgiving weekend with little left to spend.

The shoppers best placed to wait are the ones who have kept money aside for the weekend.

The open question is whether shoppers who plan to look at gifts in a store will still go home and order them online. The season will show whether shoppers follow through on their plan to buy in person.

A present that someone does rather than unwraps, such as a night out, a trip or a concert ticket, has become a mainstream part of holiday planning. The National Retail Federation reports that a record 27% of shoppers intend to give someone an experience this season. Millennials are among the most likely buyers of these gifts, with 38% of them planning one. Gen Z is the only generation in which the share planning an experience gift has grown, and the youngest adult shoppers are behind the gain. Younger buyers appear to be changing what they count as a gift.

When shoppers rank where their money should go, boxed presents still come first. In a Visa $V survey of shoppers with holiday wish lists, 27% made physical gifts their spending priority, while 15% chose experiences for others, according to CNBC. The record measures what shoppers intend to give more than what they will pay for.

The desire behind that record is genuine. "Consumers are aspirational. They want those experiences, they want to travel, they want to be out in the world," Michael Brown, Visa's principal U.S. economist, told CNBC. That wish competes with gift shopping for the same household money, and Brown expects people who travel more to give fewer presents they can wrap.

The record shows what shoppers, and young ones above all, want to give, not yet where their gift budgets will go.
