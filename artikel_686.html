How a football team rebuilt after 19 players died in plane crash

It was 2am when Ivan Tozzo picked up his mobile phone and heard his football club's secretary screaming his name.

A plane carrying the Chapecoense team had crashed into a mountain.

Seventy-one people were dead, including 19 of 22 players from the Brazilian club who were on their way to the biggest game in their history.

It would later emerge the plane had run out of fuel and investigators would find the airline and pilot were to blame as the fuel load was insufficient for the flight on 28 November 2016.

But in the early hours, as Tozzo watched the TV news in the changing room at the club's stadium, his only thought was for the players who would not be coming back to this room.

When he sat alone in the club's boardroom after the funerals as the only director still alive, he cried.

Ivan Tozzo recalls the phone call he got after the crash: "It was our club secretary, Jorilda, who was desperate, screaming 'Ivan Ivan'."

Chapecoense had been travelling to Colombia to play Atletico Nacional of Medellin in the first leg of the Copa Sudamericana final, South America's second most prestigious club tournament.

It was a first continental final for the Brazilian team.

"The team was very motivated, knowing that we were engraving our names inside the history of the club," goalkeeper Jakson Follmann, one of the three surviving players, tells BBC Sport.

Aged 24 at the time, Follmann had also been looking forward to his imminent wedding, little knowing he would be spending two months in hospital instead.

"Everyone was completely proud of everything they were doing, both for the team and for the community of Chapeco and the region," Follmann says.

Commentator Rafael Henzel, who was one of six survivors of the crash but who died in 2019 following a heart attack, described the Copa Sudamericana as the team's "World Cup" and said in 2017 that "if anyone's ever died happy, it was the people on that plane".

Survivors Neto, Alan Ruschel, Follmann and Henzel (left to right) appeared together at a friendly between Brazil and Colombia two months after the crash

The LaMia 2933 flight from Santa Cruz in Bolivia to the Colombian city of Medellin had a journey distance similar to the plane's flying range, but it did not stop to refuel and crashed into a mountain just 18km from its intended destination.

The pilot, Miguel Quiroga, who was also a co-owner of the airline, died in the crash. In a leaked tape, he can be heard warning of a "total electric failure" and "lack of fuel". However, he did not make a formal distress call.

A Bolivian investigation concluded that the pilot and the airline were directly responsible for the crash, while a Colombian investigation said there was no technical failure and blamed the pilot, the airline and Bolivian regulators.

"I don't call it an accident, I say it was a crime that could have been avoided," says Follman, whose right leg had to be partially amputated to save his life.

"It's the crime of not stopping to refuel, the greed to think that he [the pilot] could get there... with the fuel practically finished."

To date, no-one has been found guilty in relation to the crash.

At the time, Bolivia did not have a system for prosecuting companies. Even when such a facility was introduced in 2021, it did not cover negligent homicide or causing an air disaster.

Charges were brought against individuals in LaMia's management - who denied wrongdoing - but in 2020 a trial was delayed by jurisdictional disputes and difficulties involving defendants who were abroad.

The plane was just 18km from its destination when it ran out of fuel

A number of key details about the disaster were established early on.

At 21:16 local time, the aircraft displayed a low fuel warning but flew on without the crew alerting local authorities.

Fourteen minutes later, the plane began its descent to Medellin airport but was put in a holding pattern.

At 21:49, the crew requested priority permission to land, vaguely citing "problems with fuel".

"I remember the lights went out suddenly; then I started praying, asking God to help us," recalled defender Neto, the last of the survivors to be found.

"But a lot of people thought the plane was just landing because it was not an abrupt fall."

Within five minutes, all the engines, drained of fuel, failed.

"Then, I couldn't hear the plane engine any more. It was just a wind. And then an alarm: beep beep beep," Neto told the BBC World Service in 2017.

Thousands of people gathered in the rain as the funeral cortege went past

Seven thousand kilometres away in the small southern Brazilian city of Chapeco, Tozzo watched the scale of the disaster unfold on TV screens at the stadium in the middle of the night.

"At five o'clock in the morning, the players' wives began to arrive. It was despair. People threw themselves on the ground and cried," says Tozzo, a local entrepreneur who was the club's vice-president at the time and had joined the club in 2009 when they were not even playing in Brazil's national football pyramid.

Two days earlier, Tozzo had suffered misgivings about the trip that were so strong that he had changed his mind about travelling to Medellin.

"It was a thing from God. It was not for me to go. I called [financial director] Decio, who organised all our trips, and I told him that I would not go," he says.

"He went crazy with me. 'Why? You agreed to go, now you do not want to go? You have to go.'

"My sister was next to me, saying, 'You need to go to this match, you worked so hard to get to this moment.' But in my head I said, 'No Ivan, I will not go', and I stayed at home."

After the crash, he had another strong feeling about his path.

"I think I had a mission here on Earth to continue Chapecoense after the accident," Tozzo says. "I was the only one of the directors left alive so I took over as [interim] president."

A sleepless week followed in which Tozzo arranged flights to Colombia for doctors, relatives and pastors to identify and recover the remains and be with the survivors.

A memorial service was organised and, in pouring rain, the coffins were brought into Chape's stadium, Arena Conda.

During the service came the news that Atletico Nacional had asked for the Copa Sudamericana trophy and winners' prize money to be awarded to Chapecoense.

Tozzo was determined Chapecoense would compete again, but none of the surviving players were in any shape to play and they only had two other squad members â who did not travel to Medellin because of injury â and a handful in the academy.

They called on Rui Costa, who had been sacked by Gremio as football director six months earlier and accepted the same role with Chapecoense, and appointed Vagner Mancini as coach.

In a matter of weeks, 22 new players were signed, most of them on loan.

Tozzo's message rang clear in the ears of the new players.

"Our fans were very sad, Chapecoense was very sad, and playing again with joy was not easy," he says.

"We explained very well the responsibility they would have."

Chapecoense's first match back â eight weeks after the crash â was a friendly against the last team the club had played, Palmeiras.

Before the game at the Arena Conda, Follmann â in a wheelchair â was joined by Neto and the other surviving player, Alan Ruschel, to receive the Copa Sudamericana trophy.

"It was the first time I had gone back to the arena, the first time I really had contact with people, because I was still hospitalised," says Follmann.

"When I received the trophy, a movie went through my head because our captain Cleber Santana should have been holding it surrounded by all of us, celebrating."

The friendly against Palmeiras was the first of 80 matches that Chapecoense played in 2017, only five of them friendlies.

There was an offer from the Brazilian Football Confederation to give the club immunity from relegation for three seasons, but Tozzo rejected it.

"How can you psych up the players to play their best if they know that their classification is guaranteed?" he asks.

As it turned out, Chapecoense stayed up on their own merits for precisely three seasons.

Chapecoense's new players faced Atletico Nacional in the South American Super Cup in 2017

In 2017, as Sudamericana champions, they once again had to face Atletico Nacional, the reigning Copa Libertadores winners, in the South American Super Cup.

It meant another flight to Medellin just months after the crash. This time Tozzo was on board.

"We went to see the exact location [of the crash]," he says. "I can't believe it was so close [to the airport]."

Chapecoense lost 6-3 on aggregate but later in the year won their state championship and finished as high as eighth in the national league in a demonstration of remarkable resilience.

The following year, in the second wave of new players, defender Bruno Pacheco was signed.

Pacheco, who has made nearly 150 appearances for the club in two spells, says: "Stepping on to the pitch at the arena was an emotional experience.

"I was representing those who are no longer with us, as well as the fans. I'm happy to be part of such an important chapter in the club's history."

After relegation from Serie A in 2019, Chapecoense won the Serie B title to earn promotion back to the top flight.

But that lasted just one season and they spent the next four terms in the second tier. They have spent this year in Serie A but will be relegated again.

Neto spoke at a ceremony in memory of the 71 passengers who died, marking the 10th anniversary of the tragedy

After the crash, Neto's only appearance for Chapecoense came as a substitute in a charity friendly in December 2017, when money was raised to support relatives of the victims.

He remained at the club as a player for two more years until being forced to retire by the pain of his crash-related injuries.

He became a Christian evangelist, and he speaks for the relatives of his former team-mates who are still seeking justice as some families pursue compensation and hold out hope for criminal convictions.

Left-back Ruschel, who suffered severe injuries and had spinal surgery, was the only player to continue his professional playing career meaningfully, returning to the game nine months after the crash and staying with Chapecoense until 2021.

He only retired this November, helping his side Juventude win promotion to Serie A in his final game, ironically to swap places with Chapecoense.

Neto presented him with a commemorative plaque at half-time in Chapecoense's game against Palmeiras at the Arena Conda on 24 November when both men held aloft a jersey bearing Follmann's name.

Ruschel referenced the plane crash in a video announcing his retirement, calling it "a day that changed my life forever".

"I lost friends, companions and people with whom I shared dreams and projects," he said.

"And God gave me a second chance. I didn't know if I would be able to play football again, but I believed that if I was here, there was still a purpose for my life.

"And today, I have the privilege of being able to choose the moment to say goodbye."

Follmann's wedding - the one he had been preparing for before the crash - did happen and he became a father.

Despite having an offer to join Brazil's sitting volleyball team at the Paralympics, he turned to careers as a singer, motivational speaker and author, but with a new perspective.

"I observe everything around me. I observe life," he says.

"I have issues with making plans for the future, because life showed me that I only have today to live.

"I don't leave anything for tomorrow. I say everything today. I forgive today, I hug today."

Tozzo, the director who stayed at home, continues to live in Chapeco where he runs various businesses.

"I haven't talked about the accident for a long time," he says. "We keep remembering, and every time we talk, tears fall, but these are things in life that we have to face."
