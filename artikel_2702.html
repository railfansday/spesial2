Michigan does about-face on Trev Alberts, potentially owing him $17.2 million

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

The last time Trev Alberts made this kind of a stir, he was the subject of Bill Tobin's legendary rant against Mel Kiper.

This time, the situation potentially involves a significant payment to Alberts.

Via ESPN, Michigan backed out of an agreement to hire Alberts (who played in only 29 career games over three seasons after being the fifth overall pick in the 1994 draft) to become the university's next athletic director. Alberts told ESPN that he had signed a "binding memorandum of understanding" for the position.

Alberts's lawyer, Tom Mars, told ESPN that Michigan owes Alberts a $17.2 million buyout.

For that kind of money, Michigan will be very tempted to refuse to pay, forcing litigation and the expense, uncertainty, and delay that goes along with it. Alberts could decide to take less than he's owed, simply to end the fight.

Of course, Alberts also could become determined to prove a point — if he and Mars believe Alberts has an ironclad claim to the money.

Michigan's about-face apparently relates to deposition testimony from Alberts in a Title IX case arising from his tenure at Nebraska. Per ESPN, Alberts gave testimony on December 4, 2025, that Michigan regards as problematic.

Alberts was asked whether he believes it's true that college coaches should never have sex with the athletes they're coaching. The obvious answer is, "Yes." Alberts pursued a different strategy.

"I don't know the answer to that," Alberts said.

"Well, I mean, you know, it just depends," Alberts replied.

He then said the situation could be "nuanced," due to preexisting relationships or eligibility waivers that could result in athletes being nearly the same age as young assistant coaches.

It's an example of the gamesmanship in which some witnesses will engage when grilled by lawyers during depositions. The lawyer wanted to get an admission from Alberts that could be used broadly, and repeatedly, in the case arising from a relationship between a women's basketball player and an assistant coach. Alberts tried to resist by clumsily splitting hairs.

Then there's the question of whether Michigan is genuinely troubled by the testimony, or whether the school is using it as a way to justify not hiring Alberts — and not paying him.

Whatever happens between Alberts and Michigan, it's another example of the so-called "chaos" in college sports being not the result of players doing their best in trying to get paid but schools doing their worst in allocating resources.

If Michigan's concerns about the testimony are legitimate and not a pretext for hiring someone else, it shows that Michigan engaged in poor vetting of Alberts before signing him to a memo of understanding. If it's a pretext, it shows that Michigan made a hasty decision and then tried to justify a change by trumping up testimony that otherwise wouldn't have been an issue for Michigan.

Regardless, the possibility that Michigan will owe Alberts all or part of $17.2 million is another tangible example of the fact that the "chaos" comes not from players making good decisions but from schools making bad ones.
