Exertional rhabdomyolysis workouts range from spin classes to military training; here are 10 workouts and conditions tied to the muscle injury and its warning signs

Exercise is supposed to break muscle down a little so it can rebuild stronger. Push the dose far enough past what the body is ready for, and the damage stops being a training signal. It becomes a medical problem that can reach the kidneys.

That condition is exertional rhabdomyolysis, often shortened to rhabdo. It is the breakdown of skeletal muscle fibers brought on by physical effort. When those fibers rupture, their contents spill into the bloodstream. Two of those contents matter most. Myoglobin is a protein that stores oxygen inside muscle cells. A heavy load of it in the blood can injure the kidneys. Creatine kinase, or CK, is an enzyme that doctors use to gauge how much muscle has been damaged.

The classic warning signs are severe muscle pain, weakness and dark urine the color of tea. Many patients do not show all three at once. Swelling in the affected muscles can also appear, along with soreness far beyond the usual post-workout ache.

Diagnosis rests on a blood test for CK. A level of roughly five times the upper limit of normal is a commonly used threshold for the condition. Treatment is medical care, centered mainly on intravenous fluids.

The risk does not come from any single sport. It builds from a familiar set of contributors: unaccustomed or extreme exertion, heavy eccentric work in which muscles lengthen under load, heat and dehydration. Sickle cell trait, certain drugs and supplements, and a recent illness can also raise the odds. Those factors show up across very different settings, from a boutique fitness studio to a military training base.

The slides that follow cover exertional rhabdomyolysis workouts and conditions — 10 settings where those factors can converge. Some are named formats, such as indoor cycling classes, CrossFit-style sessions and college football conditioning. Others are circumstances that can turn an ordinary routine into a risky one. Examples include a hard return after a long layoff and training in hot, humid weather. The list is a selection, not a ranking. It does not claim to cover every way the injury occurs, and its order says nothing about which activity causes the most cases.

Case reports in the medical literature describe exertional rhabdomyolysis after spin classes, mostly in beginners who rode hard in one of their first sessions. The common thread is an unconditioned rider working against heavy resistance at high effort.

Indoor cycling classes use stationary bikes with a weighted flywheel. Each rider controls the resistance with a knob or lever. An instructor sets the pace, often to music, and calls out climbs, sprints and standing intervals. That format lets a newcomer try to match regulars who have trained for months, even when the newcomer's legs are not ready for the load.

The thighs do most of the work on a spin bike, especially the quadriceps at the front of the leg. In published spin-related cases, thigh pain and swelling are the dominant complaints. That fits the mechanics of the exercise. Every pedal stroke loads the same muscle group, and a hard class strings together long efforts with little recovery between them.

The case reports do not establish how often this happens. No reliable incidence figure exists for spin classes. What the reports share is the profile of the rider. They mostly involve people new to the format rather than experienced cyclists who have built up tolerance over time.

The warning signs are easy to misread after a first class. Some soreness after any new workout is expected, so a rider may dismiss thigh pain that is far worse than usual. Pain severe enough to make walking or climbing stairs difficult is a reason to seek medical care. So are legs that swell or feel weak, or urine that turns dark after the ride.

For first-time riders, the risk described in these reports comes from the gap between conditioning and effort. Resistance is the variable a rider controls most directly. Keeping it lower in early classes and raising it over several sessions narrows that gap.

CrossFit-style workouts that pack very high repetition counts into a short window are a recognized setting for exertional rhabdomyolysis. CrossFit has acknowledged the risk through its own cartoon mascot, Uncle Rhabdo.

Uncle Rhabdo is drawn as a clown hooked up to a dialysis machine, with his kidneys and other organs spilling onto the floor. He came from CrossFit itself, not from outside critics. The image links the training style's intensity to the kidney damage that severe muscle breakdown can cause. A second clown, Pukie, represents the vomiting that can follow a hard session.

The danger in this format comes from density. A single session can call for dozens of repetitions of one movement, such as pull-ups, push-ups, burpees or wall-ball shots. Many workouts are scored on time. Others ask for as many rounds as possible, a format known as AMRAP, within a fixed window. Rest between rounds tends to be short or absent. Muscles pushed toward failure again and again in a compressed period accumulate damage faster than they can recover.

Newcomers are a particular concern. A person new to this style of training may try to match the pace or volume of experienced members in a group class. Their muscles have not adapted to that workload. Going all out from the first session skips the gradual build-up that conditioning normally provides.

CrossFit programming includes scaling, which means cutting the load, the repetitions or the difficulty of a movement to fit an athlete's current ability. Scaling lets a beginner follow the same workout structure at a lower dose. It is the format's own built-in tool for limiting how much new work a muscle takes on at once.

Published medical case reports have described rhabdo after CrossFit-style training. Those reports do not establish how often it happens. They do show the condition turning up in ordinary gyms, not only on military bases or at endurance events.

Early symptoms after a high-rep session can pass for normal soreness. The difference is degree. Pain, swelling or weakness far out of proportion to the workout calls for a doctor, not another training day. After a session heavy in pull-ups or push-ups, those symptoms may concentrate in the upper arms.

Other factors can compound the strain of a dense, high-volume workout. Dehydration, a recent illness and certain drugs or supplements are all recognized contributors.

Military training is a setting where exertional rhabdomyolysis is formally tracked. The U.S. Armed Forces Health Surveillance Division reports on cases among active-component service members in its Medical Surveillance Monthly Report, known as the MSMR.

The division draws those counts from the Defense Medical Surveillance System, a database of medical encounters across the services.

That surveillance shows the injury is not spread evenly across the force. The reports break cases down by service branch, sex, age and race. Rates differ across each of those groups.

Three conditions recur as drivers in military settings: heat, an abrupt ramp-up in training and repeated exertion without enough rest. Each one adds strain to muscle in a different way. Recruit training can combine all three in the same week.

Much military conditioning happens outdoors, often in uniform and gear. Heat raises the physical load of any given effort. When it is paired with dehydration, working muscle is under more stress than the same workout would put on it in cool conditions.

Recruits arrive from civilian life with a wide range of fitness levels. Basic training then puts them on a fixed schedule of daily physical training, marching and drills. For someone who was mostly inactive beforehand, that change in workload is abrupt. Muscle that has not adapted to a workload is more vulnerable to breakdown when it suddenly meets one.

The third driver is repetition across days. Damaged muscle fibers need time to recover. When a unit trains hard again before that recovery happens, damage from one session can add to damage from the next. A volume of work that a body tolerates once can become a problem when it is repeated without rest.

Recruits also have limited control over the pace. Training follows a unit schedule, so a trainee cannot simply skip a session to let sore muscles recover.

In basic training, soreness is part of the routine, which can make warning signs easier to dismiss. Pain far beyond what the day's training should cause is one warning sign. Weakness that makes ordinary tasks hard, such as climbing stairs or lifting a rifle, is another. Swelling or dark urine is also a reason to see medical staff.

In January 2011, 13 University of Iowa football players were hospitalized with rhabdomyolysis after a single offseason workout built around back squats. The episode is a well-documented example of exertional rhabdo striking several athletes on the same team at once.

The players involved were not beginners. They were Division I athletes on a Big Ten roster. Fitness built over months of training does not fully protect against a session that demands far more volume than the body has recently handled.

The back squat helps explain the scale of the injury. The lift loads the largest muscles in the body — the quadriceps, hamstrings and glutes — along with the lower back. When a workout piles many sets of a lift like that into one session, a large mass of muscle tissue absorbs damage at the same time. More damaged tissue means more myoglobin and creatine kinase spilling into the bloodstream.

Timing was the other factor. The workout came during the offseason, when players are not in the weekly rhythm of games and in-season practice. A sudden jump in volume after time away from heavy training is one of the clearest patterns behind exertional rhabdo, whatever an athlete's baseline fitness.

A team setting widens the reach of that risk. Everyone in a group session does the same work, at the same time, under the same coaching plan. A session that overwhelms one player can overwhelm many.

Warning signs can be easy to dismiss in football. Sore, heavy legs are routine after a squat day. The difference is degree: pain and swelling well beyond normal soreness, weakness that makes walking or climbing stairs difficult, or urine that turns dark. Those signs call for prompt medical evaluation rather than another day of training.

The Iowa hospitalizations show how a standard lift can become dangerous through dosage. The lesson concerns the size of the first heavy session after a break, not the choice of exercise.

Heavy eccentric training, in which a muscle lengthens while resisting a load, damages muscle fibers more than raising the same weight does. Slow negatives and high-volume lowering work are the clearest examples. They are a recognized route to exertional rhabdomyolysis when the dose outruns a lifter's conditioning.

Every repetition has two halves. The concentric phase shortens the muscle, as when the arms curl a dumbbell up. The eccentric phase lengthens it under tension, as when the dumbbell is lowered back down with control. Lowering into a squat, descending from a pull-up bar and running downhill all load muscles eccentrically.

Muscles can produce more force while lengthening than while shortening. That lets a lifter control a weight on the way down that they could not lift on the way up. Some programs use this through negatives, in which a partner helps raise a heavy load and the lifter lowers it alone. Other programs stretch the lowering phase to several seconds per repetition.

Eccentric contractions also cost less energy than concentric ones at the same load. A set can feel manageable, with less breathlessness and burn, while the muscle absorbs substantial mechanical strain. That gap between the effort a lifter feels and the damage the muscle takes makes eccentric-heavy sessions easy to misjudge.

The strain shows up as microscopic disruption inside the muscle fibers. In most cases the result is delayed onset muscle soreness, which typically builds over the following one to three days. Exercise physiologists use eccentric protocols in laboratory research because they reliably raise markers of muscle damage, including creatine kinase.

The line between ordinary soreness and rhabdo is a matter of degree. Pain far beyond normal stiffness points to a medical problem rather than a hard workout. So does a limb that swells or loses strength, or urine that turns dark. Blood markers of muscle damage after eccentric work can keep climbing for days. Symptoms that worsen instead of easing are a reason for a medical evaluation.

Muscles adapt to this kind of loading quickly. One eccentric session substantially reduces the damage caused by a similar session later, a phenomenon known as the repeated bout effect. The risk is concentrated in the first exposures, especially when volume or load is high from the start.

Long-distance races reliably push creatine kinase above normal, yet most finishers never develop clinical exertional rhabdomyolysis. Marathons and ultramarathons serve as a contrast case. They show that a high lab number and a dangerous muscle injury are not the same thing.

A marathon covers 26.2 miles, or 42.195 kilometers. An ultramarathon is any race longer than that. Common ultra distances include 50 kilometers, 100 kilometers and 100 miles. Hours of repeated foot strikes cause small-scale damage to muscle fibers over the whole course. That damage lets CK leak out of injured muscle cells. As a result, its level climbs in the blood after a race.

A raised CK reading on its own does not settle a diagnosis. Rhabdomyolysis as a clinical condition involves muscle breakdown heavy enough to cause symptoms. In serious cases, the myoglobin released from that muscle puts the kidneys at risk. Many runners with elevated post-race levels feel nothing worse than the stiff legs that follow any long effort. Serious cases among race finishers are uncommon.

Preparation explains much of the gap. Runners who build mileage over months condition their muscles to the specific stress of distance running. The protection that develops after an initial damaging session is known as the repeated-bout effect. A well-prepared marathoner reaches race day with muscles already adapted to hours of impact. That places a planned race in a different category from a workout the body has never done before.

Risk rises at the extremes. The longest ultramarathon distances carry more exposure than a standard marathon. Hot race-day weather and dehydration also raise the odds of tipping from ordinary soreness into a medical problem. Starting a race while recovering from a recent illness adds to the strain. So does taking certain drugs or supplements that affect muscle or kidney function.

The practical warning for distance runners is pain out of proportion to the effort. Legs that stay weak instead of loosening up over the following days are one signal. Urine that turns dark brown, like cola, is another. Either is a reason to get medical care promptly rather than rest at home and wait it out.

Returning to full-intensity training after weeks or months away is one of the best-established triggers for exertional rhabdomyolysis. The core problem is unaccustomed exertion. Muscles that have lost conditioning are asked to absorb a workload they once handled but no longer can.

A layoff can start in many ways. An injury, a surgery, a demanding stretch at work, a holiday season or an offseason can all pull a trained person away from the gym or the road. The reason for the break matters less than the loss of muscle tolerance that builds while it lasts.

Exercise physiology has a name for that tolerance. The repeated bout effect describes how one session of unfamiliar exercise protects muscle against damage from a similar session later on. Regular training keeps that protection in place. Time away lets it fade. Returning muscle ends up closer to the state of muscle that has never done the exercise at all.

Experienced athletes face a specific risk here. They tend to remember the weights, distances, paces and repetition counts they handled before the break. Picking up at those numbers on the first day back can cause far more muscle damage than the identical session would have caused at peak fitness.

Illness adds another layer. A recent illness is a recognized contributor to exertional rhabdomyolysis. When sickness was the reason for the layoff, the returning athlete may carry two risk factors into the same workout.

Telling ordinary soreness apart from muscle breakdown can be hard in the first day or two after a comeback session. Stiffness that is uncomfortable but manageable is expected. Pain far out of proportion to the workout, swollen or weak muscles and dark urine point to something more serious. Those signs call for medical evaluation.

The practical response is a gradual return. Starting well below previous loads and volumes, then building across several sessions, gives muscle time to readapt. That build-up restores the protective effect the break erased before the hardest efforts resume.

Hard exercise in heat and humidity raises the risk of exertional rhabdomyolysis because the body has to cool itself and fuel working muscle at the same time. Heat and dehydration are recognized contributors to the muscle breakdown, and they add to the strain of an overly demanding workout.

Sweat is the body's main cooling tool during exercise. It works when the moisture evaporates from the skin. Humid air slows that evaporation, so sweat drips off without carrying away much heat. As a result, core temperature climbs faster, even at a pace that would feel manageable on a cool, dry day.

The heart then faces competing demands. Blood is sent to the skin to shed heat, while working muscles need blood to deliver oxygen and clear waste. As fluid is lost through sweat, total blood volume shrinks. Less blood reaches the muscles and the kidneys, and the strain on both grows.

Dehydration matters twice in rhabdo. It makes muscle fibers more vulnerable during the workout. It also leaves the kidneys less able to clear the myoglobin that damaged muscle releases afterward. Concentrated urine and reduced kidney blood flow give that protein more opportunity to cause injury.

Heat-related muscle breakdown can also overlap with exertional heatstroke. Heatstroke is a medical emergency in which core body temperature rises to dangerous levels. Rhabdomyolysis is a known complication of heatstroke. Confusion or collapse during hard exercise in heat calls for emergency care rather than rest and fluids at home.

Heat acclimatization is the body's gradual adjustment to training in warm weather. Over repeated sessions, sweating begins earlier and plasma volume expands. Coaches and military trainers use a measure called wet-bulb globe temperature to decide when to scale back outdoor work. It accounts for humidity, sun and wind as well as air temperature.

Dark urine after a session in a heat wave or a humid gym is a reason to seek medical care. So is muscle pain or swelling out of proportion to the workout. A CK blood test can confirm whether muscle breakdown is under way.

High-intensity bootcamp and interval classes can lead to exertional rhabdomyolysis when a participant stacks repeated all-out efforts with little recovery. The format packs a large volume of hard muscular work into a short window.

Bootcamp sessions typically string together bodyweight drills such as burpees, jump squats, walking lunges, push-ups and mountain climbers. Many of those movements load the same muscle groups again and again. The thighs and glutes, for example, may absorb squats, lunges and jump landings within a few minutes of each other.

Jumping drills add a specific kind of strain. Each landing forces the quadriceps to lengthen while they brake the body's fall. That lengthening action, known as an eccentric contraction, is the type most closely tied to muscle damage. A class built around jump squats, box jumps or skater hops repeats it many times in a single session.

Interval formats compress effort further. The protocol associated with Japanese researcher Izumi Tabata calls for 20 seconds of maximal work followed by 10 seconds of rest, repeated for eight rounds. Tabata's original research used stationary bikes, not the mixed bodyweight circuits that now often carry the name. Many classes borrow that structure or similar work-to-rest ratios. Short rests limit how much the muscles recover between bouts.

The group setting adds a separate pressure. Participants follow the instructor's clock rather than their own condition. Some classes also display heart rates or scores on screens around the room. Keeping pace with a room of regulars can push a participant to work harder than their muscles are prepared for.

Ordinary soreness and an injury tend to follow different patterns. Delayed onset muscle soreness usually peaks one to three days after exercise and eases with gentle movement. Pain after a bootcamp class that keeps building needs medical evaluation. So does soreness that comes with swelling, marked weakness or dark urine.

Sickle cell trait is a recognized risk factor for exertional rhabdomyolysis during hard, sustained exercise. Athletes who carry it face greater danger when a session pushes them to maximal effort without enough rest between bouts.

The trait is not the same as sickle cell disease. A person with the trait inherited one copy of the gene for sickle hemoglobin and one copy for normal hemoglobin. Most carriers have no symptoms in daily life, and many do not know they have it. Someone with sickle cell disease inherited two affected copies and lives with a chronic illness.

The risk emerges under physical stress. During intense exertion, red blood cells in a carrier can change into a rigid, curved sickle shape. Those cells can clog small blood vessels in working muscle. Muscle deprived of oxygen-rich blood can then break down quickly. Heat, dehydration and the thinner air at high altitude can add to that strain.

In a carrier, the breakdown can surface during the workout itself rather than the next morning. An athlete may slump with sudden weakness or report muscle pain well beyond normal fatigue. Dark urine afterward is another signal. Any of these calls for prompt medical care.

College sports have taken up the issue. The NCAA has made sickle cell trait part of its athlete-screening and precaution policy. That brings the condition to the attention of team medical staff. Knowing an athlete's status lets coaches and trainers plan sessions with it in mind.

The common precautions follow the same logic that applies to other rhabdo risks. Intensity builds gradually across weeks rather than days. Longer rest between hard repetitions lets muscle recover before the next bout. Stopping a drill at the first sign of unusual weakness takes priority over finishing it.

The trait does not rule anyone out of sport. Carriers compete at every level, from high school to the professional ranks. Screening gives staff a chance to account for a known vulnerability, both in how a workout is designed and in how they respond to early symptoms.
