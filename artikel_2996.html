Warren Buffett Just Gave Away $5.5 Billion of Berkshire Stock. He Says His Confidence in Berkshire Is "As Strong as Ever."

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The rest of his shares are set to leave his hands by 2034. Does this matter for the stock?

Warren Buffett gave away another big part of his wealth on Monday, Nov. 16. The chairman emeritus of Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -1.02%)(BRKB -1.03%) converted 6,150 Class A shares into 9,225,000 Class B shares and donated them to four family foundations. At Monday's closing price, the gift was worth around $5.5 billion.

This gift is over three times the 2.7 million Class B shares he gave the same four foundations last Thanksgiving. Buffett also paired it with a short note to shareholders.

"You heard plenty from me in September about my confidence in Berkshire. That confidence is as strong as ever," Buffett wrote.

Berkshire's Class B shares finished at $592.85 on Monday, just under Friday's record close of $592.94. The stock is up around 18% in 2026 as I write.

Warren Buffett. Image source: Getty Images.

This year's note is much shorter than last year's, and most of it is about kindness, not business.

On Berkshire itself, Buffett just pointed back to September. That month, Berkshire announced that he had become chairman emeritus and that his son Howard Buffett had taken over as chairman.

In a letter put out with that news, Buffett wrote that he was more confident than ever in the future. In other words, Monday's gift isn't a vote against the stock.

After Monday's gift, Buffett holds 182,140 Class A shares and 1,162 Class B shares. His stake's worth around $162 billion at Monday's prices, or about 13% of Berkshire's market value.

It's all spoken for. In July, Buffett wrote that he hopes his children can "carry out the disposal of my shares" by Dec. 31, 2034. Either way, he said, the shares will reach the four foundations by then.

Showing how much the giving has picked up, Buffett's two gifts this year come to around 21.2 million Class B shares -- 12 million in July and about 9.2 million on Monday. The four family foundations got around 5.6 million Class B shares from him in two gifts in 2025. This year's total is almost four times as big.

Buffett explained the faster pace last year, saying that his children are all past normal retirement age and that he wants them to finish the work themselves. Of course, he added that the acceleration "in no way reflects any change in my views about Berkshire's prospects."

The foundations aren't built to sit on the stock. Buffett's 2006 pledge letter to Howard's foundation, for example, said there was no need to build large reserves, because new gifts would come every year. The Susan Thompson Buffett Foundation, the biggest recipient, paid around $1.5 billion in grants in 2025 alone.

It's fair to expect most of Buffett's remaining stake to be sold over time. Spread evenly through 2034, around $162 billion comes to about $20 billion of stock a year. That might seem like a lot. But Berkshire bought back around $14.4 billion of its own stock through September, including about $9.6 billion in the third quarter. Monday's 9.2 million shares also equal only about two days of normal trading in the Class B stock, based on its average volume over the last three months.

True, the buybacks might slow. Berkshire only repurchases shares when its CEO thinks the price is under the company's intrinsic value (an estimate of what the whole business is worth). And the stock is now near a record. But selling spread across gifts planned and announced years ahead arguably isn't likely to move the stock much by itself.

The price matters more. Berkshire's third-quarter operating earnings (which strip out the swings in its stock portfolio that hit net income) dropped about 6% year over year.

The largest drag was insurance underwriting, where profit slid about 43%.

But the stock now trades at about 27 times operating earnings over the past four quarters, up from about 22 times operating earnings at the end of September.

Ultimately, I don't see anything in Monday's gift or letter that should worry a Berkshire shareholder. For a new buyer, though, the record price is the bigger issue. I still think Berkshire is worth a look for the long run, but I'd be more comfortable paying under $593 for it.
