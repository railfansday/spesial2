Stock-Split Watch: 3 Industrial Stocks That Could Split in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

These three high-priced industrial stocks could be credible 2027 split candidates.

By Todd Shriber – Nov 26, 2026 at 11:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than W.W. Grainger ›

Some investors, particularly those new to the game, like the idea of forward stock splits (the good kind of split) because they're getting more shares of the stock in question.

In reality, forward stock splits don't alter a company's overall market value. That corporate action merely lowers the share price to make it more accommodating to a broader swath of market participants.

As nice as it is when companies do that, the frequency with which corporations are engaging in forward splits has been declining since the early 1980s. Blame the advent of fractional shares and the increased catering to institutional investors, who aren't price-sensitive.

Stock splits aren’t a sure thing, but these three industrial stocks could make the move in 2027. Image source: Getty Images.

In the first half of this year, fewer than 40 companies engaged in forward share splits. But hope always burns on this front, and there's nothing like four-figure or high triple-digit price tags to stoke the flames of a stock split.

So, with that in mind, Deere (DE +4.44%), Transdigm Group (TDG +1.90%), and W.W. Grainger (GWW +1.28%) are three industrial stocks that may be primed for share splits in 2027.

Among credible split candidates, W.W. Grainger isn't the most glamorous or most popular name, but it warrants a place in the split conversation. For example, the stock's Monday, Nov. 23 closing price of $1,390.12 is the second-highest among all industrial stocks, trailing only conglomerate Seaboard.

Likely more instructive than that is the fact that Grainger has a history of splitting. It has undergone five 2-for-1 splits since 1970. The rub is that the last one occurred in June 1998, suggesting this industrial parts supplier may not be prioritizing share splits and that 2027 may come and go without one.

With or without a move to lower its share price to woo more retail investors, Grainger offers other sources of appeal. Those include wide-moat positioning in the industrial and factory products supply space and its status as a Dividend King, or one of the companies that have raised dividends for at least 50 consecutive years.

Like Grainger, Deere, one of the largest industrial companies, at least has a history of stock splits. The agricultural machinery giant has done so four times since 1972, though its last split occurred in December 2007.

Deere's number of splits is comparable to that of other industrial companies. Likewise, these moves are a reminder of the value of long-term investing because market participants who bought just one share of Deere before the September 1972 split would have 24 shares today.

To be sure, there is no fixed price at which a company considers or is obligated to split its shares. But with a Monday, Nov. 23 closing print of $633.03, Deere is merely the 11th-most expensive industrial stock in dollar terms.

That doesn't mean Deere won't announce a forward split next year. The company may look to broaden its investor base, boost liquidity, or signal confidence in its growth projections. Still, ultimately, split hunters need to consider Deere a wait-and-see proposition.

Just as there is no fixed price at which companies are more likely to mull splits, there is no such thing as an "overdue" split, and that wisdom is worth remembering as it relates to Transdigm. The purveyor of components and parts found on nearly every commercial and military aircraft worldwide, Transdigm celebrated its 20th anniversary as a public company in March. Over those two-plus decades, it's never split its shares.

That brings us to today, well, Monday, Nov. 23, when the stock closed at $1,158.65 -- a price exceeded by just three other industrial stocks. Given its lack of split history, it's possible that Transdigm isn't actively evaluating a split to make its share price more accommodating.

On the other hand, splits have to start somewhere, but that thesis is rooted in a lot of hope and not much else.

Looking ahead to 2027, if investors are going to bet on anything with Transdigm, the safe wager is that the company will remain acquisitive. Estimates vary, but Transdigm has made dozens upon dozens of acquisitions since its founding in 1993, and CEO Michael Lisman recently said the company has "substantial M&A firepower...in excess of $10 billion."
