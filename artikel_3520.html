6 Near-Perfect Prime Video Miniseries With 8 Episodes or Less

Miniseries have to tell a complete story without wasting a single minute. That's easier said than done, because so many of them lose their way by stretching a two-hour idea across 10 episodes. However, Prime Video seems to have perfected the art of short-term storytelling because, over the years, the platform has amassed a pretty impressive collection of endlessly entertaining and brilliantly written miniseries.

These titles know exactly how much story they have to tell, and they trust the audience enough to leave out the filler. With that said, eight episodes or less seems to be the sweet spot, with enough time to develop compelling characters and deliver a satisfying story, but not so much that the plot starts going in circles. These six near-perfect Prime Video miniseries prove exactly why that is.

The Pursuit of Love, based on Nancy Mitford's 1945 novel, is one of the most charming period dramas in recent memory. The three-part BBC series, which streams on Prime Video in the U.S., follows cousins Linda Radlett (Lily James) and Fanny Logan (Emily Beecham), who grow up together in an eccentric aristocratic family in England between the two World Wars. The two young women are exact opposites of each other, as Linda is a hopeless romantic, while Fanny wants nothing to do with love. As they grow older, Linda throws herself into one dramatic romance after another, and their lives take very different paths.

Emily Mortimer, who wrote and directed the series, gives the story a playful, modern energy that stays true to the time the narrative is set in without ever feeling dated. The show’s modern soundtrack and quirky dialogue make it feel fresh, but the real heart of the story is the evolving friendship between Linda and Fanny. At just three episodes, The Pursuit of Love proves that period dramas can be fun, breezy, and bittersweet all at once. For anyone looking to ease into the genre, this show is the perfect watch.

The English is a Western unlike anything else on streaming. The six-episode series is set in 1890 and follows Lady Cornelia Locke (Emily Blunt), an English aristocrat who travels to the American Midwest to take revenge on the man she blames for her son's death. Along the way, she crosses paths with Eli Whipp (Chaske Spencer), a Pawnee ex-cavalry scout on his way to claim land he was promised for his service. The two form an unlikely partnership as they make their way across a violent, lawless frontier.

For starters, The English looks absolutely stunning. Every wide shot of the open plains feels like a painting. What makes it special, though, is how honestly it looks at the brutal history of the American West, especially the treatment of Native Americans. Creator Hugo Blick isn't afraid to let a scene breathe, which means that the show feels slow sometimes, but the choice to slow down is always intentional. The English is both brutal and beautiful, and much of the show’s charm has to do with Blunt and Spencer’s tender chemistry. That is also what makes the final episodes of the show so devastating. The English easily earns its place among the best modern Westerns thanks to its great storytelling and a fresh take on the genre.

Four icons and four completely different ways of walking into trouble. This is the one you would have been out there, why it fits, and what it would have cost you.

You have never explained yourself and you have never needed to, because people work out fairly quickly that you are the most capable person in the room without you ever having to mention it.

You are the funniest person in most rooms and the most loyal person in all of them, and you would be genuinely insulted if somebody suggested those two things were in any tension.

You never wanted this job, and you took it anyway because the alternative was standing there watching somebody less careful do it badly, which is the most Western reason there has ever been.

Nobody has ever successfully talked you out of something you had already decided was right, and a long line of grown adults has found that out the hard way.

The Girlfriend is addictive and easily the most fun Prime Video has ever had with a thriller miniseries. The story follows Laura (Robin Wright), who lives the perfect life and dotes on her son Daniel (Laurie Davidson). But things start to fall apart when Daniel brings home his new girlfriend, Cherry (Olivia Cooke). Now, Laura is convinced that Cherry is a manipulative social climber, while Cherry sees Laura as an overbearing mother who can't let go. The six-episode series turns this central rivalry into a tense game of cat and mouse, where the audience never knows who to trust.

The brilliance of The Girlfriend is that each episode shows the same events from both Laura's and Cherry's points of view. It’s hard to know which version to trust, which adds unbearable weight to even the simplest of conversations. Wright and Cooke perfectly play off of each other’s energy; watching their characters try to outsmart each other keeps the show engaging until the very end. The series is full of unexpected twists and shocking revelations, with each one completely shifting the audience’s understanding of the truth. This Prime Video series is six episodes of pure jealousy and paranoia, perfect for anyone looking for the ultimate guilty-pleasure binge.

Dead Ringers gives David Cronenberg's 1988 cult classic a bold, modern makeover. Rachel Weisz plays identical twins Elliot and Beverly Mantle, two brilliant gynecologists who share everything, including their lovers. Beverly dreams of opening a birthing center that will change how women give birth, and Elliot is willing to push every medical and ethical boundary to make that dream happen.

Weisz delivers a jaw-dropping double performance. She gives each sister her own body language and energy, so viewers always know exactly which twin is on screen. Created by Alice Birch, the series uses its twisted premise to take a sharp look at how the medical industry treats women, and it isn't afraid to get uncomfortable. On the other hand, it's also darkly funny and strangely moving, especially when it explores how much the twins need each other. Dead Ringers is unsettling in the best way, and Weisz's work here ranks among the best of her career.

Tales From the Loop is one of the most beautiful sci-fi shows ever made, and also one of the most underrated. The eight-episode series is inspired by Simon Stålenhag's paintings and set in a small Ohio town built above the Loop, an underground facility designed to explore the mysteries of the universe. Each episode focuses on a different resident whose life is somehow altered by strange technology. The stories stand on their own, but slowly connect to paint a full picture of this mysterious town. While the sci-fi genre typically thrives on spectacle, Tales From the Loop takes the exact opposite approach. It uses the genre’s conventions to tell intimate, human stories about growing up and growing old.

There are giant rusted robots in snowy fields and strange machines in the woods, but the show treats them as a normal part of small-town life. The show genuinely cares about the people at the center of its sprawling narrative instead of the technology. Its slow pace is definitely not for everyone, but the gorgeous visuals and gentle score alone make every single episode worth several watches. Tales From the Loop is a hidden gem that is bound to stay with the audience long after the credits roll. It makes one think not just about how quickly technology is advancing, but what it means to be human in a world where the line between the natural and the artificial is becoming increasingly blurred.

Swarm is the boldest and perhaps the most unsettling show on this list. The seven-episode series follows Dre (Dominique Fishback), a lonely young woman whose entire life revolves around her obsession with pop superstar Ni'Jah (Nirine S. Brown). Her foster sister Marissa (Chloe Bailey) is the only meaningful relationship in her life. However, when she takes her own life following a painful breakup, Dre loses the only person who truly understands her. Her grief soon twists into something far more disturbing, as she begins hunting down people who criticize Ni’Jah online.

The show provides eye-opening commentary on stan culture and feels comfortably close to reality. Fishback plays the lead character with frightening unpredictability. Dre is terrifying, yet it’s easy to empathize with her, which is the show’s primary conflict. The show occasionally gets carried away with its own weirdness, but that's also what makes it so memorable.
