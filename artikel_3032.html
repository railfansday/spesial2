Lightwave Logic (LWLG) Q3 2026 Earnings Call Transcript

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Foundry delays push volume production to 2028 despite a Foxconn milestone.

Need a quote from a Motley Fool analyst? Email [email protected]

Lightwave Logic, Inc. (LWLG -10.59%) reported a revised timeline for volume production while achieving system-level validation of its technology through a demonstration with Foxconn. Management indicated that the transition from initial demonstrations to commercial scaling is currently influenced by extended foundry production cycles and process qualification requirements. The company is focusing its resources on enabling its ecosystem of five major customers and four foundry partners to integrate electro-optic polymers into silicon photonics platforms for artificial intelligence infrastructure. Financial priorities remain centered on maintaining liquidity through the development phase as the company prepares its Denver facility and negotiates manufacturing partnerships for higher-volume requirements.

Yves LeMaitre: Thank you, Nick, and good afternoon, everyone. Before turning to our quarterly progress, I want to put our work at Lightwave Logic in the context of a broader industry shift. One of the main themes of our previous calls has been the convergence of photonics and semiconductors, and recent developments show that convergence is accelerating. As AI systems grow, their performance increasingly depends on the ability to move data through optical connections with greater bandwidth, lower power consumption and a smaller footprint. This is bringing photonic materials closer to the heart of semiconductor design, enabling new architecture for scale-out and scale-up connectivity among AI accelerators, processors and switches.

Future scaling architectures will bring optical connectivity even closer to the compute, requiring tighter integration of photonic materials and advanced packaging technologies. Leading semiconductor design and manufacturing companies are incorporating optical technologies and materials into their road maps. Marvell's recently announced agreement to acquire Lumentum underscores this direction with plans to bring semiconductor and optical capabilities under one roof to address these emerging requirements. Our recently announced collaboration with Hon Hai Technology Group, otherwise known as Foxconn, incorporating our electro-optic polymers into a scale-out optical transceiver is a concrete example of our role in this transition.

Foxconn plays a critical role in the semiconductor ecosystem by integrating advanced chips into complex electronic systems and providing the manufacturing scale to bring those systems into high-volume production. We believe this industry shift creates a compelling opportunity for Lightwave Logic. Our proprietary materials combine high-speed performance and power efficiency with compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing processes, positioning us to help customers integrate advanced optical capabilities into silicon photonics platforms. During the third quarter, we continued to make progress advancing Lightwave Logic's electro-optic polymer technology towards commercial qualification and ultimately volume production.

Our efforts remain focused on working closely with customers and foundry partners to establish the technical performance, reliability and manufacturing processes necessary to bring our technology into commercial products. In October, we achieved an important system-level validation milestone through the public demonstration of our technology within an optical transceiver developed by Foxconn, and I will cover this project in more detail during this call. On the manufacturing side, we continue to invest and expand our Perkinamine production infrastructure in Denver, Colorado. To ramp capacity of our back-end of line process, we are in advanced negotiation with a leading specialist in wafer-level optical manufacturing whose material processing and encapsulation capabilities can help scale silicon organic hybrid solutions for AI.

I'll discuss these developments today, along with our updated expectations for the timing of volume production and the priorities guiding our efforts as we move closer to 2027. So let me begin with customers. On October 29, we announced that Foxconn will demonstrate an optical transceiver, incorporating a silicon organic hybrid optical engine enabled by Lightwave Logic's electro-optic polymer technology. The demonstration at Hon Hai Tech Day in Taipei marked an important milestone for Lightwave Logic and meaningful validation of our progress with Foxconn. What makes this demonstration significant is that it goes beyond the performance of individual materials or devices.

It shows our technology operating within a functional optical engine and transceiver designed to address the high-speed, low-power requirements of next-generation AI networks. It provides tangible system-level evidence of how our polymer platform can be incorporated into customer products, and I want to recognize the teams whose collaborative efforts made this milestone possible. This project illustrates the work that takes place during Stage 3 of our design win process. We began working with Foxconn in the first half of 2025 and have now demonstrated the first iteration of an optical engine capable of supporting 200 gigabits per second per lane. We also want to be clear about what this milestone means.

A functioning transceiver demonstration is an important step towards product qualification. Further chip design iterations, integration, reliability testing, manufacturing preparation and customer-specific qualification activities are required before a program can move into volume production or Stage 4 of our design win process. We consider Stage 4 reached when our customers' product is fully qualified, accepted by the end user and ready to be manufactured and shipped in volume with consistently high yields. Every Stage 3 program must progress through a rigorous development and qualification process. Let me explain that in more detail because it is a recurring question from shareholders.

The sequence varies by customer and application, but typically begins with alpha devices or early optical engine prototypes integrated into test platforms or evaluation boards. The next step is to design and produce beta devices that meet the target specification for integration into the customer's transceiver. Once the silicon photonics chips have been fabricated and the necessary back-end processing completed, testing takes place at both the chip and assembled transceiver levels. This covers optical and electrical performance, mechanical and thermal requirements and other application-specific criteria. At the transceiver level, it also includes software functionality wherever applicable. Comprehensive reliability testing will also be completed on both the photonic chip and the finished product.

This design and qualification process assumes that in the background, the silicon foundries have qualified their own equipment and processes and have achieved predictable yields. This is usually announced to the external customers with a new PDK release, including the steps necessary to produce SOH modulators. In parallel, our customers' new product introduction or NPI teams prepare the optical engine and transceiver manufacturing line for volume production. Equipment must be installed and qualified operators trained and assembly processes and test systems validated to achieve the required throughput, consistency and yields. The final critical step is end user qualification.

Sample products are delivered to the end user, which may be a hyperscaler or an AI infrastructure supplier for testing within its own systems. Those customers must confirm compatibility, performance and reliability in their intended operating environment. Once these requirements and the manufacturing readiness criteria have been met, the product can move into commercial volume shipments. In addition to Foxconn and Marvell, which we have previously disclosed, we have 3 other Fortune Global 500 customers in Stage 3. Each program is progressing through its own development and qualification requirements, and our team is focused on helping these customers advance towards Stage 4.

Ultimately, Stage 4 is where the necessary technical and qualification criteria have been satisfied, commercial terms have been agreed upon and programs can move towards volume production. Now let me turn to our silicon photonics foundry activities. On our second quarter call, we reported 3 dedicated foundry runs underway with 2 wafer sets expected in August and a third foundry expected to deliver wafers in the fourth quarter and a dedicated run with our fourth partner progressing towards tape-out. As a reminder, our active foundry ecosystem includes GlobalFoundries, Tower Semiconductor, SilTerra and the fourth partner that remains unnamed. I'm pleased to report that we received wafers from 2 of our foundry partners during the third quarter.

Following receipt, our engineering teams performed the necessary back-end processing, including depositing and poling our electro-optic polymer encapsulating the devices, preparing them for testing and completing characterization. Chips received on an expedited schedule from one of our foundry partners showed promising results and were used in the transceiver demonstration at Hon Hai Tech Day. We expect to receive additional wafers from that foundry by the end of 2026. Our customer currently plans to launch another run in early 2027, targeting the final product configuration. Our second foundry partner ran into process issues at one of their facilities, which translated into a limited number of usable chips.

This foundry partner is expected to deliver 2 new sets of high-performance chips from 2 different manufacturing locations during the first half of 2027. We expect wafers from our third foundry partner in December 2026, while the wafer run with our fourth foundry partner is tentatively scheduled for the first half of 2027. These programs are progressing against a backdrop of strong demand and significant pressure on the optical supply chain. Shortages of indium phosphide lasers, particularly electro-absorption modulated lasers, or EMLs, are accelerating interest in silicon photonics architectures that use continuous wave, or CW lasers. CW lasers are simpler devices than EMLs, easier to produce, and these architectures typically use fewer lasers.

We believe this shift towards silicon photonics further validates the opportunity for electro-optic polymers, which can be integrated into silicon photonics to provide higher performance modulation. At the same time, this demand creates near-term challenges for silicon photonics foundries. Our foundry partners are prioritizing the immediate need for additional silicon photonics production capacity, which can affect the resources and schedules available to qualify new materials and processes. They are also managing the need for additional capacity investment and expansion. These pressures have extended our development time lines and continue to affect our schedule. We remain closely engaged with each partner to advance our programs and appreciate their support under these demanding conditions.

The value of this work extends beyond individual foundry runs. Each completed run provides information that helps us refine the device performance, improve manufacturing yield and achieve greater consistency in subsequent runs. We also incorporate these findings into our process design kit, or PDK, to support future customer designs and foundry integrations. Although our foundry programs are at different stages of maturity, progress across this ecosystem continues to strengthen the manufacturing foundation needed to scale our technology. I also want to address what these time lines mean for our expectations for volume production. On our second quarter call, we discussed the goal of beginning volume production in the second half of 2027.

We now expect volume production to begin in 2028, reflecting longer foundry wafer production schedules and process qualification cycles alongside the remaining customer development and product qualification work. Throughout 2027, our focus will be on completing these activities and helping our customers advance towards Stage 4. We will continue to report meaningful technical, manufacturing and commercial milestones as they are achieved. With that, I will turn the call over to Fred to review our third quarter financial results and financial priorities.

Nick Teves: Thank you, Fred. We received a number of questions from investors in advance of today's call. We've consolidated those submissions around the topics raised most frequently, and we'll now address several of those questions. Our first question is, on the second quarter call, management discussed beginning volume production in the second half of 2027. What specifically has changed in your assessment of the commercialization time line? And what key technical manufacturing and customer qualification milestones should investors expect during 2027 as you work toward volume production in 2028?

Yves LeMaitre: The main challenge we have been facing in 2026 is a slower pace of silicon photonics foundries, both in terms of production cycles and PDK process qualification. So today, this is our main hurdle to speed up the introduction of silicon organic hybrid modulators incorporating our materials. Obviously, together with our customers, we also have to execute flawlessly all of the design and qualification activities I described earlier. At this time, our engineering team is 100% focused on accelerating the enablement of our silicon foundry partners, both for front-end and back-end, as well as supporting the transition to commercial revenue of our 5 lead customers.

Regarding the production of our Perkinamine in our Denver facility, I'm pleased with the progress we have made over the last few quarters.

Nick Teves: With the pressure on AI infrastructure stocks and questions on CapEx funding, is your revised timing for production volume tied to a slowdown in R&D investment for next-gen 400G solutions?

Yves LeMaitre: No, not at all. All of our programs continue with the same intensity and our customers continue to invest into a path towards 400G per lane in all types of applications from scale-across to scale-up and potentially scaling in the future. We are also seeing an increase in activities beyond 400G per lane, especially in the field of POH or plasmonic organic hybrid solution.

Nick Teves: What were the principal findings from recent foundry wafer testing and characterization, particularly regarding device performance, repeatability and manufacturing yield? Beyond the foundry runs themselves, what additional progress is needed to scale the back-end processing and manufacturing capabilities required for commercial production?

Yves LeMaitre: That is a good question. On the back end of line for polymer processing onto the silicon photonics chip, this is a big area of focus with our customers on reaching wafer scale back end of line with a path towards both high volume and larger 300-millimeter wafers. As you know, this activity currently resides in Denver, but our plan is to outsource it to a partner specializing in wafer testing and material processing. As I mentioned earlier, we are actively negotiating an agreement with that company and kicked off the technical project a few months ago.

Nick Teves: What is the status of the second material supply and licensing agreement previously discussed by management? And are there specific technical qualification or commercial milestones that must be achieved before an agreement can be finalized?

Fred Graffam: Yes. Thanks for the question. On the second material supply and licensing agreement, all I can say at this time is that it's an ongoing and active negotiation. This is obviously a sensitive and confidential matter protected by nondisclosure agreements. And unfortunately, I'm not in a position to share more at this time.

Nick Teves: With volume production now expected to commence in 2028, how does the revised time line affect the company's anticipated investment requirements and capital allocation priorities? And how does the current liquidity position support the commercialization plan?

Fred Graffam: As I noted previously, our liquidity position provides significant financial flexibility with sufficient cash to execute our business plan into 2028. Importantly, the revised timing for volume production does not fundamentally change how we think about capital allocation. We will continue to actively manage our liquidity to ensure that we have the resources necessary to support our customers and execute our commercialization plan while remaining disciplined about the timing of our investments. In particular, we will ensure that the timing and level of our investments will be aligned with the expected business requirements and the benefits we expect those investments to generate.

Nick Teves: Are both 200G per lane and 400G per lane solutions active development priorities? And what additional work is required to commercialize 400G per lane?

Yves LeMaitre: Yes, both are active development priorities. We view 200G per lane as a stepping stone for 400G per lane with most of the technical activities overlapping. Obviously, 400G per lane requires more demanding performance specifications, particularly in terms of bandwidth, but the programs are very similar. What is different is what happens beyond 400G per lane. This is where new solutions might be required beyond SOH and where plasmonics might play an important role. Our CTO and advanced technology group is leading these activities.
