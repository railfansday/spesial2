Misery Index: Kyle Whittingham's sad story at Michigan just got worse

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Lore is a powerful concept in sports, and over more than two decades, it did a number on our perception of Kyle Whittingham.

He was the guy who hadn't missed a workout in 100 years. He was the guy whose teams overachieved by playing hard-nosed, physical football that was not aesthetically pleasing but often effective. He was the guy who found future NFL players in places nobody else looked and won for so long at Utah that you had to wonder what he might be capable of at one of the sport's blue-blood jobs.

Most of all, he was intense because how else could Whittingham do all that at Utah for so many years without being a bit of a maniac?

So when the opportunity came to coach at Michigan in his mid-60s, it seemed like a natural fit. What was more in the Whittingham wheelhouse than Big Ten football, gray November days in Ann Arbor and a fan base that doesn't mind if you win ugly as long as you win big?

It fit the stereotype and the lore. It hasn't fit the reality.

There is something missing from the Whittingham era at Michigan – well, there's a lot missing, to be honest, including wins – but the most glaring characteristic of his 10 games in charge is a decided lack of urgency and intensity. It's to the point where you almost have to ask whether this is even the same guy we watched pull off some amazing feats at Utah over the years.

If Whittingham is one-and-done at Michigan, which must be on the table at this point after a 31-7 loss to Oregon, it will not just be about the record. Yes, being 5-5 at this point in the season is a disappointment for a program that aspires to compete for championships. But just as problematic are a series of things he's said that suggest Whittingham doesn't quite understand what he signed up for.

The reaction to his preseason comment about not hating Ohio State was overwrought, but then he also downplayed the Michigan State rivalry game (which Michigan also lost, 15-6). Nobody would expect Whittingham to understand every single nuance of Michigan football, but when you become the head coach of a team, part of the job is understanding what the customers (i.e., the fans) care about. Having a sense of the rivalries is a pretty big one!

But every time Whittingham steps in front of a microphone, he comes across as if the Michigan job is a timeshare rental property that he doesn't intend to ever buy. He enjoys spending time at the house and wants to take care of it, but eventually he's going to go home to the plays he owns. Whether it's the rivalry issue or dragging his feet on making offensive staff changes, even after several weeks of bad results, Whittingham does not convey the same urgency that most Michigan fans feel.

Where is that understated intensity that sometimes bubbled into rage? Where is the no-excuse coach who made you feel like he could bench press as much as his linebackers?

Kyle Whittingham of the Michigan Wolverines looks on during the first half of a college football game against the Michigan State Spartans (Aaron J. Thornton/Getty Images)

That's the guy Michigan thought it hired, but he hasn't shown up on the job. Instead, Michigan has gotten the grandfather-philosopher version of Whittingham whose general serenity is not matching the nature of the results. If anything, the team has taken adopted his aura of listlessness.

It's tricky to talk about age because 66 is not that old, and plenty of people in Whittingham's age group are doing some of their best work – including Indiana's Curt Cignetti, who is 65. But something has changed at this stage of Whittingham's career, and Utah saw it too. Remember, Whittingham did not take the Michigan job until Utah had already nudged him out the door, which they would not have done without feeling confident Morgan Scalley would take the program to an even higher level. Obviously, they were right. Something about Whittingham's approach is not in touch with the current era.

Now Michigan is paying the price for misreading the situation, but there's another complication to what might be a straightforward decision. The school hasn't hired an athletic director yet, meaning any coaching decision would be driven by its highly political and sometimes messy Board of Regents. That's a recipe for disaster.

That puts Michigan in serious limbo until an AD is named, and time is running short before the coaching carousel really starts moving. Having such a problematic situation without a clear path out makes Michigan the most miserable fan base in America for Week 11.

ACC: It's hard to find anything new to say about the sadness of Clemson football. The era of winning big under Dabo Swinney is functionally over, and has been over for a while now, because the same things keep happening without much recognition from the head coach that the program is in a multi-year nosedive. But now, after a 20-6 loss at home to Georgia Tech — the same Georgia Tech that was 2-7 coming into the game and fired four assistant coaches last week — we might have finally reached a nadir the university can no longer abide. If you think Clemson can't tell the greatest coach in school history, a future Hall of Famer and a two-time national champion that his services are no longer needed, a two-touchdown home loss to the ACC's last place team might have a way of forcing some honest conversations. Yes, Clemson had injuries, including to quarterback Tait Reynolds early in the game. But giving up 269 rushing yards to Georgia Tech is an indictment of the entire operation and demands accountability. Swinney's $57 million buyout (without a mitigation clause if he takes another job) is ridiculous and perhaps prohibitive. A forced separation would be painful and ugly. But so is a 2-9 record in Swinney's last 11 home games against power conference opponents.

Big 12: If you're in the administration at Florida State and talking yourself into Jimbo Fisher as the new/old head coach, it might be useful to look at a recent example of a school reaching into its past. At West Virginia, the second go-round with Rich Rodriguez simply isn't working. If anything, it's worse than ever after a 37-14 loss at home to Kansas. This is what happens when boosters who don't know anything about football beyond their nostalgia clamor for a coach who makes them feel the warm fuzzies they remember from the good old days. Rich Rod wasn't just a good coach at West Virginia from 2001-07, he was a great one — losing just five games in his final three seasons and finishing No. 5, No. 10 and No. 11 in the final AP polls. That was enough to get offered both the Alabama job and the Michigan job during that stretch, the latter of which he accepted. But trying to recreate a moment in time like that so many years later is usually a recipe for disaster. There's a reason why rock bands go from playing big stadiums at the height of their popularity to off-brand casinos at the end. The world constantly changes and moves on, and West Virginia isn't the same job it was 20 years ago in a league, the Big East, that no longer exists in football. This loss to Kansas feels like the moment when everyone realizes the past isn't coming back. At 5-5, the Mountaineers really, really needed this game to get bowl eligible with Houston and Utah to finish the season. Instead, they laid a complete egg — and it wasn't just about their awful defense, which has given up an average of 495 yards per game. The offense wasn't so hot either this time, accounting for just 262 yards. That's an all-around disgraceful effort in a very winnable matchup, and it may be time for West Virginia to think hard about where Rich Rod, The Sequel, goes from here.

Lane Kiffin of the LSU Tigers on the sideline. (Derick Hingle/Icon Sportswire via Getty Images)

SEC: It's official now that LSU's season is a $100 million bust. Does that mean it was a mistake to move heaven and earth to get Lane Kiffin to Baton Rouge, only for him to deliver this four-loss dud in Year 1? Not necessarily. Kiffin's stock has taken a beating this year, no doubt, but you can make a pretty good case that in his rush to ingratiate himself into the LSU machine, he accepted things that may not have been the best fit. Like keeping defensive coordinator Blake Baker. Or getting a quarterback with Sam Leavitt's particular skillset out of the transfer portal. Let's face it, we know a Lane Kiffin team when we see one — and this ain't it. This is not how Kiffin wants to play football, but he's working his way through it. That should end now. Lane needs to get back to being Lane — social media and all — and that will hopefully begin after the heartbreak of a last-second, 22-21 loss to Texas. After whiffing on both monster opportunities at home against Alabama and Texas, LSU is out of the playoff mix. Given the drama they caused with their bevy of local judges and bloviating politicians, there are celebrations around the SEC. Hopefully this unleashes Kiffin, because what is the point going from Ole Miss to LSU if he's too intimidated by the place to be himself?

Group of Six: New Mexico's fan base understands more than anyone how fleeting moments of success can be. Particularly in this era, fan bases understand that successful coaches will get lured away to the power conferences and continuing to win will depend on a game of coaching roulette. So nobody at New Mexico is going to be mad when Jason Eck gets the kind of job offer he can't turn down, most likely at the end of this month. What they'll be mad about, or at least a little sad about, is that they didn't fully maximize this run with a CFP appearance. That's off the table now for New Mexico after a pretty shocking 21-17 loss to UNLV. Though the Rebels were the preseason favorites to win the Mountain West, they'd fumbled their way to double digit underdog status by the time this game kicked off. Now, the Lobos are almost in must-win territory next week at Wyoming if they want to win the conference title.

Luke Fickell: Just when you thought you were off the hot seat, some really bad football pulls you right back in. On Oct. 24, Wisconsin was 5-2 after a big home win over Southern Cal. It looked like the administration's decision to give Fickell another year and more resources to build his roster was paying off. Some were even whispering about the Badgers being a sneaky playoff contender. Then on Halloween, they went to Iowa and got thumped. No big deal, right? Iowa is really good. But now, after a 24-21 loss to Maryland (against a backup quarterback no less), the entire picture looks different. At 6-4, with some very winnable games left against Purdue and Minnesota, Wisconsin's improvement this season is undeniable. But you can't lose to a previously 3-6 Maryland team that fired Mike Locksley weeks ago and not expect a lot of heat. Coming off two losing seasons, can Wisconsin really make a coaching change if Fickell goes 8-4 in his prove-it year? Probably not. But if it's 6-6 or even 7-5, who knows. Losing to Maryland could make all the difference in a multi-million dollar decision.

Lincoln Riley: After Jayden Maiava threw a crushing interception near the gold line with about 6 ½ minutes remaining at Indiana, CBS cameras panned over to Riley, who was sort of bent over in frustration on the sideline. It was understandable. Down 20-13, that play was USC's shot to tie a game they'd basically played on even terms. In some ways, it was USC's last, best shot at getting into the playoff picture. Instead, like most of USC's other big, defining moments during Riley's five seasons, it was a reminder that the Trojans have not come far enough to do what he was hired to do. The painful part for Riley is that both this game and the loss to Oregon back in September were there for the taking. Had USC won either of them, they'd have had enough margin for error to lay an egg at Wisconsin and not get penalized too harshly. Whether it was that mistake by Maiava or the missed 27-yard field goal at the start of the fourth quarter, that entire 60 minutes illustrated why Indiana is so good at winning close games and USC is not. There is just a certain, difficult-to-define toughness and resolve that gets Indiana through those moments time and again. And at USC, there's an equally difficult-to-define Murphy's Law vibe where the exact wrong thing will happen predictably. With UCLA now 10-0 and closer to CFP-bound, we could be setting up for a last stand at the Rose Bowl where Riley can either win back hearts and minds by playing spoiler or perhaps offer a final verdict on his disappointing tenure.

Greg Schiano: The whole point of Rutgers pushing its way into the Big Ten was to eventually make enough money through its conference membership that it wouldn't have to settle for bad football. And yet here we are, a dozen years into being a Big Ten program, and Rutgers can't afford the $28 million it would take to get rid of a coaching staff that is 2-8 this year after a 31-0 loss at home to Nebraska. That was the essence of athletic director Keli Zinn's announcement earlier in the week that Schiano would return. Even though expectations have not been met and standards have not been lowered, Rutgers is giving one more year to a coach who is 16-45 in the Big Ten. What does that mean in tangible terms? Barring a course correction that is, frankly, hard to envision, we're going to do this all over again next year. Rutgers fans will spend an entire offseason mad at the administration. The atmosphere around the program will be toxic. And much like Florida State this year with Mike Norvell, the season will be a long march of paper bags over heads, booing at lineup introductions and airplanes with messages flying overhead. Nobody ever wants to spend $28 million on a fired coaching staff, but if all that comes to pass, it'll seem like a bargain.

James Franklin: We may need Pablo Torre to investigate what the heck happened during Virginia Tech's bye week. After winning at Clemson on Oct. 24, the Hokies were 6-2 and in good position to have the kind of Year 1 under Franklin that would really push the program forward and immediately justify the big financial investments it took to get him in Blacksburg. Then came the bye week, and all Virginia Tech has done since then is stink up the ACC. Last weekend, the Hokies fell behind SMU 44-14 before making the score look a little better in a 51-28 loss. This week, the same thing happened at home against Stanford. After starting the game with three defensive stops, Virginia Tech gave up three touchdowns in a row and fell hopelessly behind before finally losing 40-22. Make no mistake, Virginia Tech was mediocre before the bye week too. But this collapse is going to deflate a fan base that had been all-in on the Franklin plan since he became available last October.

A&M's offense hit a new low: It has been ugly in Aggieland all season, but when you put this offense on the field against a great defense like Tennessee's, it turns into a tragicomedy. In a 22-7 loss at home, Texas A&M managed just 179 yards of offense, one-third of which came on a 61-yard touchdown drive in the second quarter. Their other 11 drives averaged 11.7 yards with two turnovers. Quarterback Marcel Reed, who has had a truly awful and confounding season (He was pretty good last year!), completed 9-of-22 for 71 yards. Head coach Mike Elko snapped at a reporter this week for asking about his confidence in offensive coordinator Holmon Wiggins, calling it a "stupid question," but it's actually the only question that matters right now for the Aggies. At 4-6, with a road game at Oklahoma and a home game against Texas remaining, Elko is looking 4-8 square in the face just a year after making the CFP and signing a big contract extension. If the offense isn't significantly better next season, Elko will find himself on a smoldering hot seat. He knows it, too, calling the offense "inept" and "completely non-functional" after the game while emphasizing that "It's my fault. I own it."

Tosh isn't getting the message: There's been an interesting dynamic this season at Cal where first-year coach Tosh Lupoi continues to show that he's learning on the job (to put it kindly) and general manager Ron Rivera delivers strange but very candid on-the-record criticisms of the work he's doing. "He has bumped his head a lot," Rivera said on KNBR radio recently. "I mean, we've talked a lot, tried to give him a lot of advice, point him in the right direction. Some of it he's been very receptive to and other portions he hasn't." It's fair to wonder what Rivera thought as he watched Lupoi, down 9-0 at Virginia late in the first half, send out his team in a bizarre formation on fourth-and-goal and snap it to tight end Mason Mini, who subsequently threw an interception. Though it was just one of many bad moments in a 36-7 loss, Cal is now 3-7 and has to be wondering if it made a mistake. Lupoi has a lot of experience as a defensive coach and recruiter, but Rivera is basically comfortable admitting in public that Cal has a coaching problem. You have to give a first-year head coach room to grow and make mistakes, but you can't tolerate incompetence. We'll see soon where Cal thinks its program is on that delicate spectrum.
