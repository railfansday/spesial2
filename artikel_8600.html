Miller-Howard Q3 2026 Quarterly Report

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

DESPITE PERSISTENT INFLATION and rising interest rates, US markets continued to march higher during the quarter on... stop us if you've heard this one... the back of continued momentum in tech. For the last decade, equity markets have witnessed unprecedented outperformance from the tech-focused companies with the largest market capitalizations. These companies' dominant returns have created a flywheel in which an increasingly concentrated group of companies increasingly dominate major markets and market indices.

From a sector perspective, information technology (IT) is the largest allocation within the S&P 500 Index, comprising nearly 40% of the index and more than three times that of the second largest sector (financials). Over the last 10 years, IT's weight in the index has increased nearly two-fold from ~21% to the aforementioned 40%.

While the S&P 500 has become the poster child for market concentration, many other indices are also becoming heavily influenced by mega-cap tech. Counterintuitively, this has even led to indices that purport to provide value exposure being driven by the very same names (see Miller/Howard's 4Q25 QuarterlyâIs There Growth in Your Value?). The Russell 1000 Value Index is participating in the same growing concentration, with IT more than doubling over the last five years from ~9% to ~21% of the index.

As dominant as the IT sector has been, it is worth noting that these figures understate the true exposure to "tech." Companies such as Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon* are currently classified as communication services and consumer discretionary by the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS), despite owning AI and cloud infrastructure.

FTSE Russell periodically reconstitutes its indices, and these changes have heavily influenced the IT weighting within the Russell 1000 Value, particularly since June 2025. For the Russell 1000 Growth and Value indices, FTSE Russell evaluates the companies in the broad market Russell 1000 Index to determine where they lie along the investment style spectrum. FTSE Russell uses three metrics (one value- and two growth-oriented) to calculate a composite value score that reflects how strongly each company displays value and growth characteristics. The constituents are ranked and an algorithm is applied to determine style index membership weights. Since a continuous style-scoring system is used (versus a binary system), constituent weights can be divided and allocated to both growth and value indices.

At their most recent reconstitution in June 2026, the majority of Amazon's weight shifted out of the Russell 1000 Growth Index and into the Russell 1000 Value. Apple and Microsoft* transitioned from being exclusively growth allocations to being split between the growth and value benchmarks. Due to the companies' large market capitalizations, Amazon, Apple, and Microsoft became the top three index holdings, comprising over 15% of the Russell 1000 Value. After the reconstitution, the IT sector weight was over 800 basis points higher year-over-year (and, as we mentioned, this dramatic shift excludes Amazon since it is not in the IT sector).

A natural byproduct of highly concentrated positions outperforming is an increasingly narrow market. One simple way of monitoring market breadth is by comparing the returns on the market-cap-weighted S&P 500 to the returns of the equal-weighted S&P 500. When the market-cap weighted index outperforms, it typically means fewer names are advancing, indicating less market breadth.

Narrow market breadth has historically been considered indicative of late-stage market advances.

Early cycle is generally marked by broad advances as interest rates fall, earnings recover from depressed levels, and valuations are attractive. As the cycle progresses, conditions generally become less forgiving with rates beginning to rise and earnings beginning to slow. Market breadth generally narrows as only the strongest companies (those with superior balance sheets and growth profiles) are able to continue advancing. This can result in crowding and stretched valuations. This market structure is considered more vulnerable as returns are concentrated in a handful of names with higher expectations.

The market-cap-weighted S&P 500 has outperformed the equal-weighted S&P 500 in eight of the last 10 years, including a streak of four consecutive years. Over the last 10 years, the market-cap weighted index has outperformed by over 110%.** While narrow markets can persist for longer than expected, the last time the market-cap-weighted index outperformed by this magnitude was 1994-1999, when it outperformed by over 125%. Notably, or perhaps presciently, this period of outperformance was followed by the Dot-Com Crash and a 6-year period in which the equal-weighted index outperformed by over 50%.

With markets increasingly concentrated and all styles moving deeper into tech exposure, diversification has been harder to find, even among actively managed products. We believe a disciplined approach to high dividend yielding stocks provides one of the purer ways to diversify exposure to the market. Dividend investing creates a natural barrier to chasing growth as high and secure (well-covered) dividends are naturally incompatible with high price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios. It also creates a philosophical difference from companies that are often prioritizing aggressive investment over the return of capital to shareholders.

Even within the Russell 1000 Value Index, the largest sectors by weight have been poor hunting grounds for high dividend yielding stocks. At quarter end, the IT sector comprised over 20% of the value index, an amount greater than the four highest dividend paying sectors combined. What's more, of the 97 IT holdings, 57 did not pay a dividend at all, making the median yield within the sector 0.0%. Only six IT positions had a yield of over 2.5%, and this group made up a fairly inconsequential 1% of the index. Conversely, the real estate and utilities sectors had median yields of 4.3% and 3.5%, respectively, but made up less than 7% of the index. For many portfoliosâwhether active or passiveâincluding a high dividend yield approach can provides natural diversification in a market where both growth and value are piling into similar stocks and sectors.

The natural conclusion to this dynamic is high-yield dividend stocks (classified as the top 40% of dividend paying equities) now comprise a decade-low component of the Russell 1000 Value Index. Since 2016, high-yield dividend stocks have dropped from 49% of the market capitalization of the index to 23%.

LAST QUARTER, WE DISCUSSED THE inverse relationship between valuation and subsequent 10-year returns. The data showed that buying at lower valuations had historically led to higher future returns. Our Income-Equity portfolios, because of their emphasis on a high and well-covered dividend yield, will mathematically have a lower valuation than the broad market.

This discipline forces us to hold different stocks, in different weights, than our benchmark index. This differentiation can be measured by active share.*

Because of the portfolios' mandate (high current income, growth of income, and financial strength), the current active share for our Income-Equity (No MLPs) portfolio versus the Russell 1000 Value Index is 89%, meaning that 89% of our portfolio is different from the index. Looking at our portfolio against the S&P 500 Index tells a similar story, with an active share of 93%.

Why should investors care about active share? We recently met with a prospective client that had a "diversified" portfolio. They had investments in products focused on broad market US equities, US growth equities, US value equities, a global stock portfolio, and a couple of actively-managed formulaic products specializing in areas such as income, quality, and momentum. It all looked good on the surface, but we noticed a lot of similarities in the top 10 holdings across the portfolio components. The product names sounded diversified, but their low active share relative to each other revealed they had many large holdings in common. Their "diversified" equity approach was unintentionally contributing to further concentration in specific underlying stocks and industries.

*Active share compares a portfolio's positions and weights with that of an index and totals those differences (and divides them by two) to show just how different a portfolio is from an index. An active share of 100% means that the portfolio shares nothing in common with the index, while an active share of 0% would indicate a perfect index fund.

For investors who are concerned about the US stock market's concentration and about increasingly overlapping and overexposed equity positions, a portfolio with high active share can offer a differentiated source of returns that should help investors sleep a little more soundly at night. However, the potential benefits of diversification don't only apply in market environments like today. As described in a study by a Notre Dame professor and a Rutgers professor, "A necessary condition for long-term outperformance is that the actively-managed portfolio is substantially different than the benchmark."* In other words, closet indexers historically underperformed.

Our Income-Equity portfolios have consistently offered investors a high active shareâand continue to do so now. This is not because it is an explicit objective of our approach, but it is a result of our discipline and commitment to our clients' goals.

Our portfolios' weighted average earnings-per-share (EPS) growth over the next 12 months is expected to be +17.2%, somewhat less than the S&P 500's +20.4% consensus increase (measured by FactSet). Importantly though, our portfolio's projected growth comes without the index's AI spending related controversies, as AI spending is expected to drive about half of S&P 500's EPS growth (estimated by Goldman Sachs). Meanwhile, our portfolios' expected dividend growth over the next 12 months is about 6%, well below projected EPS growth, providing a margin of safety that could either result in higher financial strength, better dividend coverage, or faster dividend growthâeach of which would support our discipline of high current income, growth of income, and financial strength.

OUR MLP AND MIDSTREAM ENERGY INCOME portfolio outperformed its benchmark, but the midstream energy industry slightly trailed the broad market during the quarter. The industry lagged on a combination of lower crude prices (due to somewhat more normalized flows from the Middle East) and higher interest rates. Despite this, the earnings season provided a bright picture for the continuing health of the midstream industry, in our view.

A recurring theme was that demand for secure, reliable energy continues to grow, and international customers are increasingly turning to North America as a preferred supplier. There were several project announcements during the quarter in response to the call on North American energy. Midstream energy companies continue to raise EBITDA guidance for the year. Capex also continues to increase given the opportunity set of projects.

Coming out of the pandemic, the midstream industry was in harvest mode, rather than growth mode, with a focus on returning value to shareholders through distribution increases and share buybacks. However, industry dynamics started to change a few years ago, as emerging tailwindsâprimarily, but not exclusively, related to natural gasâbegan to emerge.

While we view this new step-change in growth as a positive development, it comes with certain challenges. Investors would not be blamed for questioning: Will midstream companies revert to being serial issuers of debt and equity, with no free cash flow? We do not believe this to be the case. Certainly, free cash flow has declined as capex has increased, given the amount of infrastructure that needs to be built to meet the rising consumption. However, the one key point that we believe is being overlooked is the increasing cash flow from operations. This is important, as those cash flows will be used, along with debt, to finance the additional midstream assets. Once the projects become fully operational, we believe cash flow should increase again, supporting high and rising income for investors.

INFRASTRUCTURE OUTPERFORMED ITS benchmark this quarter, but the sector was unable to keep pace with the broad market that continues to be driven by a concentrated group of tech companies. While AI-related growth remained a powerful tailwind for a narrow portion of the market, rising interest rates created a headwind for much of the market. Despite the pullback in the sector this quarter, the Infrastructure portfolio continues to materially outperform its benchmark year to date.

Within the portfolio, higher-yielding positions generally outperformed. Telecom followed this trend on strong earnings. Energy was mixed with refining and liquefied natural gas (LNG) exposure exhibiting strength while natural gas pipelines and Canadian-exposed names showed weakness. Utilities lagged as intensifying data center pushback and interest-rate sensitivity created headwinds for the group.

Data centers have changed the game, stressing existing systems and structures andâat timesâcreating some less-than-desirable outcomes.

For utility companies, key among the opportunities and risks is the impact on customer bills. Reports that data center development has driven customer bills higher AND lower correctly highlight the incredible complexity of the issue.

Miller/Howard supports initiatives that have data centers pay their own way, and we think there are real, attainable paths to accomplishing this objective. Investor sentiment was further weighed down by intensifying data center pushback over rising electric bills and environmental concerns. This led to additional moratoriums, a pause on data center interconnections in Texas, and ongoing legislative efforts across PJM* states to address bill pressures. As a result, investors grew concerned around the timing of data center load growth and utilities' ability to recover costs. Despite headwinds facing the utilities sector, our Utilities Plus portfolio outperformed its benchmark this quarter.

Within the group, water utilities and pure-play natural gas local distribution companies (LDCs) outperformed on lower exposure to the data center buildout, while regulated utilities underperformed given their higher data center exposure. Independent power producers continue to stall amid limited incremental data center deals, and California utilities performed the worst among the group as legislatures failed to meaningfully reduce wildfire liability for utilities.

OIL PRICES ENTERED THE THIRD QUARTER near their lowest levels since the war in Iran began, then rose as the quarter progressed, lifting energy stocks with them. While our North American Energy portfolio slightly trailed its benchmark during the quarter, it has outperformed its benchmark and significantly outperformed the broad market year-to-date. The biggest contributors during the quarter were the refiners, where the supply/demand balance has led to elevated crack spreads. Our natural gas-focused exploration and production (E&P) and midstream holdings were modest detractors.

For most of the past decade, North American rig count has run in one direction: down. August rig counts fell from 1,268 in 2018 to 712 in 2025âa near-halving that, taken at face value, would suggest an industry in retreat. But the headline numbers obscure what actually happened at the wellsite. Rigs became dramatically more productive. Operators pushed longer laterals, drilled faster, and cut non-productive time between wells, so each active rig now delivers a multiple of the footage and output versus history.

That makes the recent inflection all the more notable. After years of declines, monthly rig additions have turned decisively positive, and August 2026 counts stood 80 rigs above the prior year. The gains were broad-based: US oil (+41) and Canadian oil (+24) led, with Canadian gas (+9) and US gas (+6) rounding out the increase.

Producers do not invest in new rigs when they see a spike in commodity pricesâotherwise we would have seen rig-count increases in March when WTI first crossed $90/barrel in the early days of the war in Iran. Producers tend to invest when they are convinced that prices will stay higher-for-longerâdriven by a call on North American energy.
