Customer financing/investment is not inherently a red flag. Ford, Caterpillar, Boeing and Intel provide counter examples. The real question is whether Nvidia is enabling economically sound customers to finance productive assets, or whether its capital is artificially supporting demand for its own GPUs.
If Caterpillar was investing its money in raw startups that ordered Caterpillar tractors, but had, as yet, no business for which to use those tractors ....

Nvidia is ramping its own chip demand by deploying its cash as venture capital, into companies which then buy Nvidia chips. Which then increases its sales and earnings, and that in turn increases its valuation, which means it can deploy equity into AI companies that buy its chips...

Positive feedback loops don't go on forever. And can be really unpleasant when they unwind ...
Thanks for the thoughtful response. I appreciate you taking the time to explain your reasoning.

I think the Caterpillar example narrows the original argument considerably. The original claim was that Nvidia is investing in its customers. From the examples I gave, Intel also invested in startups and ecosystem companies that could increase demand for Intel chips.

The Caterpillar example changes that into a much more specific case: investing in raw startups with no viable business yet whose purchases depend on the investment. That is a different and much stronger assumption.

If this thesis is as certain as the argument suggests, then under the efficient-market argument it is difficult to reconcile that certainty with NVDA still being valued at around $5T and remaining one of the largest companies in the market. If investors broadly believed that this feedback loop was likely to collapse, I would expect that risk to be reflected in a materially lower valuation.

The market can certainly be wrong, but these concerns are public information and are presumably already reflected in the price.
