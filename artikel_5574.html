Can enterprise AI pay for the boom?

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

BREAKING ANALYSIS by Dave Vellante

The artificial intelligence buildout is making an enormous bet on what businesses will eventually be willing to pay for intelligence. In our research this summer on forecasting the AI bubble, we argued that a key indicator to watch is productive utilization by enterprise customers.

Specifically, we said at the time, if infrastructure supply catches up before productive monetization materializes, the risk of a bubble popping increases to what we felt were dangerous levels. We forecast that the supply constraints and healthy capital funding appetites at that time could provide conditions for a soft landing. And we pegged 2029 as the highest-risk year.

And that brings us to the next obvious question: What has to happen inside mainstream enterprises for their AI investments to become economically self-funding? Michael Burry’s analysis of Anthropic PBC highlights the scale and interconnectedness of the commitments that Anthropic is making. Apollo’s research by Torsten Slok raises questions about how broadly the financial benefits are showing up today. And the history of the personal computer and internet eras reminds us that productivity gains aren’t always immediate, and that powerful technologies can coexist with what initially seem to be poor investment returns that take time to appear.

We think the path to a soft landing runs through changes in how businesses operate, specifically the work that they do, the people that they need, how they scale and the value that their customers are ultimately going to pay for. Welcome to Breaking Analysis, episode 332: Can enterprise AI pay for the boom?

Joining us today is Crawford Del Prete. He’s the former president of IDC and now senior adviser at PSG Equity. For decades, Crawford has had a front-row seat on technology transitions, and he brings current perspectives on how software businesses are adapting to AI, how new AI-native companies are forming, and what the emerging operating model looks like. I think what people aren’t thinking about enough is the hybrid permutations that can happen there. For example, what if I get an open-weight or an open-source model from a cloud provider where that utilization can be shared, and I can start to see my utilization go higher going forward? So I think you’re going to see a lot of experimentation. You’re also going to see other compute alternatives start to come onto the market. That’s going to represent incremental capacity, and that’s going to impact pricing as well.

You’re also going to see more efficient AI software going forward. We haven’t talked much about this, but I think we’ll get to it a little bit later. What we’re seeing out of some of the most progressive AI software companies is that they’re not thinking about human interface any longer. They’re thinking about MCP. They’re thinking, “I want to build software for agents to be able to manage.” That brings tremendous efficiency to the overall discussion. That can impact your token costs, get your token costs down and get your utilization better.

I think this gets to that point: I’ve never seen a technology move so fast. Things build on each other in a six-month period, and then you get different solutions that come to bear. So those are real challenges that people are facing today, but they are surmountable. And I’m seeing the trial-and-error happening to be able to get over or around some of those problems.

Dave Vellante: Awesome. Thanks for that. I want to tap your operating experience because you ran a fairly large company at IDC, and now you’re sitting on boards. So let’s explore how software companies redesign the business so the economics can keep pace. We’ve talked about the SaaSpocalypse endlessly. Established software companies have a lot of customers. They have deterministic workflows. They’ve got revenue to protect while they’re introducing these AI extensions into their product. They’re AI-fying it.

Then you’ve got AI-native companies that are building operating models that are modern. They’re agile. They’re designing their architecture and their labor and their pricing around AI from the start. So these create different opportunities and different constraints. Crawford, another sort of multipart question: When you help an established SaaS company become an AI business, how do you think about that? Where do you start? What does an AI-native startup look like?

And the hardest question is, no matter how you price software, you’re going to get it wrong. But if AI reduces the labor that’s required to write code and deliver software or a service, who captures that value? Is the vendor just going to get better margins? Competition comes in. The customer benefits. We talked earlier off-camera about outcome pricing. How do you see all this?

Crawford Del Prete: Let’s unpack it a little bit. It starts at the top, right? When you’re talking about a historically SaaS-based company moving to becoming an AI company, if the ELT and the CEO are not bought into AI, if it’s just, “I’m going to give Copilot to a few people,” it’s over. You’ve got to have buy-in from the very top. It sounds trite. It sounds easy, but it’s super, super important in terms of a transformation.

Probably one of the biggest areas that we see as companies start transforming is that roles are starting to blend. So you used to have a head of product, and then you had product management, and then you had product engineering. We’re seeing those roles all kind of blend together. Product management is probably the area where I’m seeing the biggest redefinition. It’s actually merging with product engineering, and you don’t necessarily need that handoff anymore.

You can start to see massive amounts of scale within how you engineer the product and then how you get that product out to market. You’re seeing technical product managers being able to use AI to kind of blend those roles together. I think that’s probably, in the early days here, one of the biggest changes I’ve seen in companies, whether they be AI-native companies or SaaS companies: Those roles are all kind of getting slammed together.

Obviously, we saw the move to product-led growth under SaaS, and I think we’re seeing that even more so in AI, where you want to give the product a try, you want to be able to download the product, you want to be able to use the product. That’s also a big change. Another really big change is not only in the selling model, but how they think about bringing a product to market.

I’m seeing a lot of these small regional resellers becoming way, way more capable and able to use AI to become more efficient in their selling skills. What I’m also seeing is that a lot of the very large distributors are super interested in bringing on software assets into their marketplaces because they can make higher margins on them. So we’re seeing that the channels to market for AI companies are much more plentiful than we ever saw in the past. I would say those are some of the changes that I’ve seen in the marketplace.

Now, when you start thinking about how you price the product and how you bring that product to market from a pricing standpoint, I think the biggest change associated with AI, whether this is an established company or a company coming out, is that we’re seeing a lot of interest in usage-based pricing. I think the biggest thing for a non-SaaS company who’s bringing in AI, and this is the biggest challenge I see multiple times, is: How do I discretely monetize AI? How can I look and say, “I made these investments in AI, and I’m getting customers to pay more as a function of it”?

Because in so many cases, I see this: You don’t sell AI separately. You sell AI embedded into the core product that you have, and then you get customers to buy in. So what I’m starting to see is, you see it in cross-sell, upsell. You see it in price increases. And then you ultimately start to bring out incremental features that you can charge extra for that are AI-only going forward. You’ve got to start moving away from seat-based. You’ve got to start moving to usage-based, which is what I’m seeing most companies doing.

Now, the problem with the outcome-based stuff — and outcome-based comes up a lot — is that it becomes very episodic. It becomes exactly what you mentioned, which is it’s associated with a project. And when it’s associated with a project, then you don’t get that stickiness. Maybe, if you’re good, you get re-occurring revenue, but you don’t get recurring revenue. You don’t get that subscription revenue, and that’s a problem for these companies. Obviously, for the same reason that they moved to SaaS, they don’t want to move away from that.

Dave Vellante: Right. So, it’s a complicated situation. Pricing always is for software, and it’s early days. But you’re clearly seeing some positivity. In our experience, and you’ve seen this, when we see a new wave, it’s oftentimes the case that the new is not large enough to offset the decline in the old. This time, there may be a difference because you look at what Anthropic’s doing. At the same time, we still want to test whether the monetization for mainstream enterprises happens before the bubble bursts, and that’s kind of what we’re trying to figure out.

So really a lot of pain. I don’t think this is the bubble-burst moment. In fact, we’re letting a little air out, which I think is a positive thing.

But in this chart, we’re looking at four related factors. One, are customers getting measurable outcomes? And maybe, if those become measurable, we can figure out software pricing a little bit better. Two, is there a product-market fit? In other words, does that product produce repeatable spending after the initial POCs? We’d love to see ARR, not just another project the next year.

Three, can software and model suppliers earn sustainable margins after accounting for their delivery costs? (With) tokens, building intelligence is expensive. And four, does the infrastructure usage produce enough cash to support operations and capital obligations and to fund the next cycle? Customer productivity is necessary to our thesis. But remember, value can flow to customers through lower prices without necessarily supporting the returns of the factory. That’s something that infrastructure investors are looking at to justify the higher valuation. So you’ve got to examine the whole chain.

Crawford, over the next 12 to 18 months — maybe that’s too long of a time frame in this world — what are the key developments that we should be watching that are going to make you more confident that productive customer demand is catching up with the buildout? It’s widely spread. You said it’s not narrow. And what, by the way, would change your mind? What are the metrics and KPIs you look at with your portfolio companies? How do you see it?

Crawford Del Prete: So obviously, you’ve got to be looking at retention, right? You’ve got to be looking at net retention, at gross retention, and seeing how those are moving as they roll out new AI features. What I see is a lot of value in looking at win-loss analysis. Why did a customer leave? Where did a customer go? Did a customer go to a more AI-native factor? Did the customer go to an internal effort where they could create the product themselves? You see that on win-loss.

That’s a super important metric because it gets to what customers are willing to risk that accountability layer on by building it themselves, unless they’re building that accountability layer in. How much the company is able to uniquely monetize AI is also super important, or do you see it in the retention numbers in terms of cross-sell, upsell? The other areas that are really important to me are: Are you attracting more customers with your AI features? And is the product getting better over time because of those customers coming to the platform?

Then, even more importantly, is the product not only getting better, but is each incremental customer getting a better experience? Is the data that’s being created more valuable to that broad set of customers because they’re part of that ecosystem? And of course, that’s all fueled by better decisions associated with AI. The other thing that I’m spending a lot of time looking at is this notion of what is the next action that a company can find and uncover with AI.

So, for example, maybe they’re in the ITSM space. What happened to the ticket? Was it reopened? How long was it until the ticket was closed? Maybe you generated an invoice with a piece of AI software. Well, was that invoice disputed? Was it paid late? Was it written off? What was that next outcome? Maybe you made a recommendation. Did the customer accept it? Did they challenge it? Did they ignore it?

Those kinds of follow-on interactions fuel that two-way customer value, where more customers rejecting a recommendation tell you the recommendation wasn’t very good. So the recommendations get better. Those are all the kinds of things that I’m looking at in order to see the benefits associated with AI.

Dave Vellante: Basically that next best action is really interesting because we’ve been pretty good at what happened and pretty good at why it happened, but we’re not really good at what’s going to happen next and what we should do next, right?

Crawford Del Prete: That’s the possibility AI opens us up to.
