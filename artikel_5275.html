TL;DR: US spot Ether ETFs saw approximately $139.7 million in net outflows on November 2, Farside Investors’ daily data show. The amounts are published for specific trading dates and can be adjusted by data providers.

On November 2, BlackRock’s ETHA was responsible for roughly $138.3 million of the reported net withdrawal. That clustering suggests the fund-level series deserves careful reading. Investors can track one issuer and the whole category without treating the two as interchangeable, particularly when other funds show smaller and sometimes opposite daily movements.

US spot Ether ETFs saw approximately $139.7 million in net outflows on November 2, Farside Investors’ daily data show. The previous listed sessions were also negative, with approximately $155.7 million leaving on October 30 and $105.5 million on October 29. The sequence offers a more informative starting point than any single day, although the scale of redemptions varies from session to session. The dated original disclosure is the starting point for the figures and product scope discussed here.

ETH ETF flows are subscriptions and withdrawals through an investment wrapper, not a direct count of ETH sold by everyone in the ecosystem. Exchange traders, staking participants, derivatives desks and users of Ethereum applications have different motives and do not all appear in the same table. ETF holdings reflect a familiar securities-market channel that may lag or diverge from blockchain activity.

A series of negative sessions can change how investors talk about Ethereum exposure, especially when a large issuer accounts for most of a particular day’s redemptions. But a falling ETF share count and weaker network fundamentals are not synonymous. Analysts should compare flows with changes in broader risk appetite, fund allocations and the health of Ethereum-based services rather than assume one metric explains the others.

Ethereum has multiple sources of demand and risk: trading, staking, stablecoin settlement, application fees and long-term treasury allocation. A change in one part of that system should be tested against the others instead of promoted into a universal explanation. The market often overreacts when one publicly available metric is mistaken for the whole network economy.

For additional perspective, it is worth reading our existing reporting on an earlier Ethereum ETF outflow comparison and the October Bitcoin and Ether ETF weekly review. Those background reports help separate established facts from a new, still-developing announcement.

The amounts are published for specific trading dates and can be adjusted by data providers. They do not tell us who redeemed, whether positions were hedged or whether the cash moved to Bitcoin, other funds or unrelated assets. Nor does this table establish that protocol staking withdrawals caused the ETF activity; those are different processes with distinct timing and constraints.

For investors, the right comparison is between liquidity, expected holding period and obligations. Staked ETH, ETF shares and treasury holdings may all track the same underlying asset, but their exit routes and operating constraints differ. A useful report therefore asks not simply how much ETH is involved, but how readily the exposure can be changed when conditions deteriorate.

The next useful evidence is the issuer breakdown over several additional sessions, alongside spot-market depth and whether redemption pressure broadens. A return to sustained net creations would change the recent trend, but an isolated inflow would not erase the larger pattern. Investors should keep ETF demand separate from claims about Ethereum’s throughput, staking or rollup adoption.
