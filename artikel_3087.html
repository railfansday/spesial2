The Stock Market Is Flashing a Warning Seen Just 6 Times in 156 Years, and History Is Clear What Comes Next for Stocks

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

A time-honored valuation metric is flashing a warning, but history suggests the real danger for investors might not be another market crash.

By Steven Porrello – Nov 20, 2026 at 2:43PM EST

History doesn't repeat itself exactly, but sometimes it comes pretty close. And right now, one of the stock market's most reliable valuation tools is flashing a warning seen only six times since 1871.

The warning comes from the Shiller price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, one of our most time-honored valuation tools. This handy metric divides the current price of the S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.77%) by its average inflation-adjusted earnings from the previous 10 years, helping us gauge how expensive stocks are. Over 156 years, the Shiller P/E has averaged nearly 18. Only six times has the metric crossed a threshold of 30; in five of the last six, it was followed by a significant market decline.

Today, the Shiller P/E clocks in at around 40. On the surface, that seems concerning for investors. But there might be a catch to this seemingly ominous warning, and it might change how we interpret what history is signaling to us.

First, let's start with the historical record. Fair warning: It's not particularly encouraging.

The five previous periods in which the Shiller P/E rose above 30 for two consecutive months during a bull market did not end well for investors. That's an euphemism. It ended badly, very badly. One only has to mention the crash of 1929, or the bursting of the dot-com bubble in 2000, or the COVID-19 crash of 2020, to get a mental picture of the damage. The S&P 500 also dropped nearly 20% in late 2018 and roughly 25% during the 2022 bear market.

From this perspective, the Shiller P/E looks like a crystal ball. Jump above 30, so it goes, and a market crash will follow. But there's a problem with this interpretation, and it could be making the Shiller P/E look far better at predicting crashes than it actually is.

The problem could be that we're looking at these six occurrences separately when at least four could be considered as one extended period of unusually expensive stocks. This is the argument of former Federal Reserve economist William Emmons, who has pointed out that the Shiller P/E hasn't fallen below 24 since October 2013.

Why would this matter? Simply put, it means we have fewer truly independent examples of the Shiller P/E predicting a market crash. Instead of six separate warnings, we might be looking at just three periods of extremely high valuations, with the third still ongoing. That doesn't change the fact that market crashes occurred. But counting each episode separately could make the Shiller P/E look more reliable at predicting crashes than it actually is.

That said, Emmons doesn't discredit or dismiss the Shiller P/E. He thinks it's a valuable metric. But its real value, he suggests, doesn't lie in predicting market crashes; rather, it helps investors understand the long-term returns stocks might deliver over the next decade. And when valuations are unusually high, Emmons argues, those returns are historically disappointing. Which brings me to my next point.

Again, it's easy to look at the history of the Shiller, and assume that a market crash will end this period of unusually expensive stocks. But a stock market crash isn't the only way high valuations could level out. Indeed, stocks could simply go nowhere for years while earnings remain strong, and a similar result would occur.

Here's what I mean. Let's say that the Shiller P/E drops from 40 to 30. Well, that could mean stock prices dropped 25%, assuming average inflation-adjusted earnings stayed the same. But it could also mean that stock prices stayed relatively flat while earnings increased by a third or more. These are totally different investing experiences -- a market crash versus years of stagnant returns -- but either way, the market comes out looking less expensive.

In this way, the warning from history isn't that stocks are about to plunge even more than they have recently. They might, they might not. Either way, it's a good reminder to pause, take a hard look at our investments, and reassess how much risk we're taking. After all, the stock market could avoid a crash, corporate profits could continue growing, and investors could still earn disappointing returns. That might sound paradoxical, but at today's valuation it is entirely possible.
