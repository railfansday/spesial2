An Abandoned Pacifier Stirs a Moving Conversation

My friend told me about an experience that jolted her the other morning. She is my age, give or take a few years, and the mother of two adult daughters.

She was walking near the Hudson River when she spotted an upturned pacifier/dummy on the ground.

This image brought a flood of memories for her and became a visceral reminder of her own early mothering days. She told me about this, and I immediately connected with her experience. We gave ourselves the space to indulge our own imaginings and immerse ourselves in our individual inner worlds.

There is something embedded in the form of a dummy, which is intriguing. Maybe it's the shape, adapted from the maternal breast, and the meaning this can hold. Maybe it's something about the maternal collective unconscious and our need to soothe our child at any cost; our hope of harmony with our infant. The pacifier is also a gauge of social judgement; do "good mothers" use them?

My friend’s story took me back to the early days of my mothering. For me, the dummy played a prominent part in the mothering of each of my children. It represented an almost sacred object holding the potential of a peaceful retreat for my infant and myself.

A similar playbook emerged for each of my children. Each time, there was an initial resistance to the dummy, quickly followed by acceptance as my baby ceded to its comfort.

I remember the wonder of watching my baby’s rhythmic sucking, the fluttering of the eyes. Then, a momentary halt to the sucking, as if he forgot. Then, the sucking started again. I was mesmerized watching my baby remembering and forgetting, her eyes fluttering while drifting off to blissful sleep in her own world; being aware of the dummy one minute, and then unaware.

This was often followed by a release of the dummy, a realization, and a cry as it was gently returned to his mouth so the sucking could continue. This became a regular routine which brought confidence and solace. A respite for both of us.

Then, my memories shifted to that of my older infant’s cot filled with dummies one minute, then empty the next. My infant never tired of this game of throwing and retrieving it.

It has dawned on me that this game held so much meaning for my later understanding of mothering. The importance of laughter and games and the reality that the dummy, like the mother, is both necessary and dispensable over time.

Then, my thoughts began to meander back to this particular dummy left on the street. What is its story?

Is there a baby crying and screaming for it? How will the baby and the mother manage without it?

Who lost it? Maybe the father? The grandmother? Did a sibling throw it out? Was it older children or teenagers playing with it for a stunt?

Was this an infant’s planned farewell to this dummy?

Is there a mum somewhere ferreting through her diaper bag to find it, or is she looking for another, saying to herself, “Why did I only bring one?”

The anticipated and real screams from her baby, and worrying about how she would soothe her.

What about characteristics of the dummy itself? Is it pink or blue or maybe white? Is it new or old? What does this tell us about the baby?

Is it a newborn’s dummy? Is it one of those new ergonomic ones, which is a reminder of the years that have passed since my own hands-on mothering and what has changed?

Then, some truths about dummies/pacifiers:

For some women, a dummy has no meaning, and for others it means so much.

The idea of a dummy is also wrapped up in the social and inner discourse of judgment. In my day, there was a lot of narrative surrounding the use of dummies, many shoulds and should nots. Does a good mom give her baby a dummy? Is she lazy? Will it damage her baby’s bite and teeth? If you give your child one, how do you get rid of it? Will he still be walking around with it at 3 or 4 years of age?

Questions such as these return to the everlasting judgment and moral reckoning that mothers continually endure and can hopefully challenge.

My friend’s story of the dummy abandoned on the ground provokes reflection:

For the current hands-on mother, the dummy captures multiple moments, thoughts, and possibilities of her everyday life: The many challenges that are embodied in the face of an inconsolable child. The mother’s constant striving to soothe her child, to do her best, and the reality that there is a continual struggle between her own and her baby’s needs and questions about how these are balanced.

It is a reminder of the social judgment that surrounds the mother, where she really needs compassion, support, and encouragement to name and manage her daily life and her feelings. Our job as post-hands-on mothers is to champion this cause.

For mothers such as myself and my friend, while we are no longer hands-on, we own our memories. They are treasures that we forever hold that belong only to us and one day to our daughters. Moments of bliss, connection, and struggle are embodied in the image of a dummy.

The power of these objects offers a valuable and seamless conduit back to that time and into the future, embodying what we are as mothers, immortalizing our maternal memories, and continuing to enrich our lives.
