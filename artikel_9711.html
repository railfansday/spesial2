Build a care home agency staffing review in a spreadsheet

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

A workbook with 5 tabs can show what a care home's single staffing figure hides. This post builds it step by step, with a formula for each column.

The workbook suits a buyer, lender or investor with monthly figures from a UK care home. Every number in the examples is invented, and the post says so again where it matters. The numbers show how the formulas behave. No real home sits behind them, and they set no standard.

Two outside sources sit behind it. The 12-week point in Step 11 comes from GOV.UK's agency worker pages, read in November 2026. The scope of the firm's financial review comes from its page on buying a care home, listed in the closing line. The formulas themselves are practical reasoning, and a result prompts a question. It is not a finding and it is not advice.

Five tabs keep each job separate and make the workbook easy to hand to someone else.

Each of the 5 tabs is fed by a different record from the seller. A missing record is itself a question for the seller, so the list below doubles as a request list.

On the Data tab, put one month in each row. Row 1 holds the headings and rows 2 to 7 hold six months. A real review would use 24 rows. The columns are as follows.

The invented inputs below fill columns A to I for the 6 months. Costs are in pounds. Residents are the monthly average.

The home in this example has 50 beds, so the monthly average never reaches full occupancy. Hours come from payroll and agency timesheets. Money comes from the accounts. Keeping the two apart lets the later steps compare them.

An occupied bed day counts a resident who holds a bed for a full day, so a 30-day month with 40 residents gives 1,200. It is the unit that income follows, so it becomes the denominator for most ratios in this workbook.

Fill the formula down to row 7. January gives 31 days times 40 residents, which is 1,240. The six months give 1,240, 1,148, 1,302, 1,320, 1,395 and 1,380.

One caution applies. An average of residents is only as good as the figure behind it. If the home reports a snapshot taken on the last day of the month, the average can mislead. Daily occupancy from the resident register can give a truer count. The ratio below can then use that count in place of days times average.

Four more columns turn the inputs into ratios.

Fill each down to row 7. January gives a total cost of £127,475 and 7,200 hours. That is £102.80 per occupied bed day and 5.81 hours per occupied bed day.

The six months of cost per occupied bed day read £102.80, £112.15, £98.39, £98.19, £92.72 and £93.46. Residents rose from 40 to 46 over the period, and total cost stayed between £127,475 and £129,615. Cost per bed day therefore fell over the period, though not every month. That fits a rota with a floor, which is reasoning and not proof.

Total hours moved from 7,200 to 7,400. Hours per occupied bed day fell from 5.81 to 5.36. A buyer reading the average alone would see a stable cost line and miss the change in occupancy beneath it.

Agency can be measured in two ways, and the two answers differ whenever an agency hour costs more than a permanent hour.

January gives 1,450 of 7,200 hours, which is 20.1 per cent, and £34,800 of £127,475, which is 27.3 per cent. By June the hours share is 12.2 per cent and the cost share is 16.7 per cent. In every month the cost share is the larger of the two.

The gap between the 2 columns is the pricing signal. A home that quotes only the hours share understates how much of its bill goes to agency. A home that quotes only the cost share hides how many shifts agency covers.

Rates are derived, not typed in. Dividing a cost by its hours gives the rate the home actually paid, after all invoices and employer costs. Two rates matter here, permanent and agency.

In the invented figures, permanent staff cost £16.00 an hour in January, and £16.40 from April after an assumed pay rise. Agency cost £24.00 throughout, on the coded line, so the premium would rise if costs coded elsewhere were moved back (see Step 9). Bank staff cost £17.50, which the workbook can show in a column of its own if it is useful.

January's premium is 1,450 hours times £8.00, which is £11,600. The six months give £11,600, £12,000, £10,400, £8,360, £7,220 and £6,840. A formula at the foot of the column adds them.

The six-month premium is £56,420 in this example. It is the size of a gap, not money waiting to be saved. Filling the posts takes recruitment time and cost. Recruitment may be hard in some towns, which is reasoning and not a measured fact.

A flag column tells a reader where to look first. It compares each month's cost per occupied bed day with the month before.

The formula starts in row 3 because January has no earlier month. Fill it down to row 7. The 5 per cent threshold is an assumption, and a reviewer can change it.

In the example, three months are flagged. February is up 9.1 per cent, March is down 12.3 per cent and May is down 5.6 per cent. February and March make a pair: February had the fewest days and a dip in occupied bed days, and March recovered.

A flag is a prompt for one written question to the manager. It does not say anything is wrong. Short months and changes in occupancy can both move the ratio, and an experienced reader would expect them to.

The Floor tab shows the same direction as Step 4 with simpler numbers, on separate invented figures. In this reasoning, a rota has a floor of hours that does not fall with each empty bed. Above a threshold, each extra resident adds hours.

The four resident counts 30, 35, 40 and 45 go in A7 to A10. Three formulas then fill the row beside each.

The model gives these 4 results, all invented.

Cost per resident is highest at 30 residents because the floor is spread over fewer people. It falls to £54.86 at 35 and then falls more slowly. The step from 35 to 40 residents adds only £200 a day.

In the 30-resident row, the model shows how a low-occupancy year raises cost without any agency use. It also shows why a full home cannot be priced on a half-empty home's staffing cost, because the hours above the threshold do rise.

The agency line shows what was coded to it. Some of the 6 months' invoices may sit in other codes, so the Ledger tab compares the line with the supplier records.

Column C comes from the purchase ledger, for the same months as Tab 1. Run a report of every supplier paid for staff. Add up their invoices for the month, whatever code each was posted to. A list of suppliers paid over a set amount in the year helps to find the names.

For three months, the invented figures are as follows.

The true share is higher by between 2.2 and 2.8 percentage points in each month. In this example, the missing amounts were coded to care wages, so total cost is unchanged and only the split moves. Permanent cost on the Data tab would be overstated by the same amounts.

Nothing in the 3 months above accuses anyone, because a code can end up wrong for several reasons. The aim is to read the share on the right base.

Accrual means booking a cost in the month the work happened, whenever the invoice arrives. The Invoices tab tests whether that was done for 3 sample invoices.

Three invented invoices follow, entered as full dates with the year 2026.

Two invoices are late, totalling £13,500. February is understated by £7,200. March carries invoice A and loses invoice B, so it is overstated by £900 net. April carries invoice B, so it is overstated by £6,300.

Posting dates like these can move a month's ratio, so a flag in Step 7 may come partly from timing and not from the rota. If the seller books late invoices on receipt, the buyer can ask for the timesheets and rebuild each month on the week worked. Month-end journals for shifts worked but not yet invoiced show whether accrual is done at all.

GOV.UK sets the point at 12 weeks. An agency worker who reaches it in one job qualifies for rights equal to a directly employed person's, and equal pay is among them. Sick leave or breaks hold the count, and a new or substantively different role begins it afresh.

The sheet can count weeks for the 3 workers below, but it cannot say whether a role is the same job. That is a legal question for a solicitor, and the firm gives no legal advice on it. The sheet only builds the list that the solicitor needs.

Column D is a prompt only. The formula does not use it, so a changed role means entering the new start date in column B. Three invented workers follow, shown by reference only.

W1 and W2 have passed 12 weeks. W3 has 4 weeks to go. A buyer can use the flag to ask how pay was set for W1 and W2. The agency's contract terms for the period after week 12 are a second question. The pay point concerns the worker. On GOV.UK's pay page, a dispute about pay falls to the contract between agency and hirer. The agency's charge to the home is a separate question.

Normalised cost is the cost a buyer expects under its own ownership, with unusual items adjusted. The workbook can show one adjustment as a scenario. The rule is simple: every assumption sits in a cell with a label.

With the example's six-month premium of £56,420, a 50 per cent share removes £28,210. The assumption carries all the weight. It needs a recruitment plan behind it, and the hours may be vacancy cover, absence cover or short peaks. The first kind is the only one that recruitment could address.

A second assumption can sit beside it. If the home pays a recruitment fee or an induction cost for each hire, a fuller scenario could deduct that cost. A forecast with no cost for the change is incomplete.

The scenario is a forecast, so the post labels it that way. It does not claim the saving exists. If the seller's rota shows that most hours are absence cover, the share in cell B11 on the Data tab should be lower.

The workbook's last job is to produce written questions. Each flag or gap in the tabs above becomes one question, so the answers are on record.

None of these asks anyone to defend a choice. Each one asks for a fact that the ledger cannot supply alone.

The workbook reads money and hours. It cannot show whether the home had enough people on shift, because that turns on the needs of each of the 46 residents. Whether 5.36 hours per occupied bed day is enough for 46 residents is for those who watch the care on the ground to say.

It also leaves out the mood of the team and the home's pull as an employer in its town. A visit helps with both. It cannot say what an employment contract allows or how a tribunal might rule. Those 2 points sit outside the workbook.

This post is general information. Key Ledgers Global gives no legal advice and no regulated investment advice, and nothing here advises on one home.
