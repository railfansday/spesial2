Speculoos Scents Are the Cozy Perfume Trend Taking Over Winter

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Just as fragrance notes make up a perfume’s composition, fragrance families can also encompass their own layers of niche categories. Case in point: speculoos scents. Consider this emerging perfume profile an offspring of the beloved gourmand family. Gourmands home in on different kinds of sweet notes, whether it’s fruity, creamy, or, in the case of speculoos, reminiscent of freshly baked treats. Jeniece Trizzino, VP of Innovation and Physical Product at Scentbird and Drift, explains why this emerging category feels so seasonally appropriate and how to add them to your current scent library.

Like any gourmand, the note is built as an accord, a blend of materials your nose reads as one smell. “Ethyl maltol gives the burnt sugar. Vanillin gives the vanilla. Tonka bean adds a soft, hay-like warmth. The spices do the rest. Woods, patchouli, or musk sit underneath, so it smells like a perfume and not a dessert,” she notes.

Much like traditional gourmands, speculoos are built on familiar sweet cues: vanilla, caramel, chocolate, cream, and fruit. “Think cocoa with caramel and condensed milk. It’s creamy and comforting, and it leans toward dessert,” says Trizzino. A speculoos scent is sweet too, but it’s more spice-forward and toasted. The main difference is that speculoos scents lead with cinnamon, nutmeg, and cardamom. The addition of burnt sugar and browned butter allows it to smell baked, not syrupy. “You still get the comfort, with more going on. It can add complexity, not just more sweetness,” she adds.

Speculoos scents are carving out a place in fragrance as traditional gourmands evolve. “The very literal vanilla, caramel, and dessert scents can feel predictable now,” Trizzino points out. “The move is toward bitter, savory, and smoky contrasts. Traditional gourmands aren’t going away, but speculoos is one way to keep the comfort and add depth.”

Key notes: speculoos (cinnamon, cardamom), vanilla, sandalwood

Mancera’s Amore Caffè moves speculoos to the café. It’s built like an affogato. Black coffee and amaretto open it, then vanilla ice cream with a hint of speculoos, over vanilla, brown sugar, and grey amber.

Key notes: black coffee, amaretto liquor, vanilla ice cream, speculoos, vanilla pods, brown sugar, grey amber

Across its library, Kayali has nailed gourmand scents. What puts Freedom Musk Latte in this new category is its creamy, cozy, latte-inspired notes of cinnamon, chocolate musk, and café latte, punctuated with a burst of mandarin and pink pepper.

Key Notes: bergamot, café latte, chocolate musk

Key notes: cinnamon, coffee, cocoa, vanilla, musk

Jousset Parfums’ Mascarpone & Speculoos goes all the way to dessert. Mascarpone, butter, and whiskey open it. Coffee, cinnamon, cream and caramel follow, then vanilla, sugar and biscuit. It’s creamy, rich and unapologetically gourmand.

Key notes: butter, mascarpone, whisky, black coffee, caramel, cinnamon, cream, biscuit, sugar, vanilla

What makes these scents so seasonally appropriate is that winter is when people reach for scents that feel warm, comforting, and a little enveloping. Speculoos does all three. “The baking spices bring warmth. The toasted sugar and biscuit notes bring a familiar kind of comfort. It has the appeal of a favorite winter drink or something baking at home, but you can wear it,” says Trizzino.

Winter perfume has a reputation for being sweet and heavy. In contrast, speculoos is sweet, but the spice keeps it from being just sugar. “You get the comfort without the weight. And it travels. A recognizable food scent works in candles, diffusers, body care, and perfume, and it makes an easy gift.”

Although there’s already a lot of depth, speculoos scents are also layerable. “I actually think layering is where speculoos gets really interesting. It has enough warmth and sweetness to work as a base, but it’s also distinctive enough to change the personality of another fragrance.”

Trizzino says the best pairings depend on what you want to bring out of it:

For darker layering: woods and amber add depth. Cedar, sandalwood, amber, and vetiver balance the sweetness and make speculoos feel warmer and more sophisticated. Smoke, rum, and leather take it darker still. Tobacco, incense, rum, and leather add depth and make it a rich, comforting gourmand.

For richer layering: coffee and other gourmand notes lean into the dessert quality. Vanilla, tonka, chocolate, milky notes, and coffee make it indulgent. Baked fruit makes it seasonal. Apple, pear, or dates sit naturally with the toffee, vanilla, and baking spices of a winter gourmand.

For lighter layering: florals soften the spice. Jasmine, rose, and orange blossom add a more unexpected dimension and keep it from feeling too heavy. Citrus adds contrast. Bergamot, mandarin, and orange cut through the sweetness and give the scent a brighter opening. And a soft skin musk makes it more intimate. It can take speculoos from something that smells like a bakery to something warm and personal on skin.

Trizzino’s layering advice is to start with the fragrance you want to be most prominent and build around it. “You don’t need two scents that smell exactly alike. Sometimes the best combinations come from contrast. A simple way to start is with a familiar base like vanilla or sandalwood, then add one contrasting accent: fruit for a brighter blend, smoke or rum for a darker one. You can layer across formats too, with a perfume over a bodycare product,” she explains.

Speculoos doesn’t have to smell like dessert. It can smell like spice, warmth, woods, or skin. That’s what makes it fun to wear.
