It’s been over a decade since a relatively fringe 1700s religious sect—the Shakers—found unlikely ubiquity in the modern world. It started with Shaker peg rails (this story, originally published in 2013, took us by surprise when it went wild). Then “Shaker-style kitchen cabinets” became a household term. A New York City restaurant, a French bakery, and a Mexico City hotel all took design cues from Shaker villages. And on it went.

In all the years since, enthusiasm for the simple, utilitarian design has barely dipped. If anything, it’s grown. But recently a new slew of Shaker-adjacent news leads us to believe the Shaker fervor is reaching a fever pitch.

First, Fan alerted us to this exhibit, at the American Folk Art Museum, on Shaker art (utterly “exuberant” compared to their pared-back furnishings). Then this pop-up, co-curated by the actress Frances McDormand, caught our eye; featuring Shaker cradles, it invited visitors to “take your time. Rock yourself. Mend if you’d like.” Now an exhibit called  “The Shakers: A World in the Making”, backed by the Vitra Design Museum in Germany and previously at the ICA in Philadelphia (where it was covered by the New York Times), is on view at the Milwaukee Art Museum through the end of January.

If you’ve ever wanted to adorn your envelopes with Shaker designs in miniature, now you can, thanks to a collection of Shaker Forever stamps. And with the debut of the Amanda Seyfried film The Testament of Ann Lee last year, filmed in part at Hancock Shaker Village in Massachusetts, it’s safe to say that Shaker design has officially entered the mainstream.

The 250th anniversary, in 2024, of the first Shakers’ arrival in America is perhaps a reason for this latest resurgence—though we can’t help but wonder whether Shaker cradles and handmade wares answer a yearning, now, for simpler times.
