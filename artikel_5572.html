Homeownership divides widen as older, wealthier buyers dominate 2026 market

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

NAR’s 2026 profile shows a median buyer age of 58 and repeat buyers at 62, with owners holding homes for 12 years before selling. Repeat buyers reported $127,000 median income and 30% paid all cash, reinforcing a market split by equity.  AI Summary

The 2026 “Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers” from the National Association of Realtors paints a housing market increasingly split between older, equity-rich owners and younger households still locked out by high costs.

The report, based on buyers and sellers who closed between July 2025 and June 2026, shows that inventory began to loosen but remained largely unaffordable for households earning under $75,000. Mortgage rates averaged 6.33% during the period, dipping below 6% briefly in early 2026 before climbing again, which gave first-time buyers only a short-lived affordability window.

The typical homebuyer is 58, just under last year’s record of 59. Repeat buyers are even older, with a median age of 62, and they bring substantial equity and income to the table. Their median household income rose to $127,000 from $111,700, and 30% of repeat buyers paid all cash for their home.

Many of these buyers are using long-held equity to fund moves driven by lifestyle choices rather than employment. The top reason for moving among repeat buyers is proximity to friends and family, and the plurality of sellers moved into larger homes, while nearly half moved into newer homes. That reflects how existing owners, sitting on equity and often low-rate mortgages, can still trade up when they choose, even in a higher-rate environment.

Homeowners now typically own their property for 12 years before selling, the longest tenure the survey has recorded. One in four sellers had never sold a home before, meaning many are navigating their first “move-up” or “right-sizing” transaction after a long period of ownership.

First-time buyers clawed back a small share of the market, rising to 22% from last year’s record low of 21%. Historically, before 2008, first-time buyers made up about 40% of sales, underscoring how far the market remains from prior norms.

That all-cash segment of first-time buyers is now at its highest level on record, at 10% of all first-time buyers. NAR links this to a growing “silver tsunami” of wealth transfer: older generations are passing down cash or property that allows recipients to bypass traditional financing. Those buyers are not typical renters graduating into ownership; instead, they are often older adults entering the market later in life with outside financial support.

The data shows that younger households are feeling the squeeze from both prices and rates. The U.S. homeownership rate for Americans under 35 has fallen for four straight years to 37%, while homeownership for those over 50 remains above 70%.

Census data between 2021 and 2024 shows that households ages 25 to 34 grew by more than 700,000, but the number of homeowners in that age group rose by only about 65,000. Their homeownership rate slipped from 41.6% to 40.4%, and the share of recent movers in that age range who became owners dropped sharply. At the same time, more young adults are living with family: 16.4% of 25- to 34-year-olds lived at home in 2025, up from 9% in 1980.

This is not because young adults have abandoned the idea of owning. Pew Research Center polling cited in the report shows that nine in 10 say it is harder for their generation to buy a home than it was for their parents, but they still see homeownership as a good investment. Yet the New York Fed’s housing survey finds only about 35% of renters now believe they will ever own a home, down from nearly 56% in 2016.

Faced with high rents and high prices, more aspiring first-time buyers are moving in with family to save. In 1993, 12% of first-time buyers lived at home before their purchase; by 2026 that share rose to 21%, suggesting that avoiding market-rate rent is becoming a key strategy to accumulate a down payment.

Married couples still dominate the market at 63% of buyers, but their share has fallen over time, especially among first-time buyers. Single women account for 20% of buyers and single men 8%, while unmarried couples hold steady at 6%.

One of the fastest-growing segments is buyers purchasing with non-romantic roommates. That “other” category hit 4% of all buyers and 5% of first-time buyers, a 45-year high. Sharing a mortgage can help manage monthly costs and reflects how affordability pressures are reshaping household structures.

Most buyers — 74% — do not have children under 18 in the home, though that share ticked down slightly from last year. About 26% of buyers have children at home, and 14% bought a multigenerational home. Among multigenerational buyers, key reasons were caring for aging parents (30%), cost savings (23%), adult children never leaving home (19%) or moving back (18%).

On income and identity, 85% of buyers identified as White or Caucasian. Seven percent identified as Hispanic or Latino, 6% as Black or African American, 4% as Asian or Pacific Islander and 2% as another ethnicity. Seventeen percent of recent buyers, or someone in their household, were veterans and 2% were active-duty service members.

The single most common reason to buy a home was simple: 19% cited the desire to own a home of their own, and 60% of first-time buyers gave that answer. Nearly half of all buyers — 46% — said “the timing was just right” and they felt ready to purchase. Another 19% said they had little choice and had to buy when they did, suggesting life events and external pressures still drive a significant share of moves.

A key shift over the past two decades has been why people move. Job-related relocations have faded sharply: 25% of buyers moved for a job move in 2000 compared with just 5% today. Moves to be closer to friends and family more than doubled from 6% to 14% over the same period, aligning with the aging buyer base and the growing importance of social support in housing decisions.

Even as technology options expand, real estate professionals remain central to the transaction. The report shows 90% of buyers and 92% of sellers used a real estate agent or broker while for-sale-by-owner (FSBO) transactions are stuck at an all-time low of 5%.

Buyers primarily wanted help finding the right home (51%), negotiating terms (15%), negotiating price (12%) and handling paperwork (7%). Only 1% found their agent through artificial intelligence. Most relied on repeat relationships or referrals, underscoring the continuing importance of personal networks and trust.

Sellers prioritized marketing, pricing the home competitively and selling within a set timeframe. For many, particularly the 25% selling for the first time, agents are guiding them through a complex equity trade in a market where price discovery and timing are difficult.

For lenders, real estate agents and other housing professionals, NAR’s 2026 profile underscores a market defined by scarcity, delayed household formation and unequal access to ownership. Older, equity-rich repeat buyers and wealth-assisted first-timers are shaping demand and pricing, while large segments of younger renters see ownership as increasingly out of reach.

That disconnect will influence product design, marketing and policy debates around inventory and affordability. Strategies that address multigenerational households, co-buying arrangements, down payment accumulation and the needs of aging buyers looking to move closer to family are likely to become more central to business planning in the years ahead.
