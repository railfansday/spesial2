Morton may be struggling but charity is bringing hope to the community

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Christmas is an important time of the year for Morton in the Community. Since 2019 the social impact arm of the struggling Championship club has taken part in Festive Friends, the programme run by the SPFL Trust to provide turkey and trimmings for those who need them most.

For the first time families from the east end of Greenock will also be invited to Cappielow for a separate dinner this year while a Boots and Pieces pop up shop in the town centre provides sports kit and equipment for  a community ranked the most deprived in the country.

“Think of the pressure families come under at Christmas,” says charity Chief Executive Brian McLaughlin. “They come under pressure to put stuff on their credit cards and it’s such a difficult time for everyone. Even for businesses and football clubs….”

For the local football club in Greenock this Christmas threatens to be more difficult than usual. Morton face a financial short-fall of £320,000 after main sponsors Dalrada failed to make promised payments on time.

A recent financial update from Financial Director Kenneth Weldin refused to rule out administration by ‘early to mid December’ unless a solution arrives soon. A fans’ Fighting Fund has raised over £70,000 and might buy a little more time.

The best hope now lies with a consortium of local businessmen led by businessman Raj Samrai and featuring local billionaire Sandy Easdale.

A £150,000-a-year investment package is on offer in return for 60 per cent of the club and ownership of Cappielow.

While there appears to be no great enthusiasm for an offer amongst members of Morton Club Together - the fan ownership group - 427 of the 1200 members voted in favour of a period of due diligence in order to give both parties a chance to work through the structure and terms of a deal. Alternative options are thin on the ground.

Working from an office in Cappielow, the charitable arm of the football club can do nothing but continue to offer help and support for a community which now has one more financial hardship to fret over. Shipyards and heavy industry left Greenock decades ago and the loss of the football club which has played out of Cappielow since 1879 would feel like another savage blow.

“This club has been a big part of my life for 20 years,” says McLaughlin. “My grandad used to be a steward at Cappielow and my dad and his twin brother used to jump the turnstiles.

“Saturday at 5pm is either the best or the worst part of a supporter’s week. And these are difficult times.

“But we can’t control what happens on that side of things.

“What we can control is the work that we do to support the wider community during difficult times.”

A former player with Greenock Juniors, McLaughlin always had a plan to forge a career out of football in some capacity.

After completing a degree in Sport Development he worked as a community coach for Street League before joining his local club as a Development Officer in 2014. In 2018 he was appointed Chief Executive of Morton in The Community, an award winning operation running over 20 initiatives to engage locals aged from 18 months to 80.

Billed as ‘The Heartbeat of Inverclyde’ the ticker of the local club has faltered more than once over the years.

There was a previous brush with administration until the Rae family - local confectioners - stepped in to prop things up and absorb years of losses. A move to community ownership in 2021 saw Morton Club Together assume majority control of a debt free club.

Where other fan owned operations appointed a figurehead, a captain of industry, to run the show Morton rely on volunteers with little or no experience of running a football club. And placing all their eggs in one financial basket proved a calamitous mistake when the Dalrada money dried up.

An investment package by Dutch ownership group Estrella has since been turned down and MCT members now debate why the local consortium should gain a 60% stake in the club and control of Cappielow simply for handing over the same amount of money every month as they do.

Resigned to the fact that fan ownership at Morton in its current form has failed many now fear that rejection of the consortium’s offer would render Greenock Morton the ghost of Christmas past.

“I am optimistic about everything,” says McLaughlin. “What I say to our staff and volunteers is that we need to focus on controlling the controllables.

“We are here to support the club any way we can. Because the club is a key part of the community and we really hope that the future is positive.

“If we do our bit we will be okay and we will continue to support people and I think we will be alright.

“And, touch wood, I think the football club will be okay as well.

“If they can continue to provide the football we will continue to build and provide the projects that Inverclyde needs.”

The needs of Greenock and its surrounding areas are deep-rooted. In the Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation of 2020 Inverclyde was found to be the most deprived data zone in Scotland, with extreme scores for income, employment, health (including drug related hospital admissions), education and crime.

Around half of the region’s population live in the three of the most deprived localities. Workless households are amongst the highest in the UK while premature mortality is in the top 10 across local authorities.

“Inverclyde is top of the league table in so many areas for the wrong reasons,” says McLaughlin.

“At one point we were the covid capital of Scotland.

“We are top in terms of health and poverty rates and child poverty is through the roof.

“As a father that really affects me because I look at my two year old son and wonder what kind of life I want to give him. And what can I do to help other people have a better life?”

While the national game is regarded as a curse on society by some politicians at Holyrood, Morton in the Community are just one of the club charity arms reaching into local areas to improve lives through the power of football. Their work is neither sexy or appreciated and goes largely unseen.

This week marked the 50th anniversary of Morton’s greatest ever player, Andy Ritchie, moving to Greenock from Celtic.

Signing in time for a Wednesday night game against Davie Cooper’s Clydebank in October 1976 his first impressions of Cappielow were unfavourable. An old fire in the home dressing room stunk of gas and he gained the impression that Vikings had just looted the main stand stand and moved to for the escape boats 20 minutes before his arrival.

Viewed as a slightly archaic, shambolic, old school 20th century football stadium, Cappielow hasn’t changed a great deal in the five decades since. And yet, where the old shutters only opened for games and training sessions in days gone by, Morton in the Community have spent the last seven years since 2019 quietly turning the old place into a multi-purpose hub for those who need someone to talk to.

“That time from three to five on a Saturday is so important to a lot of people. But it can’t be the only time a stadium is open,” McLaughlin observes.

“What I really love about this job is that it’s not just dealing with the people who live for the three to five on a Saturday.

“People come in here and say, ‘I’m not a football fan, I have never been in here.’

“And that doesn’t bother me one bit because what happens on the pitch will happen. We can’t control that.

“What we can control is the impact we have on peoples’ lives.

“We can offer them a bit of hope. We can give them a wee bit of direction. Or a bit of support.

“That’s probably the biggest source of satisfaction for me. When I look back and say, ‘you know what? We helped someone today.”

Grassroots football offers weekly sessions. Worried that football is becoming a middle class game, McLaughlin grew up kicking a ball around the streets of Braeside but sees the cost of participation rising to a level parents can no longer meet.

“Football is becoming very expensive now. When I grew up I was fortunate because we played football in the street with a plastic ball using lampposts or bottles for goalposts and a driveway as a pitch.”

For a number of reasons that doesn’t happen now. Inverclyde has some of the highest stats in Scotland for childhood obesity in the under-fives and McLaughlin wants to introduce the kids of the area to movement, physical activity, sport, football, gymnastics via the charity’s nursery programme. To play football any way they can.

“We have delivered free provision for football holiday camps. We have also trialled ‘pay what you can’ for parents to walk in and put a tenner in a bucket, a pound in a bucket or nothing at all.

“And what you find is that the numbers are double or triple what we would normally get.

“We did a background survey asking people if they could afford to pay the normal £12 a day and the overwhelming answer was ‘no. The cost of playing football is a real issue now.”

Football now accounts for just 60 per cent of the charity’s activities they roll out. On a Monday night Team Talk opens the stadium up for those who feel the need to have a cup of a tea, a biscuit and just talk. On a Friday morning they co-host a session with Moving On Inverclyde, a local drug and alcohol recovery charity.

“Last week we had 16 men in a room. Some were 18 some were 50 plus, some had high flying careers, some less so.”

He takes pride in the story of one man who refused to leave his own home for years. He is now one of the charity’s volunteer facilitators, delivering programmes for Scottish Action for Mental Health after completing a degree.

“And all that started with coming to a freezing cold Cappielow on a Monday night for a cup of tea...”

For Inverclyde finding the money to keep the lights on at Cappielow is not just a case saving one of Scotland’s oldest football clubs. For some of the area’s most vulnerable people it’s a lifeline.
