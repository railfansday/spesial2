How Iran’s Pegah Ahangarani Turned Her Family Archives Into a Regime-Challenging Oscar Hopeful

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The director of 'Rehearsals for a Revolution,' the U.K.'s submission for best international feature at the Academy Awards, on 40 years of Iranian history told through her own life — and how an exile built the film with almost no money.

“Cinema is not just part of my life,” begins Pegah Ahangarani. “It’s me, actually.”

The director and actress grew up on film sets. Both of her parents were filmmakers themselves and immersed their children in the Iranian entertainment industry. Ahangarani became a child star, skyrocketing to fame at just 14 in 1999’s The Girl in the Sneakers. “I won a lot of awards,” she smiles to The Hollywood Reporter. But even she couldn’t have predicted the award-winning success of her debut feature-length documentary, Rehearsals for a Revolution. The Cannes-premiering — and L’Œil d’or-winning — project is a poignant reminder of the power of memory. Ahangarani, whose other essay documentaries include shorts I Am Trying to Remember (2021), My Father (2023) and Child Soldier (2024), sketches her entire life story in this latest work. Drawing from personal archives, home videos, street protest footage, newspapers and recorded voices, she retraces more than 40 years of Iran’s turbulent history. From the late ’70s up until the war with the U.S. and Israel, Ahangarani pieces together and narrates intimate recollections from her life — the vanilla scent of her schoolteacher’s hair and desperate search for her uncle in videos from the 1999 Iranian student protests among them — that help to form the portrait of a country shaped by political repression, and in constant hope for a revolution.

Personal is the operative word in this co-production between the Czech Republic and Spain. “I want to tell my audience: ‘Come and sit with me. I want to share everything with you, even my fragility,'” says its director. As daunting as that task was — Ahangarani has already been arrested and spent time in the notorious Evin Prison, and expects not to be able to return to Iran at the risk of a lengthy jail sentence following the release of this film — she remains steadfast in her belief that using family archives is the greatest weapon at her disposal against the regime. “I realized, ‘Okay, these are my materials, these are my tools,'” says Ahangarani. “Because I think these personal archives can stand against the dictatorship, the official version of history that the dictators want us to believe.”

Having left Iran in 2022, Ahangarani shares candid reflections with viewers about the guilt she’s experienced in the wake of this year’s civilian massacres and brutal crackdown on the Woman, Life, Freedom movement back home. From London, where she has been settled since 2023, Ahangarani talks to THR about putting that guilt up on screen — and being selected as the U.K.’s submission for best international feature film at the 2027 Oscars: “When you leave your country, you think you are kind of stateless. You need somewhere to attach. You need a place. You need a land… [Now], there is one place that accepted me.”

You grew up in film and TV. You were a child star. But when did you first realize you’d like to direct films yourself?

I grew up in cinema, yes. All of the crew members were like my family members. I called them aunties and uncles. (Laughs.) I cannot separate myself from cinema. Cinema is not just part of my life — it’s me, actually. I started cinema as my main career when I was 14. I acted in one film, The Girl in the Sneakers, and that film became very successful. I won a lot of awards. I was a teenager, and I was super happy. And then a few years after that, I remember I saw some documentaries and I thought, “OK, I’d like to maybe be a documentary filmmaker.” I made a few documentaries, but they were very naive. It [was] TV-style documentaries. Then I got familiar with [the] essay style in literature, and then I started exploring that style in some documentaries. I saw some Chris Marker documentaries for example, and Jonas Mekas.

I thought maybe I should find that style for myself, so six or seven years ago I made a short documentary with this style. I used personal archives and I used my voiceover as a narration, and I started making very personal documentaries. That film became very successful as well. The New Yorker published that documentary. It premiered at IDFA Film Festival and a lot of festivals after that. And then I made another short documentary, same style, and another one again. And then after those three documentaries, I started making this one.

The archive of film and photos you use in this is just incredible. Is it daunting, or were you nervous, crafting an entire project around such intimate artefacts and stories?

Yes, actually. (Laughs.) I had a lot of concern about it, and not just for this film. […] A few years ago, when I was living in Iran, I went to my grandmother’s house, and I found some old family albums there. And I started looking through the photographs, and then I saw one photo. My mom was there. My father was there. I was there too. I was two or three years old, and I noticed a man who was standing next to my father, but his face had been scratched out. And I asked my grandmother, “Who is this person? Why has his face been scratched out?” She said, “He was your father’s best friend. He was executed after the revolution because he was a political prisoner.” And I realized my grandmother didn’t destroy the whole photo. She kept that photo, just scratched that face. After that shocking moment, I realized that maybe my responsibility as a documentary filmmaker is to find these old family photos, this personal footage, and find these scratched-out faces and bring them back to life, bring them back to the frame, and bring them back to our collective memory.

After that, I thought a lot about these archives, and I realized, ‘Okay, these are my materials, these are my tools.’ Because I think these personal archives can stand against the dictatorship, the official version of history that the dictators want us to believe. From that day, I started thinking about how I should use these materials as my facts. […] If you go deeper [into] one photograph, if you focus on it, you can see the part of our history in that photograph. But that wasn’t easy at all. When I made that short documentary, I sent it to my mother because my mother was one of the characters, my father was one of the characters, and I had a lot of concern because they might be in trouble after that film. But they were okay. I remember one thing: I sent my film to my grandmother, and I told her, “Okay, I made a short documentary based on the photo that you kept for me.” And she said, “Yes, maybe I kept that photo just for you to make this documentary.”

This is a co-production between Spain and the Czech Republic, is that right? What work went into getting this over the line? Because using your own photos, films and narration, it strikes me as incredibly inexpensive documentary.

[It was made] with nothing, actually. (Laughs.) I had a producer [with whom] I made three of my short documentaries, Kaveh Farnam [founder of the Czech Republic’s Media Nest], and he’s an Iranian independent film [producer] outside of Iran. I remember when I was making a previous documentary, I found him, I texted him, and he joined the project. And that one was like this — I didn’t need money because I worked with my personal archives. But I wanted support for distribution [and] things. At that time, I was inside Iran, and I told Mr. Farnum that after this film, if I got arrested or something like that, please publish the film. I needed someone just for publishing. And then when I moved to London, I talked to Mr. Farnum about this feature film, and he said, “Okay, I’m in,” and I didn’t need any money for that. I just needed a small [amount of] money for my editor [Arash Ashtiani]. The Spain company [Fasten Films]… they found me and they wanted me to make another film for them. And then I told them, “I have one film, it’s finished now. I can send it to you, and if you want, you can join this project as well.” Right after they watched the film, they texted me and they told me, “Oh, we love the film. We want to join the project.”

They supported us because we needed some money for the copyright of the music and sound design, and they have a good relationship with a French distributor… Then they sent the film to the Cannes Film Festival, and they have a very good relationship with Cannes because they are French, and everything actually happened after that. But for the process of making this, we didn’t need any money.

One standout part of the documentary, for me, is when you’re scrolling through some of those harrowing images and videos from this year’s protests and you talk about your guilt and trying to compensate for that with the film. What has it been like reckoning with that through these glamorous film festivals?

Everything was very strange because right after the massacre, we were in the process of editing episode five. We wanted to show me [and] my new life as an immigrant, living in exile, and then war happened and massacre happened, and so we didn’t know how we should respond [to] these disasters in our film. That was a very complicated situation because I couldn’t focus on the film. I told my editor, “Cinema doesn’t mean [anything] anymore after all these dead bodies.” And then I realized that, “Okay, I should share all these concerns with my audience because this is the only way I should tell them.”

But I didn’t have any idea that the the film [could] be successful. During the process of making this, it was just me and my editor, Arash, and a little mic, and a very intimate space. I remember the distribution company texted me and told me the director of Cannes, Thierry Frémaux, told us maybe you can be in the official selection. That was very exciting. That day, I just lay down on the sofa in our office… I couldn’t handle this much excitement. We realized we can compete with, for example, Steven Soderbergh, because he had a film in Cannes in the official documentary selection. And in Cannes, they told us, “You won the best documentary prize,” and I think I’m in the middle of some dream or something like that. Not because my film is successful now, but because I try to show these 40 years of Iranian history and this ongoing struggle of the Iranian people, and everybody around the world now can see this struggle.

What do you hope audiences are talking and thinking about after seeing this film?

After the first screening in Cannes, I realized one thing, which was very sweet: When the film finished and people started clapping, many of them came and told me, “We think we love your people as much as you.” I thought, ‘So this is cinema.’ You can show something that people don’t have any idea about, and they can resonate with it, and they can feel this struggle. They can feel [for] your people, which is amazing.

Many people, after watching the film, they think they know me very well [and that] they’re a friend of mine, which is very sweet. I think the film is successful because I could create a sense of intimacy. Through the narration and through the film, I want to tell my audience: “Come and sit with me. I want to share everything with you, even my fragility.”

This film is officially the U.K.’s official Oscar submission for best international feature. What does that mean to you?

That was great news. Not just because of the film, but when you leave your country, you think you are kind of stateless. You need somewhere to attach. You need a place. You need a land. When I left Iran, I thought, “OK, this is just a temporary situation.” And then I realized that no, maybe it’s permanent. I cannot go back anymore. I should accept my new life, and that was very difficult. When the U.K. announced that they picked our film … I had a feeling like I wasn’t stateless anymore. There is one place that accepted me.

I’m sure you miss your family so much, but you don’t envision a future where you can return?

When I left Iran in 2021, I didn’t have any plan to stay outside of Iran. I left my country with just one carry-on to participate in the IDFA Film Festival for my short documentary. And after the second screening, my mother, she was in Iran [and] called me and told me the regime had called her and asked her to interrogate me about the film. She told me, “Stay outside for a while, for one or two months.” And then the “Woman, Life, Freedom” movement happened and I published a video to show my solidarity with that movement, and then my mother told me they sent a letter [saying] there is a court order against me. They seized my apartment and my bank accounts in Iran and I realized that I can’t go back. If I go back, I have to go to jail. I was in jail for one month in solitary [confinement], but if I go back, maybe I could be in jail for six or seven years or something like that. I decided to stay outside because I wanted to make my film.
