The Slow Fade Might Actually Be Worse Than Ghosting

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

When someone's texts get shorter, plans become nonexistent, and you're left wondering what the hell happened.

No one likes being rejected—unless, of course, it’s some kind of kink...in which case, more power to you. But that one-two punch to the gut is only made worse when the person you’re dating doesn’t even give you the common courtesy of honest communication.

And while I’m not going to sit here and advocate for ghosting either—in fact, quite the opposite! We’re all about the anti-ghosting movement—at least the silence provides an answer of sorts, unlike the mind fuck that is the slow fade (aka the fizzle). Unless you’re in a happy, stable relationship (heard of those?) and not dealing with the same complex dating landscape as the rest of us, you’ve likely endured this form of emotional warfare at least once. The texts come fewer and farther between, they’ve stopped initiating plans, and their responses are as stale as week-old bread. It’s a pattern of mixed communication that can lead to a lot of second-guessing.

And though we may hate to admit it to ourselves (a little delusion is fine, right? Don’t tell my therapist I said that), your situationship/casual fling/almost significant other is probably not “drowning in work” or whatever other half-assed excuse they gave you last. It’s more likely they’re just not that into you and don’t have the courage, respect, and ahem, balls to admit it.

And while you—and I, and literally any living, breathing human being—deserve a whole lot more than some emotionally incompetent clown who resorts to this form of immaturity, that doesn’t mean the slow fade isn’t hurtful and difficult to move on from. Without a concrete breakup or explanation as to what went wrong, there’s a lot of room for confusion.

“It’s much more brutal for the person on the receiving end because it leaves them with mixed signals. Rather than having information outright, they’re left to wonder what the other person is thinking, which can cause stress and anxiety and prevent them from moving on,” says licensed marriage and family therapist Emily Simonian, head of clinical learning for Thriveworks.

It’s no small epidemic either. Ghosting’s passive-aggressive cousin (as Hinge dubbed it) has become a familiar post-date communication issue for singles. According to the app’s 2023 LGBTQIA+ DATE Report, 90 percent of those surveyed said they were worried about someone “fizzling,” or slow-fading them, and it’s not just an issue in the LGBTQIA+ community. Another study by Superdrug Online Doctor found that 63 percent of participants admitted to slow-fading someone themselves. Cool cool cool.

“It’s become rampant in dating culture in response to the idea that ghosting is inappropriate or lacks compassion,” Simonian says. “However, the same individuals who might ghost due to lack of communication skills have just found a new way to avoid having difficult conversations while distancing themselves from the negative associations of ghosting.”

So while, yay, good, everyone’s finally getting the message that ghosting is not okay, the slow fade is no better. In fact, some (cough, cough me and the actual licensed therapist here) might even call it worse. It breeds confusion and, as Simonian puts it, “perpetuates the trend of avoiding difficult yet honest conversations in the dating space.” And having those difficult but honest conversations is a valuable skill you’ll need in order to make a relationship work when you’re ready. If you're the slow-fader, these negative communication habits won't exactly serve you in the long run. And they can be really destructive for the person on the receiving end, since being strung along and fed mixed messages can inspire distrust with future partners.

So what do we do to survive this hurtful dating pattern—other than the occasional, healthy cry and regular therapy? Well, for starters, if you’ve participated in the slow fade (or “fizzling,” as it’s also commonly referred to) in the past, it’s time to try a different—ahem, more mature—strategy when ending things with someone.

“The best approach is always compassionate honesty,” Simonian says. “Lead with something positive when you’re having these tough conversations, but be direct. It might be harder to let someone down directly, but it is the most compassionate way to end things.”

TL;DR: Be kind and be honest, because choosing anything but that approach is only going to make things more difficult for all parties involved. And while you can’t control another person’s actions—and as a result, you also can’t necessarily avoid the dreaded slow fade from happening to you—you can be accountable for not creating that confusion on your own end.

If your situationship is too chicken to have a real convo and is instead offering lame one-word replies, you can initiate a chat instead of just enduring (read: crying into a pint of Ben & Jerry’s) the energy shift.

“There isn’t really a way to avoid getting slow-faded,” Simonian adds. “However, you can take control of the situation and ask directly whether or not the person wants to continue dating to avoid being left in limbo.”

In other words, use the maturity they couldn’t muster to pull yourself out of the gray area. You deserve clarity, and they deserve to stew in their own discomfort for attempting to slow-fade you in the first place. Hey, a good ol’ fashioned dumping is timeless. Let’s bring that back and stop with this immature, indirect approach.
