Saying a human reviews it is not a human-oversight design

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

For a long time I answered the oversight question with a sentence: "a human reviews the output before it goes out." It sounded responsible. It was almost content-free, and it took an uncomfortable review meeting for me to see that.

The follow-ups were ordinary: who is that human, what authority do they have, and how do they stop it. I had a role label, not a design. Under the EU AI Act, Article 14 requires high-risk systems to be designed for effective oversight and Article 26(2) requires deployers to assign it to competent people. Both describe a control path, not a noun in an architecture diagram.

The phrase hides three things that have to exist independently: the person, the mechanism by which they intervene or stop the system, and the conditions under which they are expected to use it. Most systems have the first, a vague second, and nothing for the third. Article 14 is written from the provider's side: the system must be built so a human can understand its limits, notice anomalies, interpret output, decide not to use it, and halt it. Article 26(2) is the deployer's mirror: assign oversight to natural persons with real competence, training, and authority. As a deployer you inherit both problems, since you cannot design the provider's system but you can be honest about whether the handover gives your named person anything real to do.

A table exposed the gaps. Column one: a named overseer, not "the team", with competence and authority written down. Column two: an override or stop path, meaning a specific action and the state the system enters when it is taken. Column three: the guards against bias, meaning conditions that force a genuine decision rather than a reflexive click. That third column is where you admit the review might be theatre, and it is the only one that changes behaviour: sample approved outputs for a second look, require a reason on override, make "stop" a button that is tested rather than a procedure in a document.

I built OversightKit to produce that table as a draft. You paste how you use AI, and it returns an Article 14 / 26(2) human-oversight design draft: a readiness read, named roles, an override and stop path, bias guards, and the gaps where no overseer is named. That is the whole feature set. It does not install a control, train staff, or observe whether anyone clicks the button.

OversightKit writes down a design. The named person, the intervention mechanism, the escalation path, the training, and the authority to halt the system are still things an organisation has to put in place. A document saying "a named overseer approves high-risk outputs" changes nothing until there is a person, a button, and a consequence. The named-overseer gap is the part I find most useful: a thin description comes back with roles that have no name attached, and "the compliance team" is not a name. Friction is the point of oversight, but it costs throughput, and the right review level is a judgement for the people accountable for the system.

This is a decision-support tool. It is not legal advice, it is not a certification, and it is not a live audit harness. It does not control your system, train anyone, or decide whether Articles 14 and 26 apply to you. That depends on high-risk classification under the Act, including the Annex III use cases, and on whether you are a provider, a deployer, or both. If your system is not high-risk, most of this design work does not apply.
