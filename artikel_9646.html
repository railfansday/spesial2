Your Coding Agent Shouldn't Be Able to Close Its Own Ticket

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Posted on Nov 16 • Originally published at arbituro.com

Would you let a developer approve their own pull request?

GitHub won't. Open a review on your own pull request and approving it simply isn't an option. And nobody takes that as an insult to developers. It's just how review works: the person who wrote the change isn't the person who says it's good.

So why do so many of us let coding agents close their own tickets? 🤔

You know how it goes. You hand the agent a ticket. It works away for a while. Then:

And the ticket moves to Done. Sometimes because the agent moved it. Sometimes because you did, on its say-so, at six on a Friday evening. 😅 Either way, the only evidence the work is finished is the word of the thing that did the work.

I think that's backwards. In this post I'll argue that an agent should be able to claim a ticket is done, with evidence, but never decide it. Then I'll show what that split looks like in practice.

Full disclosure: I make Arbituro, a work tracker built on exactly this split. The argument holds whatever tracker you use, and the Arbituro bit is near the end.

Here's the thing about a coding agent: it's trying to finish. That's not a flaw, it's the job. Every step it takes heads towards the moment it can say "done" and stop.

So for an agent, "done" isn't just a status. It's the finish line. And if the agent also gets to decide where the finish line is, you get exactly the incentive you'd expect. 🙃

None of this needs bad intent. It's what happens whenever the one being measured also does the measuring. We worked that out for people a long time ago. It's why code review exists, why auditors aren't the accountants, and why your own approval on your own pull request doesn't count.

The second problem: an agent reports success in the same cheerful tone whether it succeeded or not.

If you've worked with one for a while, you've probably seen some version of these:

Every one of them arrives as "Implemented and verified. ✅" The sentence is identical. Only the evidence would tell them apart, and the sentence has none.

So here's the fix, and it's simple: two different actors, two different verbs.

The agent claims. When it believes a criterion is met, it says so, with what it checked: the command it ran, the page it opened, what it saw.

Someone else decides. A CI check, for anything a machine can verify. A named person, for anything that needs judgement.

That's the whole idea. The agent keeps all of its speed. It writes the code, opens the pull request, runs the tests and makes its case. It just doesn't mark its own work. ✍️

Here's a claim from Arbituro's demo workspace. The criterion is "Sam is happy with the wording on the account page", and the agent wrote:

Rendered the account page on the preview with a test subscription: "Add a cold brew four-pack to this delivery, $19.95" sits under Next delivery, and the confirmation reads "Added. It comes with your coffee on Tuesday."

Sam can decide that in one press. Met, or Not met with a reason. Either way it's Sam's call, and it's on the record.

Notice what the claim doesn't do: it doesn't say the wording is good. It can't. Only Sam knows whether that's what Sam wanted. 💬

This split doesn't mean a human clicks through every ticket. Most criteria on a code change can be decided by CI:

When the check passes on the pull request's latest commit, the criterion is met. When it fails, it isn't. Nobody presses anything. 🤖

People are for the rest: wording, design, whether the change solves the customer's problem. Name one person per criterion. "The team" never decides anything.

Writing criteria that a check or a person can decide is its own skill, so I wrote it up separately: Writing Acceptance Criteria an AI Agent Can Actually Meet.

The same trap shows up one level higher. Plenty of setups close the ticket automatically when its pull request merges.

But a merge only says the code is in. It says nothing about "a four-pack can be bought on a phone", or whether the customers understood the new page, or the email that still has to go out. Those are exactly the criteria the code alone can't show.

A merge should ask the person who signs the job off to finish it, not finish it for them.

You might have a few objections by now. Here are the ones I think matter.

"It's slower." For a criterion a check decides, nobody waits on anybody: it's decided the moment the check finishes. For one a person decides, it's one press against written evidence. Compare that with the cost of a wrong "done": the bug report, the reopen, and the context everyone has lost by then.

"People will just rubber-stamp it." Some will. But a rubber stamp has a name on it now. And next to a claim with real evidence, "Implemented and verified" looks exactly as thin as it is. 😬

"Agents will get good enough that this won't matter." They'll be right more often, for sure. But "done" isn't only about correctness. It's whether the work is what the person who asked for it actually wanted, and no agent gets to decide that for them, however good it gets.

"My agent works as me anyway." That's exactly the problem. Over MCP, an agent usually works with your permissions. So if you can move a ticket to Done, so can it. A line in the prompt asking it nicely not to is a request, not a permission. The fix has to live in what the tools allow. 🔒
