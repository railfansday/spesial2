British intelligence agency misplaced 'thousands' of storage devices, years later still can't say how many

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Other agency errors included an unchecked software bug that led to Terrorism Act detentions

Thousands of GCHQ storage drives containing sensitive data have been lost for years, and the British signals intelligence agency still cannot explain where they are.

According to the Investigatory Powers Commissioner’s Office’s (IPCO) annual report, the missing storage drives have been known to officials for years, and may contain data related to the Investigatory Powers Act (IPA).

"I remain concerned that GCHQ has not yet established a sufficiently clear understanding of the scale, location, and recoverability of lost removable media that may contain IPA-related data, notwithstanding that the underlying issues were first drawn to my attention several years ago," wrote investigatory powers commissioner Lord Brian Leveson.

"While I recognize the technical and practical challenges involved, the passage of time without a comprehensive account heightens the importance of sustained senior focus and assured remediation. Given the potential sensitivity of the material involved, I will continue to monitor progress closely.”

Described as "a very significant number," the report notes that GCHQ has scoped the maximum number of potentially lost drives, but is unaware of the exact scale of the issue.

The agency has embarked on "a program of further work" to locate as many of the lost devices as possible, which includes implementing an automated asset-tracking system by the end of the year. After this is up and running, it said it will be able to provide a more precise number of lost drives.

A GCHQ spokesperson told The Register: "As IPCO have reported, our management system for removable media was previously not sufficiently robust to effectively track the items that were registered for use. IPCO are right to note the flaws in the data the system produced, and we have taken action against all their recommendations.

"We recognize the importance of security of removable media in our systems, and have a range of measures to mitigate the risks posed by these items. The findings in the report have been addressed comprehensively, and all necessary steps to correct this issue have been taken."

Peter Sommer, digital forensics expert and visiting professor at de Montfort and Birmingham City universities, told us:"Bearing in mind the sort of highly sensitive data collected and processed by GCHQ IPCO is right to be very concerned, the more so as the issue is already several years old. Vast quantities of data can be stored on the cheapest lowest cost hard disks and cards. At a much more modest level as an expert witness I know how important it is to keep careful track of the data entrusted to me."

Losing important devices is not an achievement that's exclusive to GCHQ. Myriad stories like this have emerged from all corners of the public sector over the years, such as the British Minidstry of Defence misplacing USB drives containing secret information in the mid-noughties, civil servants routinely losing their BlackBerry handsets, and UK police losing thousands of devices, too.

The missing storage drives comprise just one of the agency's various transgressions highlighted by IPCO in its report.

There are also "significant concerns" over the lax safeguards used to restrict the UK's overseas partners when accessing bulk-intercepted data collected by GCHQ.

IPCO was satisfied at how GCHQ ensured its own analysts remained compliant with safeguards around accessing confidential material related to journalism, journalistic sources,  and privileged legal information.

However, there were concerns around how these same safeguards were applied to its international partners, which have access to its so-called bulk data interception "product."

The report notes that most partners were properly restricted from accessing such material, although an internal probe found that there were inadequate arrangements in respect to one partner and their access to journalistic material.

Both GCHQ and the overseas agency committed to rectifying the situation.

GCHQ was also questioned after a software bug in a web app "used by a relatively small section of the public" misidentified individuals thought to be participating in terrorist activities.

The agency identified and reported the flaw in the software, which it used for "target discovery work," and although it only affected a small number of individuals, the errors led to five port stops made under the Terrorism Act and caused "serious prejudice" to the victims.

A port stop, for the uninitiated, involves law enforcement detaining a suspect at a UK port, airport, train station, or within one mile of the Northern Ireland border, and can be carried out without suspicion.

Once detained, individuals have a legal responsibility to provide passwords to their electronic devices, which can be seized for up to seven days, and in some cases have their biometric markers taken.

Failure to comply can lead to a three-month prison term.

IPCO ruled, however, that there was ultimately no error made as defined by the IPA.

“This is because the activity was lawfully authorised under an interception warrant and the adoption of an unintentionally and unforeseeably imperfect target discovery methodology did not constitute such an error,” the report stated.

Despite the agency’s vast technical expertise, IPCO said that the issue affecting the software was not foreseeable.

GCHQ was not the only offender among the UK intelligence community (UKIC), with MI5's mishandling of the infamous Agent X case taking center stage in the report. Agent X was an anonymous MI5 informant whose real identity has never been publicly released. A secret inquiry by IPCO as well as the report revealed that MI5 managed Agent X despite knowing he was a violent misogynist obsessed with extreme violence.

Lord Leveson said "the case of Agent X is as serious as anything I have dealt with as Commissioner," after MI5 gave false evidence to the High Court and Investigatory Powers Tribunal to protect a foreign neo-Nazi informant who was under police investigation for abusing his partner, referred to publicly as "Beth." ®
