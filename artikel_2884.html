30 years later, Lenny Brown relives Crosstown Shootout buzzer-beater

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

WILMINGTON, Del. − The sweat kept coming, even inside the hotel.

Lenny Brown used a white towel to wipe his face as he sat in a Hilton Hotel lobby off Concord Pike, the busy regional thoroughfare that runs from his hometown of Wilmington to the Pennsylvania state line.

Outside, temperatures topped triple digits amid a statewide extreme heat advisory.

Brown wore a black Nike Xavier basketball T-shirt and jean shorts. It was an otherwise ordinary scene for a conversation about an extraordinary moment in Xavier basketball history.

On Nov. 26, 1996, Brown's 13-foot jumper as time expired lifted Xavier to a 71-69 victory over No. 1-ranked Cincinnati, stunning the Bearcats in the Crosstown Shootout at Shoemaker Center and delivering one of the most memorable wins in the rivalry's history.

The shot altered Brown's everyday life and the trajectory of his basketball career.

"It changed everything," Brown said. "It was like I belong now. I have arrived."

Thirty years later, the moment that made Brown a household name among Xavier fans remains vivid, from the chaos of the final seconds to the celebration that followed.

As Brown revisits that night, he'll watch the final few minutes of the game again − from the crunch-time huddles, shocking moments and the shot that etched his name in Xavier history. He says he's never watched the whole game, just the final five minutes. He usually sees it each winter when the Crosstown Shootout comes around.

He admits he feels old as the tape begins to play, but does it ever get old talking about it?

The Crosstown Shootout had already established itself as one of college basketball's top rivalries by the time Brown and the Musketeers traveled to face the Bearcats early in the 1996-97 season.

Brown experienced that intensity firsthand as a freshman just 10 months earlier, when Cincinnati (ranked No. 3) came to Cincinnati Gardens and won, 99-90, behind Danny Fortson's 40 points and 17 rebounds. The atmosphere stuck with him.

"The first thing I remember was the crowd," Brown said. "It was crazy. The city was really red and blue. The excitement leading up to that game was unreal."

The rematch carried even greater stakes. Cincinnati, led by Fortson and a physical, high-flying roster, entered as the nation's top-ranked team.

"We'd run into those guys in the summer. We were all looking forward to the game," Brown said.

Xavier, coming off its first losing season in 14 years, would face Bob Huggins' Bearcats at the relatively new Shoemaker Center, where they were 0-3.

"It was hostile, but I enjoyed it," Brown said. "I loved playing on the road and their fans were great. They gave it to us. We were ready."

Head coach Skip Prosser's message to his team was simple. The Musketeers had to block out the noise and play their game.

"Cincinnati was crazy. You can dress it up however you want, but it's just basketball," Brown said. "I can't say that I knew we were gonna win. I just knew it was gonna be a hell of a game."

The game eventually came down to a handful of possessions, and a shot that would become part of the rivalry's lore.

Xavier trailed by five with less than 90 seconds remaining before T.J. Johnson's putback trimmed UC's lead to 68-65 with 1:17 left.

On UC's ensuing possession, Fortson fouled out with 41 seconds to go while chasing a long rebound.

"He was a bull. He was strong. Anything around the paint he's finishing," Brown said. "We were happy he was out of there."

James Posey made two free throws to pull the Musketeers within one, and Cincinnati's Darnell Burton split a pair at the line to make it 69-67.

The final minute of regulation took eight minutes of real time. There were several intense stoppages, but Xavier had a calming presence on its bench in Prosser.

"Like a Buddhist," Brown said. "Everything could be on fire around him, and he'd just look at you, 'we'll be fine.'"

Xavier's Gary Lumpkin missed a game-tying shot attempt, but the ball went out of bounds on Cincinnati. The Musketeers kept possession, and Posey slipped undetected behind the Bearcats defense for a layup that tied the game 69-69 with 6.7 seconds remaining.

Cincinnati had a chance to win it, but guard Charles Williams dribbled the ball off his foot and out of bounds near halfcourt, putting Xavier in the driver's seat.

"To this day, I'm like, 'how?'" Brown said while watching Xavier celebrate the sudden change of possession. "He is a good ball-handler, a good point guard. I understand he was frustrated. That would've killed me. It's one of those things like, 'what happened?'"

Coming out of a timeout, Brown expected to run the play called inside the huddle. He remembered the ball might go inside to teammate Torraye Braggs, with the possibility of Brown or Lumpkin coming off a screen if the first option was unavailable.

Then assistant coach Jeff Battle grabbed Brown's arm as the team broke the huddle.

"Go get the f*****g ball," Battle told him.

"I look back like, 'are you serious?'" Brown recalled.

He was still debating whether to run the play or get the ball himself when the referee handed it to Xavier out of bounds.

"That last split second," Brown said. "I'm going to get the ball."

Brown took the inbounds pass and dribbled toward the left wing, with Burton defending him. He crossed over to his right and drove inside the free-throw line. As Cincinnati's Rodrick Monroe stepped up to help, Brown rose and released a jumper.

The ball went through just as the clock hit triple zero. Xavier had stunned No. 1 Cincinnati, 71-69.

"I knew I had a good chance," Brown said. "I ain't gonna say I knew it was going in, but there was a good chance. That's something you practice every day."

Then came the celebration. Brown sprinted toward the UC bench before being mobbed by his teammates, a deliberate celebrating in front of the crowd he had spent the game trying to silence.

"It fueled me. That's what I used to love, making the whole stadium quiet," Brown said. "They hated me."

The celebration continued long after the final buzzer. Brown remembered Xavier's players going wild while Prosser remained calm, picking up water bottles amid the commotion.

When the team made the short trip back to campus, fans and cheerleaders filled the parking lot. Friends popped champagne and poured it on the players as they stepped off the bus.

"We definitely stopped at Dana Gardens, then we went downtown somewhere. We were just rolling," Brown said. "I don't think I went to sleep."

The shot followed him into everyday life. People recognized him at restaurants and shopping malls, eager to talk about the game-winner.

Thirty years later, the moment still brings pride. When told that many consider his shot the most famous in Xavier history, Brown paused to reflect.

"It's humbling," he said. "I appreciate that. I love Xavier, man. I love it. A lot of people still remember that shot and bring it up to this day, and it makes me feel good. I'm proud of that."

The rivalry has produced plenty of memorable games and performances before and after Brown's shining moment. But for Xavier fans, Brown's shot remains a defining image, from the ball leaving his hand, the mayhem that ensued on the Shoemaker Center court and radio announcer Andy MacWilliams' famous call.

"The UC Bearcats are No. 1 in the country, No. 2 in their own city!"

Brown recites it word-for-word, then chuckles: "That was classic."

Brown has lived many chapters since that night. At 51, he's now a grandfather of five, a life stage he finds hard to believe. Yet the memory of those final seconds remains close, especially as he rewatches his younger self hit the biggest shot of his career.

"Memories, memories, memories," Brown said. "Man, that was a hell of a night."
