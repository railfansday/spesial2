That sounds rather interesting -- what should I know about quantum optics?

My impression is that quantum optics is a great subject. It's a big subject, but the small parts of it, which I got to study, are very fun. In general, quantum optics just refers to a quantum mechanical theory of light. But it seems to me that courses in quantum optics often focus on the interaction between light and matter.

A basic model for the interaction between light and matter is that of a classical electric field interacting with a two-level system. This is usually referred to as a semiclassical model, because the electric field (ie the light) is not modeled using quantum operators. Still, the things you can learn from this model are widely applicable because, for example, laser light can be modeled classically. A two-level system is a quantum system that features two quantum states, which have different energies. If you know some atom physics, you will recall that electrons in atoms can occupy different states with different energy levels. That's why you can model an atom as a two-level system if you assume that there is just one electron in the atom and that the electron will only ever occupy two energy levels in the atom (in reality, there are infinitely many energy levels, but this crude model still provides interesting insights).

If you know some quantum mechanics and find this interesting, then I can recommend Daniel Steck's Quantum and Atom Optics. The dynamics of a two-level system interacting with a classical field are discussed in chapter 5, which you can just read on its own if you know introductory quantum mechanics. One thing that you need that's--to my knowledge-- not always taught in introductory courses is the interaction picture of quantum mechanics. This is discussed in chapter 4.2 of Steck's book.

Link to the book: https://atomoptics.uoregon.edu/~dsteck/teaching/quantum-optics/

[1] It's just a set of (mutable location, value) pairs with a special "swap" bytecode instruction that does what you might expect and returns a new set. It's otherwise generic but in this case keeps track of the program counter and call stack.
