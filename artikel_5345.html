3 forces moving SPCX stock on Tesla merger talk that has no terms

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Dilution fears, merger arbitrage and S&P 500 hopes all move SPCX stock, yet each rests on Tesla deal terms that no one has set.

Investors are trading SpaceX shares around a possible merger with Tesla $TSLA whose price and structure nobody has set. SpaceX's amended IPO filing warned that the company may sell a large amount of new stock to pay for future deals, according to CNBC, and investors have read that warning with Tesla in mind. Every trade in SPCX that turns on the merger is a bet on terms that do not yet exist.

The people closest to the deal speak warmly about the idea but have not committed to it. "There's no question that there are synergies between Tesla and SpaceX in our futures," Gwynne Shotwell, SpaceX's president and COO, told CNBC. Both companies have set up board committees to weigh a deal, yet no transaction has won the backing of either board. Elon Musk also cannot push a deal through on his own. CNBC reports that Musk has acknowledged shareholders would need to approve a merger first, and he is barred from taking part directly in the negotiations of those committees. Until that process produces terms, every number attached to a Tesla-SpaceX merger is an assumption, and investors should weigh each claim about SPCX with that in mind.

Yahoo Finance has laid out why SPCX shares have been moving amid the Tesla merger talks, from traders betting against the stock to the hope that a deal could open the door to the S&P 500. Shareholders have already paid for the uncertainty. SpaceX priced its IPO at $135 per share, according to CNBC. SPCX traded as low as $119.51 on Nov. 25 after a one-day drop of 5.01%, so anyone who got shares at the IPO price is now holding a loss. Most of the arguments for buying or selling the stock depend on the shape of a deal, meaning a price and a share swap that no one has set. Each argument is only as solid as the assumption beneath it. Some of the pressure on SPCX would remain even if no merger happened. Investors who separate the two kinds of pressure can tell which worries would likely fade once terms emerge and which would stay.

What worries SPCX holders most is not a merger itself but the number of new shares SpaceX might have to issue if it paid for Tesla $TSLA in stock. Yahoo Finance ranks that fear first among the forces weighing on the stock. Gary Black, managing partner of The Future Fund, estimated in a post on X $TWTR that a Tesla deal could mean roughly 52% more SpaceX shares. A possible acquisition of Cursor, by contrast, would dilute existing holders by 3.4% measured at the IPO valuation, according to CNBC. For anyone who holds SPCX, a Tesla deal on the scale Black describes would leave each existing share representing a much smaller part of the company.

The 52% figure describes one possible scenario, and its size depends on the inputs Black chose. His estimate assumes SpaceX pays a 25% premium for Tesla. A smaller premium would require fewer new shares, and a larger one would require more. The estimate also assumes an all-stock deal, so if SpaceX paid any part of the price in another form, it would issue fewer shares. Investors are right that a stock deal would dilute their holdings, but the size of that dilution depends on two assumptions that no one has confirmed.

The public holders who would share the dilution have little say in the company. Musk controls 82.4% of SpaceX's voting power after the IPO, according to CNBC, which leaves outside shareholders with a small share of the votes. SPCX buyers can factor the dilution risk into the price they pay, but their votes carry little weight against his.

SPCX holders should treat dilution as a cost they may have to accept rather than a decision they control.

Part of the selling in SPCX comes from SpaceX's role in the hypothetical deal, not from any judgment about its rocket business. In the merger investors are imagining, SpaceX would be the acquirer. Traders who bet on mergers buy the company being acquired and short the buyer, so SPCX lands on the side being sold whatever SpaceX's own prospects look like. Those traders keep selling SPCX until terms emerge, and no one has offered any. The selling rests on a deal structure that doesn't exist yet, and it says nothing about how SpaceX runs its business.

The traders are betting on a gap that no one can measure. The two companies haven't agreed how Tesla $TSLA stock would convert into SpaceX stock, so traders have no locked-in profit to aim for, only a guess at what the terms might turn out to be. Regulatory scrutiny could also block a SpaceX-Tesla merger, according to CNBC. Closing is expected to take at least 12 months, Yahoo Finance reports, so arbitrage traders would carry the risk of a blocked deal for at least a year even after terms appear. SPCX holders shouldn't expect the selling to end neatly once terms are announced.

Arbitrage can't explain every drop in SPCX. SpaceX shares were down about 9% one Thursday afternoon, and Tesla shares about 11%, during a broad sell-off in tech stocks, according to CNBC. Tesla fell even though arbitrage traders are buyers of its stock, and the sell-off reached well beyond the two companies, so SPCX holders lost value that day for reasons that went beyond the merger trade.

The political backdrop adds pressure that a deal wouldn't remove. "While policy is naturally hard to predict, we could envision SpaceX facing some incremental headwinds in this type of backdrop," Deutsche Bank analysts said in a note, as reported by CNBC. That kind of weakness would remain whether or not SpaceX and Tesla ever combine.

Only the arbitrage part of SPCX's slide depends on the merger's terms, and the rest is likely to stay after those terms arrive.

SPCX holders have one argument for buying rather than selling. A merger with Tesla $TSLA, already a member of the S&P 500, could put SpaceX on a fast track into the index and potentially set off buying by index funds, according to JPMorgan $JPM analysts, as reported by Yahoo Finance. The analysts describe a possible effect of a deal that no one has agreed to, so the upside can be no more real than the merger itself. Investors who expect a shortcut point to SpaceX's size, but the company that runs the index has already turned down that argument. S&P Global $SPGI said, as reported by CNBC, that "exceptions to the financial viability, seasoning, and IWF (investable weight factor) requirements should not be granted solely based on market capitalization."

SpaceX can't qualify on its own because it loses money. The company lost $4.94 billion in 2025, according to CNBC, and Art Hogan, chief market strategist at B. Riley Wealth, praises S&P for insisting that companies earn a profit before they join. That loss is why investors treat the merger as a back door. A deal could also let SpaceX skip the 12-month wait that a newly listed stock normally serves before it can join. S&P has not said how it would treat SpaceX as part of a company already in the index, so holders are betting on a ruling no one has made. A possible index lift that depends on an unapproved deal and an open rules question is a weak reason to buy SPCX today.

Index funds will have reasons to buy SpaceX that don't involve Tesla. The Nasdaq $NDAQ 100 has already changed its rules to ease entry for the largest companies, CNBC reports, and S&P plans to modify entry rules for the S&P Total Market Index and the Dow Jones U.S. Total Stock Market Index to give SpaceX a path in. S&P is holding its line only at the flagship S&P 500.

The index buying that is coming doesn't depend on Tesla, and the part that does is the least certain piece of the story.
