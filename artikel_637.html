Rosamund Pike On Starring in Spiky Stage Drama ‘Inter Alia’: “I Like to Do Things That Are Provocative”

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

The actress makes her Broadway debut in the role, which sees her as a powerful criminal court judge who is "shaken to her core."

Before each theatrical run of Inter Alia, Rosamund Pike spends time in court.

She sits in on criminal cases to get a better sense of how the British legal system works, with a particular eye to how female judges act. In one instance, Pike heard a judge reprimand counsel with a stern “I have ruled on that,” and asked playwright Suzie Miller to add that into the script to help give her own character more authenticity and authority.

In preparing to play Jessica Parks, a judge juggling criminal court cases as well as worrying about and taking care of her teenage son, Pike also met with the female judges, as well as with 18-year-old boys to learn how they speak to one another and how they view women. Together, all of these learnings have stayed under the surface for Pike, and helped her bring forward a layered, lived-in portrayal as she approaches the role for a third time and makes her Broadway debut.

“I wanted there to be humor. I met so many wise, witty, thoughtful, kind female judges. I really wanted to represent them. I wanted it to be as nuanced as we could make it. I wanted the teenage life to be recognizable and current,” Pike said. “And I also, I suppose, the other big thing is I wanted Jessica to fall on her sword. I wanted her to be so shaken to her core the moral compass she felt was absolute no longer is. “

Pike first took on the role during the play’s world premiere at London’s National Theatre in 2025. At the time, it had been close to 15 years since the Oscar-nominee had been on stage, after last appearing in Hedda Gabler. She wanted to return in a “modern play that really speaks to now” and finally found that in Miller’s Inter Alia, which shows a woman whose prowess at work adjudicating sexual assault cases and speaking up for women is called into question when her own son is accused of rape.

“I like to do things that are provocative. I like to do things where people feel glad that they went to the theater, that they got something out of it emotionally, and that they felt something. I go to the theater because I want to feel something, and I want to think differently about things, and then I thought, ‘Well, I think this play might do that,’” said Pike, who is also known for her on-screen roles in Gone Girl, Saltburn and Pride and Prejudice.

The play, which had a subsequent West End run before heading to Broadway, is meant to be a kind of sister play to Prima Facie, Miller’s one-woman show which starred Jodie Comer in the West End and on Broadway in 2023. That play followed Comer as a defense barrister who begins by defending men accused of sexual assault and then confronts how the legal system treats women once she is a victim of assault.

While Pike has two fellow actors on stage with her, playing her son and her husband, who is at times loving, and, at times, jealous of her rise at work, much of the show relies on Pike ping-ponging between the naturalistic settings of her home life and speaking to unseen fellow lawyers and others and monologuing to the audience about motherhood and the challenges of raising a son in modern society, as well as revisiting the memory of a former incident with her son as a young child.

“It’s a very unusual play, and I think that’s what Susie handled so deftly was the different sort of threads of a woman’s life and the bits you have narrative control over, and the bits you don’t have narrative control over,” Pike said.

And so while the play has a lot of physical demands, including Pike kicking off the show as the leader of a rock band, Pike notes that it’s been the mental workload that’s been particularly taxing, as her character switches between the naturalistic and imagined worlds.

The Broadway run began previews Nov. 11, ahead of a Dec. 1 opening. Pike took home a 2026 Olivier Award for her London performance. Combining her research with her experience doing the prior runs has allowed her to move past the technical aspects of the play, like lines and the blocking, into a more fully realized experience.

“Every step of the way, she has surprised me, made me laugh. She’s incredibly naughty, but fun. She works as hard as anyone I’ve ever met, and she’s sort of relentless in going ‘I want to make this show mean the most it can mean,’” said director Justin Martin, who has been with the play since the beginning.

Her role, and the play itself evolves, he added, in relation to current events.

Speaking ahead of her first preview, news was swirling about an alleged sexual assault in 2024 at a Cornell University fraternity house, which would be the kind of case to come before Pike’s character in the play, and in keeping with the subjects the play raises.

Pike noted that audiences can’t help but bring events like this into the theater with them, which influences their experience of the play. While the text of the play itself doesn’t change, the newspapers on the family’s kitchen table on set show current headlines, which also feeds into their performances and her portrayal as a judge.

“I think our play affects people very deeply because of all the themes it discusses about family, about motherhood, about raising boys, about responsibility of boys. We’re not going to materially change the play in any way, but that’s what great art can do,” Pike said. “The arts can shed a light or hold a mirror up to our world in a way that is sometimes more truthful and allows us to access those feelings, because sometimes the real event is too close or too confusing for us to actually access an emotional response.”

From her own time in British courts, Pike said she was struck by the way in which she saw a legal system set up to test and sometimes undermine the stories of women who come forward with a rape case, which ends up being another traumatic experience for victims. This play, from Miller, a former lawyer, may help point to the flaws in the system, she notes, in addition to its broader themes about how to bring up boys in modern society.

“I’ve sat in court and watched so many men be found not guilty of sexual assault,” Pike said. “I think Susie Miller always wants to raise a conversation within the law.”
