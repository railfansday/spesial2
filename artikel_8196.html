Midlayers have an outrageously demanding job description. Unlike the super-puffy parkas and belay jackets that we wear to stay warm while hunkering down, midlayers provide insulation during activity—a role that calls for a true multi-tasker. Its duties include keeping us warm while venting excess heat, whisking away sweat, and shielding us from the elements. It’s a tall order, yet our testing team found six insulated midlayer jackets that check all the boxes.

These breathable insulators successfully regulated testers’ temperatures while they skied, hiked, biked, and ran in winter weather conditions. Testers typically sandwiched these jackets between a base layer and a waterproof shell. On climbs, or in dry weather when they ditched the shell, testers also evaluated these toppers for their ability to shed precipitation and buffer gusts.

Pros and Cons + Balanced performance + Extended sizes + Lots of pockets – Lacks abrasion resistance

The Atom has been one of our favorite midlayers since its debut in 2009, and its design has hardly changed over the years except for sustainability upgrades. With stretchy fleece side and underarm panels bridging generous helpings of synthetic insulation in the torso, hood, and arms, it strikes the ideal balance of warmth and breathability for cold weather conditions. We found the Atom’s insulated panels—packed with 60 grams of polyester loft—plenty warm down to the teens without an outer layer while skinning and bootpacking in the Tetons. One tester, who wore his hoody ice climbing in Silverton, Colorado, called the Atom’s mix of insulation and breathable fleece “perfectly balanced.”

The unconfining cut fits most bodies, small or large, and two hand pockets and one internal chest pocket secure valuables. The cinchable hood is big enough for a helmet, which came in handy when temps plummeted during one Idaho-based tester’s fat-bike ride in the Tetons. The fabric’s fluorocarbon-free DWR finish repelled drizzle during late-November training runs. But the Atom delivers too much insulation for mild conditions and shoulder seasons (at 35 degrees and above, consider the Atom SL.) And although the breathable fabric feels soft next to skin, it gets pilly after repeated encounters with pack straps and other abrasive tools.

Selecting a midlayer is all about finding the sweet spot for your activity and weather conditions. Unlike heavy down hoodies and parkas meant for sitting around camp or belaying ice climbers, midlayers need to keep you warm while also offering breathability.

The warmth-to-breathability ratio is key: In general, a great midlayer should keep you warm, but not so warm that you overheat and soak it with sweat. Protection from wind and precipitation is a bonus, but midlayers are often paired with a waterproof shell in bad weather, so finding that magic warmth-breathability balance should be your priority.

Synthetic fabric and insulation are key to almost all midlayers. Fleece-backed jackets, with their porous fibers, offer the best breathability, but can often be too thin and permeable for very cold or windy conditions. Hybrid or “body-mapped” designs that blend baffled panels with other fabrics are excellent when working up a sweat in colder conditions. Minimally vented synthetic insulated jackets provide the most warmth at the cost of breathability, although they are still a better choice than sweating it out in a down puffy.

Here’s how to decide which midlayer is right for you:

If you are planning to work up a lather, look for breathable layers that will keep you from getting clammy, which can make you hypothermic if you stop moving or encounter big winds. Fleece-backed jackets, like the Stio Dawner (which uses loosely knit Octa fibers), are king when it comes to breathability. If conditions are too windy or frigid, however, ramping up to a midlayer with some synthetic fill, like the Arc’teryx Atom, is a better choice.

Down-filled layers have the best warmth-to-weight ratio when it comes to outdoor gear, but quickly lose that advantage if you’re sweating from the inside or getting soaked from the outside. Synthetic fill (used in most midlayers reviewed here) and fleece-backed jackets keep you warm when wet. So does wool (the Ortovox Ravine Metawool 90), though it does absorb water over time and becomes a soggier, heavier insulator.

If you know it’s going to be frigid or you’ll be moving slow, by all means, go for a thicker puffy like the Marmot WarmCube Active Novus. But if you’re moving fast in typical winter temps, chances are that much insulation will be overkill. It’s not a bad idea to pack two midlayers, or at least include a fleece in your kit, to be prepared for changing temperatures and exertion levels.

If you plan to wear a wind- and waterproof shell over your midlayer, you won’t need to worry about the storm cutting right down to your base layers. If not, look for a piece that offers some wind and water resistance, like the Ortovox Ravine Metawool 90 or Arc’teryx Atom.

Q: What’s the best type of insulation for midlayers?

A: Moisture-resistant synthetic or wool insulation does the best job at maintaining warmth when exposed to sweat and light precipitation. Synthetics come in a range of fibers and constructions, including carpet-like fleeces, sheets of batting, and loose fibers that get sewn into baffles to imitate the warmth and appearance of down (but with superior moisture resistance).

Q: Should I get a midlayer with a hood or without?

A: Depends on how many other hooded jackets you’ll layer with it. A hooded midlayer provides additional warmth and weather protection, but heavy snow can soak it unless you cover it with a shell—which can add up to surplus headwear. A hoodless midlayer under a hooded shell is generally a better choice for stormy conditions.

Q: Why are there no down puffies among the midlayers in this review?

A: Down loses its insulating qualities when it gets damp (as it will when worn as a midlayer for sweaty pursuits). Looking for a technical down jacket? Read on.

Q: Why are midlayer jackets so expensive?

A: Making materials that are simultaneously warm, breathable, and protective is a technological feat that doesn’t come cheap. These multi-tasking materials cost more than ones that do just one job well.
