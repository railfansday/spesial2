Fantasy Football Film Room: Breaking down Fernando Mendoza's first NFL start, plus Rams' backfield split

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Fantasy football is inundated with stats and charts (guilty!), but the more context the film provides, the more reliable the data gets. That's why we have a 2.5-hour show called Stats vs. Film every Tuesday at 6 p.m. ET. That show is live — join us in the chat!

This weekly column will look at some of the biggest storylines I picked up by grinding the film.

There was some good sprinkled in with plenty of bad in Mendoza's debut, one that truly was an impossible task against the Super Bowl-defending defense, and made worse by a Brock Bowers' ankle injury. In the end, Mendoza had -0.25 EPA per dropback (13th percentile) and a 31% passing success rate (5th percentile) game, leading to a QB19 fantasy outing thanks to some rushing production. More on that later.

Mendoza ran a far different offense than the one he played in at Indiana, as expected. His first three dropbacks were under-center, play-action completions, something he rarely did in college. In the first half, before the scoreboard became a little more lopsided, eight of 1his 8 dropbacks were under-center play-action reps. Almost all of them resulted in hitting an underneath receiver.

Here's a look at Mendoza's passing chart in his first NFL start.

He did not push the ball downfield at all. Only one completion traveled beyond eight air yards, and that was an absolute dot out of structure, throwing on the run to his left side. It's the type of play that you'd expect only from someone with first-overall-pick pedigree.

Mendoza is not one to put the ball into harm's way often. I didn't grade him with any turnover-worthy plays, but this is something that will need to get ironed out. He played too conservatively, sometimes getting the ball to the checkdown before there was a chance for a layered throw to be attempted. There is a balance between risk aversion and playing good hoops. Right now, Mendoza should be comfortable with a few interceptions if it means adding a lot more explosive plays. It's hard to win with the passing chart he put out.

He also had too many misses due to not finding the right club in his bag. There were a couple of high red-zone throws that had way too much velocity and not enough touch for his receiver to make a play on the ball. He's capable of doing this based on his college tape, but he was a bit wound up in his debut. There were enough sprays for me to keep track of this for the rest of his rookie debut.

The last notable problem with Mendoza's debut was timing. There were a few times when he didn't get enough depth in his dropback, which created easier interior pressure. There were other times when he was trying to throw the ball to a checkdown receiver far before he was getting to his spot. Some of that certainly can be attributed to the Seahawks' pressure packages, but some of it fell on just not having the timing of the progression down as a veteran would.

Once again, none of these are surprising or overly concerning for a debut, given the circumstances. These WRs and Michael Mayer didn't help him out at the catch point. There was a Mike Washington Jr. failed Devon-Witherspoon-blitz pickup. The OL obviously made mistakes. The RBs picked up one first down on 17 carries, for -0.47 EPA per carry. It was a mess around him, and that will absolutely continue, especially if the Bowers' injury lingers.

The last note is that Mendoza is a great athlete. No qualifiers needed. He made a couple of excellent throws on the run to his left, scrambled for a first down in a key spot, converted his signature QB draw for a rushing score, held onto the ball despite some massive hits, and converted a Tush Push. His size, toughness and good-enough speed will allow him to bail out of potential negative plays. He's going to be an exciting player to watch as he develops.

Tyson graduated to a near full-time role, taking the (wasted) snaps from rookie Bryce Lance in 3-WR sets. That makes the offense better, but the Saints are working out exactly how the offense will operate with Tyson and Lance having literally zero skill set overlap. Lance was essentially a clear-out, low-volume vertical threat, while Tyson is a movement Z/slot who largely works within 10 yards of the line of scrimmage.

As he did in his debut, Tyson was in motion, stacked in a bunch set or off the line of scrimmage on almost every rep. They are going to make sure his wild short-area athleticism is maximized, and because they can't run the ball with multiple OL starters injured, they are going to continue spamming throws to him underneath. Tyson had five targets behind the line of scrimmage, four targets between 1-9 air yards and then four targets 15-25 air yards.

Tyson finished as the WR17 on WR7 expected half PPR points. Targets to him averaged +0.13 EPA per play, so they worked to some degree, and I'd be somewhat surprised if he's not a highly-targeted player moving forward with real top-24 fantasy appeal. The Saints are still first in neutral pace and the defense being suspect will create incredible dropback volume at times.

All of this does work against Chris Olave, though. Just take a scroll through these screenshots of Tyson targets, and where Olave was at the time of Tyler Shough's release:

Olave is so much better than Lance is, obviously, but he could be sliding into more of that role in the offense, which would lower his target share and fantasy floor. Last week was a season-low in targets (3), target share (12%), air yards (22), expected half PPR points (4.6) and actual half PPR points (4.5). It was much better in Week 9 when Tyson was a part-time player, but over the past two weeks with Tyson, it's been the rookie pulling slightly ahead in volume.

Here's a look at the Saints' receiving chart from Weeks 9 and 10 with Jordyn Tyson back.

It wouldn't be hard to imagine that Tyson's underneath role proves as valuable as Olave's more intermediate and downfield role moving forward. There's enough volume for both to be top-24 fantasy WRs as long as Devaughn Vele and Juwan Johnson remain as limited as they are above, but Olave's case to be a top-10 fantasy WR is becoming harder to make. I'd expect both Olave and Tyson to have big games down the stretch. I'm not sure the Saints are good enough for either to be viewed as fantasy WR1s while both are healthy.

Corum pulled ahead of Williams on the ground last week, even though they still split drives with Williams starting the game. The Rams just let Corum cook for more than he typically does, before Ronnie Rivers became the first player to start an NFL drive with 15-straight carries. Insane!

Here's a look at the backfield split for L.A. against the Cardinals.

Corum is the better rusher in my eyes, with nothing bad muttered about Williams. He is still a good rusher himself! The difference is just that Corum can hit the hole with an extra gear at this point. That wasn't true when Corum was coming off collegiate injuries and was still developing his frame, but Corum looks more athletic in 2026 and the results are felt when he has a chance to break past the second level. Corum always had excellent vision and contact balance, which is why the Rams drafted him as a Kyren clone. Now he's paired those valuable skills with starting-caliber athleticism.

The spreadsheets agree. Corum is leading in rushing success rate and EPA per carry. Quite frankly, it's not even close either. He's 800 basis points ahead of RB2 Jahmyr Gibbs in success rate (57% vs. 49%). He's +0.04 EPA per carry ahead of RB2 Christian McCaffrey, too. The Rams' scheme, OL and the typical occasions Corum is used definitely aids his numbers in ways that aren't true for most backs, but Corum is still the RB6 in rush yards over expected per carry in my model, only trailing Kenneth Walker III, Jaylen Warren, James Cook III, Gibbs and Bijan Robinson.

Here's a look at EPA per carry vs. success rate for both Rams running backs.

The Rams will have a bye week to digest the RB room, and the truth is it'll be hard to call Corum the starter over Williams given all he's given the team. Williams is also above average in all metrics cited in this column, but I'm open to the change happening to close the year. Even if it does, which I still don't think will happen, Williams is still the far more proven pass catcher. Williams' fantasy RB2 role is safe because he's on pace for 65 receptions. His current 38 receptions are already a career high. He's needed them, but there's no way Williams' receiving role cools off much with Puka Nacua and Davante Adams out.

Corum and Williams are suddenly both top-24 RB options during bye weeks.

Moore has been so in-and-out of the lineup that it's hard to get a grasp of whether he's playing well and where he should be ranked in fantasy football.

Here's a look at where DJ Moore stacks up in terms of air yards vs. fantasy points per game.

To simplify the latter, I removed his two truly injured games when he played fewer than half of the snaps. In five other part-time games, Moore is the WR14 on WR23 expected half PPR points. He's averaging 121 air yards per game (WR5) and a 26% target share (WR10). As a reminder, this sample includes games with 58% to 76% snaps and a schedule that included the Vikings, Texans and Ravens. Moore has been the WR8, WR8, WR13, WR36 and WR53 (MIN).

In terms of yards per route run (removing screens), Moore is the WR10 at 2.52, trailing Puka Nacua, CeeDee Lamb and Tee Higgins. Has he been talked like that this year yet?!?!

On film, the Bills are using Moore exactly how I would. The more he's used on runaway routes, the better he'll be. Moore had a post-route TD and a 32-yard explosive on a crossing route against the Jets this past week. Those were his second and fourth biggest plays of the year, only trailing a 45-yard post route. His other explosives were a hole shot with some YAC, a big dig route, a glance route and a shallow cross in the high red zone. They've only thrown him one screen all year (a calm-Josh-Allen-down-versus-the-Vikings rep). It's been deep and intermediate runaway routes all year long.
