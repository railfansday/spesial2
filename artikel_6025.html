Trump’s closing midterm argument, Reflecting Pool edition

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

President Donald Trump on Monday delivered an unconventional midterms closing argument after spending weeks dropping comments at rallies aimed at sowing doubts about the election — and what he might do to contest races Republicans lose.

After barnstorming states with competitive Senate races the last few weeks, Trump delivered his final message in Washington, D.C. He even chose the Reflecting Pool by the Lincoln Memorial — the site of a refurbishment he oversaw that was plagued by questionable work that continues to reverberate politically and legally.

There had been rumblings online last week that he would be in Michigan to close out his rally blitz alongside former GOP Rep. Mike Rogers, who is in a Toss-up race against Democrat Abdul El-Sayed. That race was rocked Sunday when Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens, who lost a primary to El-Sayed, endorsed the Republican nominee. Alas, Trump chose the nation’s capital and mused about pool liner colors, an issue that has not overtaken voter concerns about the cost of living, corruption, the Iran war and other issues, according to public polling.

“We resurfaced the bottom in a beautiful dark blue, very dark blue. They call it ‘American flag blue,’ but it’s very dark,” he said, wearing a suit, tie and white baseball cap. “And if you had a regular blue, like I personally love the lighter color blue, but then you wouldn’t have any reflectors.”

He also plugged his proposed arch, made questionable statements about crime rates and boasted about his other Washington, D.C., fountain and park projects. He did not mention Tuesday’s election —which he deemed “the most important midterm I believe in our lifetime” — until after the 22-minute mark. Once he moved on from boasting about his D.C. projects, Trump touted his second administration’s policy record. And himself.

“As you know, this is the last day before the midterm elections, the most important midterm I believe in our lifetime. The Democrat Party has nominated the most extreme and dangerous slate of candidates in American history,” he said. “These are the most dangerous candidates ever put up.”

He noted he had held 29 rallies in recent weeks. He contended Iran’s military platforms and stockpiles were “way down.” He touted statistics about the economy.

“Personal incomes are the highest in history. The poverty rate is at an all-time record low,” he said, adding. “American exports are at an all-time high, and more people are working today than at any point in the history of our country. And the United States is now by far the largest supplier of oil and natural gas anywhere in the world.”

The Reflecting Pool event came after he spent weeks on the campaign trail making warnings about elections — specifically, ones Democrats have won and might win this week.

For instance, during an Oct. 20 campaign rally in North Carolina for former Republican National Committee Chair Michael Whatley, who is running against former Democratic Gov. Roy Cooper, Trump returned to casting doubt on his 2020 loss.

“What a lot of people don’t know is that we got screwed in 2020,” Trump said that evening, referring to the presidential race he lost to Joe Biden. “We did great. But you know who … didn’t allow it to happen was Michael Whatley. We were winning everything. You went to bed [at] 10:00. The race was over.

“Then all of a sudden, Georgia happened and some other things happened. Pennsylvania happened,” he said, continuing to cast doubt on his losses that year in both states despite no evidence of fraud.

“But we can’t forget,” he told loyalists in eastern North Carolina. Inside Elections with Nathan L. Gonzales lists the race as leaning toward Cooper.

Trump also offered the same crowd a preview of what his administration and political organizations have signaled for Tuesday at strategically selected polling stations with competitive House and Senate races.

“You know what didn’t happen? North Carolina didn’t happen,” he said to cheers about the 2020 election. “Because he had 619 lawyers at every station watching, watching, watching.”

Democratic lawmakers and pro-voter groups have warned for months that they fear pro-Trump election monitors — or even federal agents — could try to intimidate anti-Trump voters from casting ballots. Some sources have even raised concerns the administration might try seizing ballots in places that could swing a close outcome toward Republican candidates.

Trump’s rally rhetoric also suggests he will raise doubts not just about the outcomes the votes produce, but how they were cast.

“The whole thing is corrupt,” he told rallygoers Sunday in Dubuque, Iowa. “Go to paper ballots, everybody. Nice, beautiful paper ballots. You know, the paper ballots cost 9 percent of what these crooked machines cost, so you’d save a lot,” he said, without offering evidence that electronically cast ballots are problematic.

He even set the stage for casting doubt over or challenging Democratic-won races in states with GOP chiefs executive.

“And even Republican governors, many of them, they, they just do it,” he said of using voting machines over watermarked paper ballots. “I don’t know, it’s hard to believe. But you go to paper and you have the ballot there, and it’s so simple, and they put it in stacks.”

Still, Trump and his team have questioned the counting of paper ballots before — in 2020 in places like Arizona, Georgia and Pennsylvania, but to no avail. That didn’t stop him on Sunday, like at previous campaign stops, from alleging Democrats would still try shenanigans.

“It’s, look, they’ll try figuring something [out], but it’s much harder, because they have to cheat,” he said without providing evidence. “Because without, look, their policies are so bad.”

One GOP strategist, granted anonymity to be candid, said it was “normal” for any party to contest “some” election results.

Former Pennsylvania GOP Rep. Charlie Dent on Friday said Trump’s rally rhetoric appears mostly aimed at influencing public opinion and could be lacking a legitimate legal strategy.

“I’m not sure how he would intend to make a challenge to the certification of some members who may win by comfortable margins,” Dent said. “There might be circumstances where you have some contested outcomes shortly after the election that are very close. But then you’ll have a legal process and a recount process to go through to ultimately determine who the winner is.

“But once those winners are determined and the states certify those elections, then everything that’s done or said in Washington is merely ministerial at that point,” he added. “If there are objections to a particular outcome, there are legal remedies candidates can seek out.”

Mark Zaid, an attorney who has sued the Trump administration multiple times, said Monday that “the danger with his rhetoric is that he expects people to react to what he says and implement his words into action.

“He did that on Jan. 6 and he continues to do it now,” Zaid added, referring to the January 2021 Capitol riot by a pro-Trump mob following his fiery Ellipse speech that morning raising doubts about his loss to Biden.

“My own view is that post-Nov. 3 he will act far more like a cornered animal, more dangerous than when an avenue of escape is available — and implement a scorched-earth response to anything that is thrown at him,” he added. “I find it amazing that his rhetoric seemingly goes unchallenged by his base.”

Trump appeared in a relaxed mood on Monday, joking that he intended to rename the Reflecting Pool to include his name.

“I’m only kidding,” he said, looking toward assembled media members. “Please, don’t report it.” Later, about the midterms, he said the party of sitting presidents typically loses, adding matter-of-factly: “We gave this a very strong shot.”
