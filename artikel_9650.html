NIFTY and Bank Nifty Algo Trading: Why a Strategy Built on Bank Nifty Weeklies Will Not Port Straight to NIFTY

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Many Indian option sellers built their first strategy on Bank Nifty weekly expiries. For a few years that was the most active weekly contract in the country, and a lot of backtests, courses and shared templates were written around it. Those weekly contracts are gone. The surviving weekly expiries are NIFTY on the NSE and SENSEX on the BSE. So a large pool of old rules now needs a new home.

The tempting move is to open the strategy, change the symbol from Bank Nifty to NIFTY, and press run. The rules are the same, so the behaviour should be the same. It will not be. The entry time and the logic may carry over. The numbers inside the rules will not, and a few of those numbers can quietly double or halve your risk without a single line of the logic changing.

This article is not about the exchange rule change that retired the old contract. That story is well covered elsewhere. It is about the practical job of re-calibrating a strategy for a different index. I use a worked example with clearly labelled illustrative numbers, then a step-by-step procedure you can follow on your own rules.

What actually breaks when you copy the file Think of a strategy as two layers. The first layer is the logic: sell a straddle at a set time, protect it with a hedge, exit on a stop or a time. The second layer is the parameters: how many points for the stop, how far away the hedge sits, how big the trailing step is, how many lots. When people say "the strategy works", they usually mean that the logic plus one particular set of parameters worked on one particular index.

Parameters are tuned to the thing they were tuned on. A 100-point stop makes sense on an index trading at 55,000 and makes a very different statement on an index trading at 25,000. Move the same number to the second index and you have not copied the strategy. You have created a new one, and nobody has tested it.

There are five places where a straight copy goes wrong, and they come up again below.

Any parameter written in index points. Any parameter written in rupees, because the rupee value of a point depends on the lot size. Strike distances, because strike spacing and index level differ. Volatility-driven choices such as entry timing, stop width and filters. Liquidity and slippage, which are different in percent terms on each index. One more point before the detail. Every number I use for Bank Nifty below is an illustration. I am not stating its current level, lot size or premium. Exchanges revise lot sizes from time to time, and the contract's weekly expiry no longer exists. Treat those figures as a shape, and rebuild the calculation with your own data and the exchange's current contract specifications.

Three unit systems: points, percent and rupees Every risk rule in an option-selling strategy is written in one of three units. Knowing which one you used is the first step in a port.

Points A point is one index unit. "Stop at 80 points" means the premium moves 80 index units against you. Points are the easiest unit to type and the worst to port, because the same move in points means a different thing on a different index level.

Percent Percent can mean percent of the premium collected ("stop at 25% of entry premium") or percent of spot ("hedge 0.9% away"). Percent-of-premium rules scale on their own with the volatility that the market is pricing. Percent-of-spot rules scale with index level. Both port much better than points.

Rupees Rupees are what you actually lose. The conversion is points x lot size. For NIFTY, the lot size is 65, so one point on one lot is Rs 65. A rule such as "exit if the day's loss crosses Rs 5,000 per lot" is portable in the sense that it means the same thing for your account, but the point-move that triggers it differs. On NIFTY at 65 units, Rs 5,000 is about 77 points of loss on a single lot. On an index with a smaller lot, the same rupee figure is a larger point move.

A simple discipline follows. Rewrite each risk rule in all three units. If the three versions tell different stories on the new index, you have found the parameter that needs work.

A worked example: the same rules on two indices Here is the illustration. All figures below are made up for the purpose of showing the mechanics. They are not market data, and I do not claim they are current. Imagine a strategy that sells an at-the-money straddle at 09:20 and has these rules, written in the way many Bank Nifty strategies were written, in points.

Stop-loss: exit the combined position if the straddle price rises by 110 points. Hedge: buy a protective call and put 500 points away from the money. Trailing step: tighten the stop by 20 points for every 40 points of profit. Now compare the two indices under illustrative conditions: an index near 55,000 with a straddle priced at 0.80% of spot and a lot of 30 units, against NIFTY near 25,000 with a straddle priced at 0.47% of spot and a lot of 65 units. The 0.47% is the mean 09:20 straddle premium from EliteAlgo's desk study of 163 NIFTY and SENSEX expiry sessions for the VIX 12 to 14 band. The 0.80% for the other index is purely illustrative.

The stop becomes almost meaningless. On the first index, a 110-point stop is a quarter of the premium collected. On NIFTY, the same 110 points is 94% of the premium. The stop would fire only after nearly the whole premium has been lost, which is a very different risk profile from the one you tested.

The rupee risk more than doubles. A 110-point loss costs Rs 3,300 per lot on the first index and Rs 7,150 per lot on NIFTY, because 65 is more than twice the illustrative 30. So the larger lot and the unchanged point stop work together to inflate your loss per trade, even though the strategy "looks the same".

The hedge moves far away. A 500-point hedge was 0.91% of spot on one index and is 2.0% of spot on NIFTY. A hedge that far out costs very little and protects against only a violent move. It is cheaper, but it no longer does the job the original hedge did.

Now rewrite the rules in a form that travels. Express the stop as 25% of the entry premium, which on NIFTY at 117.5 points is about 29 points, or about Rs 1,909 per lot. Express the hedge as 0.9% of spot, which on NIFTY at 25,000 is 225 points; since NIFTY strikes are 50 points apart, round to 250 or 200. Express the trailing step as a fraction of premium, not points: the original 20 points per 40 points of profit was about 4.5% and 9% of a 440-point premium, which is roughly 5 and 10 points on a 117.5-point premium.

Rule Naive copy (NIFTY) Rescaled (NIFTY) Stop 110 points (Rs 7,150 per lot) About 29 points (Rs 1,909 per lot) Hedge 500 points away About 225 points, rounded to a strike Trail step 20 points per 40 About 5 points per 10 Even this rescaled set is a starting hypothesis and not a finished strategy. It keeps the proportions. It does not prove that the proportions are right for NIFTY. The next section explains why the choice of proportion needs its own evidence.

Volatility scaling: measure it, do not assume it Proportional rescaling assumes that the two indices behave like scaled copies of each other. They do not, and the ratio is not a constant. Bank Nifty is a sector index made of banking and financial stocks. NIFTY is a broad index of 50 companies across sectors. The narrower index has tended to swing more in percent terms, and it reacts to different news, such as rate decisions and bank results. How much more, and on which days, changes over time. Do not take a rule of thumb from a forum. Measure it from your own data.

You can do this with a short list of statistics, calculated on matching sessions from both indices.

The average and median of the day's high-to-low range as a percent of the open. The average and median of the move from your entry time to the close, in percent. The average premium at your entry time, as a percent of spot, split by India VIX band. How often the index moves more than your stop distance in percent terms, within your trade window. The third item deserves special attention, because the premium itself is the market's own estimate of the expected move. In the desk study, the 09:20 straddle premium on NIFTY and SENSEX rose in a clean line as India VIX rose: 0.38% of spot under VIX 12, 0.47% for 12 to 14, 0.58% for 14 to 17, 0.64% for 17 to 20 and 0.83% above 20. The budget roughly doubles from the calmest band to the most stressed. If you size stops as a fraction of premium, the stop widens when the market expects more movement and tightens when it does not. That is a feature, and it is the reason percent-of-premium rules port better than point rules. But it is only a feature if the strategy was designed around it. A rule that was tuned to a fixed point stop might behave worse once it floats with premium.

If you want to see how a published framework treats the two main index contracts side by side, EliteAlgo has a page on NIFTY and Bank Nifty algo trading. That is useful for context. For the porting job itself, the evidence you need is your own measured table, built from your own sessions.

Hedges, strikes and spreads: the liquidity side Parameters in points are only part of the porting job. The market itself is built differently on each index, and that touches your hedge choice and your costs.

Strike spacing NIFTY options near the money are listed 50 points apart. At an index level of 25,000, one step is 0.2% of spot. If your old rule said "hedge 5 strikes away" on an index with 100-point steps, that was 500 points. On NIFTY the same five strikes is only 250 points. So decide whether your rule is about strikes, points or percent, and convert on purpose.

Hedge cost and what it buys The further out the hedge, the cheaper it is and the less it protects. The right distance depends on the worst move you are trying to survive. A useful method is to look at the largest single-day moves in your sample, in percent, and ask what your account would lose with the hedge at various distances. Do this for NIFTY directly. Do not carry over the distance from another index.

Bid-ask spread in percent of premium Look at the spread as a share of the premium you are trading. A spread of 0.30 points on a 117-point straddle is small. The same 0.30 points on a 10-point hedge leg is 3% of its price. The hedge legs are where percentage costs bite. When you port, record the actual spreads at your entry and exit times on NIFTY for each leg, and put them into your backtest as a cost per leg. Do not reuse the slippage assumption from the other index.

Depth and order size Check open interest and traded volume at the strikes you plan to use, at the times you plan to trade. Thin strikes give wide spreads. Exchanges also cap the quantity per order, so large lot counts must be sliced, and these limits change over time. Read the current contract specifications on nseindia.com and test your slicing logic.

The re-calibration procedure, step by step Here is the sequence I would follow to move a Bank Nifty weekly strategy to NIFTY. It is deliberately dull. Each step produces a record you can review later.

Step 1. Write the rules in plain words, with units. List every parameter and mark whether it is in points, percent of premium, percent of spot, strikes, or rupees. A rule you cannot express in one of these is vague and needs to be fixed first.

Step 2. Convert each parameter to a dimensionless form where possible. Stops and trails become percent of entry premium. Hedges become percent of spot. Timing stays in clock time for now.

Step 3. Map to NIFTY contract details. Lot size 65 (confirm the current value with the exchange), strike spacing, expiry day, trading hours and freeze quantity.

Step 4. Build the NIFTY premium table. Calculate the 09:20 straddle premium as a percent of spot by India VIX band, for as many weekly sessions as you can get. Compare to the table above and ask whether your data shows the same shape.

Step 5. Re-test the timing. The entry time that worked on the old index may not be the best on NIFTY. Desk data shows the average NIFTY and SENSEX straddle premium fell from 100% of its 09:16 value to about 88.6% by 11:16, and then moved sideways in an 88% to 93% band for the rest of the day. That shape came from a fixed strike with a moving spot, which nets time decay against re-pricing, and it is not a clean decay curve. If your old strategy assumed a late-day collapse in premium, check whether it exists in your NIFTY sample.

Step 6. Fit the stop on NIFTY's own path. Use a grid of stop sizes as percent of premium (for example 15%, 20%, 25%, 30%, 40%). For each, record the number of stop-outs, the average loss on stopped days and the average result on the others. Choose a width where the result is stable across neighbouring values. If the result swings sharply from 25% to 30%, you have fitted noise.

Step 7. Re-size in rupees. Decide the maximum loss per trade and per day you accept, in rupees, from your account size. Work backwards to the number of lots. Do not carry over the lot count from the other index.

Step 8. Replace the cost assumptions. Use NIFTY spreads, brokerage and taxes. Add a slippage figure measured from small live or paper orders.

Step 9. Run golden days. Pick five to ten sessions, including a quiet day, a gap, a trend and a sharp reversal. Write down by hand what the strategy should do on each. Run the code. Compare line by line.

Step 10. Paper trade, then trade one lot. At 65 units a single lot is already a real amount of money. Run at least several weeks on paper, then one lot, and keep a log of every difference between expected and actual behaviour.

Step Output you should have 1-2 A one-page rule sheet with units 3 A list of contract facts and the date you checked them 4 A premium-by-VIX-band table for NIFTY 5 A chart or table of premium by time of day 6 A stop-width grid with results 7 A rupee risk limit and lot count 8 A cost sheet per leg 9 Golden-day comparisons 10 A trade log with expected-versus-actual notes Even NIFTY and SENSEX are not copies of each other If you are tempted to think that two indices that follow the same rules must behave alike, look at what happens between the two surviving weekly contracts. NIFTY trades on the NSE and SENSEX on the BSE. Both are large-cap benchmarks, both have weekly expiries, and yet the desk study found clear differences in one measure.

On expiry day, the study measured how far the closing level sat from the max-pain strike, which is the strike at which option buyers collectively lose the most. Across the 163 sessions, the median distance was about 0.083% of spot. But split by index, NIFTY's median was 0.130% of spot while SENSEX's was 0.060%. The mean told the same story: 0.160% for NIFTY and 0.083% for SENSEX. SENSEX closed about half as far from max pain as NIFTY did.

You do not need to believe that max pain is a tradable signal to see the lesson. Two index options on the same type of expiry, in the same period, gave a measurably different closing behaviour. A strategy that leans on pinning near a strike (for instance, a tight short strangle held to the close) would have a different hit rate on each. If that is true for two close relatives, it is even more true for a sector index and a broad index. So ask of every part of your strategy: which of my assumptions come from the old index's own behaviour?

Max-pain distance at expiry close NIFTY (83 sessions) SENSEX (80 sessions) Mean, % of spot 0.160% 0.083% Median, % of spot 0.130% 0.060% Another number from the same study applies to anyone who ports a strategy and worries about margin. Using a simple proxy for exposure, where notional is spot times one plus 0.15% of the day's VIX, the peak exposure ran only about 0.7% above entry exposure on average, with a maximum of 2.5%. This is a proxy and not live SPAN margin, so your broker's margin screen is what governs. It does suggest that for a fixed-strike short straddle, exposure does not balloon during the day on its own. The real swing in your margin and risk comes from the stop, the hedge and the sizing rules you set, and those are exactly the items a port changes.

What history you can and cannot trust The old strategy's evidence is the backtest on Bank Nifty weekly data. That evidence has limits that become sharper after a port.

First, the history is closed. The weekly contract no longer trades, so you cannot compare live results to the backtest going forward. The record is fixed, and anything you learned from it is an input to a hypothesis about NIFTY, not a proof.

Second, the regime may have been special. A strategy that sold weeklies in a particular stretch of years may have benefited from the conditions then, such as the volume of retail buyers or the level of volatility. Do not assume the conditions carry over. If you can, look at how the old results varied by VIX band, and see whether NIFTY is currently in a similar band.

Third, you now have a smaller data set on the new index at the same frequency. The desk study uses 163 sessions across both indices, which is useful but not huge, and the sample splits into 83 for NIFTY. Treat any NIFTY-only conclusion drawn from a few dozen weeks as tentative. Prefer rules that are stable across neighbouring values, and size small until the live record builds up.

Fourth, do not tune the NIFTY version until its backtest looks like the old one. If the honest version is weaker, that is information.

Frequently Asked Questions Can I use my Bank Nifty weekly strategy on NIFTY without changes?

You can run it, but you should not trust it. Point-based stops, hedge distances and lot counts change meaning on a different index. Rewrite the rules in percent of premium and percent of spot, re-test on NIFTY data and re-size in rupees before using real money.

What is the NIFTY lot size and why does it matter for a port?

The NIFTY lot size is 65 at the time of writing. Exchanges revise lot sizes from time to time, so check the current value. It matters because the rupee value of one point equals the lot size, so a stop written in points costs a different amount on each index.

Is Bank Nifty weekly expiry still available?

No. After the exchange rule limiting each exchange to one weekly expiry, the weekly Bank Nifty contract was discontinued. The surviving weekly index contracts are NIFTY on the NSE and SENSEX on the BSE. You can check current contract details on the exchange websites.

Should I use points or percent for my stop-loss?

Percent of the entry premium ports best, because it floats with the volatility that the market prices in. Points are fixed and ignore the index level and the VIX. A common approach is to test several percent widths and pick one where the results are stable across neighbours.

How many weeks should I paper trade after porting?

There is no magic number. A reasonable minimum is several weeks that include different volatility conditions, with a log of every gap between expected and actual behaviour. Then start with one lot. Judge by whether the execution matches your assumptions, not only by profit.

Does a higher India VIX mean a bigger stop is needed?

The premium collected rises with VIX, so a stop set as a share of premium widens on its own. In a study of 163 NIFTY and SENSEX expiry sessions, the 09:20 straddle premium averaged 0.38% of spot under VIX 12 and 0.83% over VIX 20. Whether a wider stop is better still needs testing on your own data.

Conclusion A strategy is a set of rules with numbers in them, and the numbers belong to the market they were tuned on. Moving from Bank Nifty weeklies to NIFTY is a re-calibration job. Rewrite each rule in units that travel, measure NIFTY's own premium and movement, rebuild the stop and hedge from that evidence, and re-size in rupees at 65 units a lot.

Then test it the slow way. Golden days, paper trading, one lot, and a log. If the result is weaker than the old backtest, accept that. The old record belongs to a contract that no longer exists, and the new record has to be earned.

A last word on risk. Algo trading carries a real risk of loss, including the loss of the capital you deploy, and a careful port does not remove it.
