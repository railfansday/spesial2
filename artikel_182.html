Over the coming weeks, Chuck Schumer and Chris Van Hollen, along with any late challengers, will vie for the party’s top Senate leadership post before lawmakers cast their votes. Here are four things to know about how the election works.

A secret, closed-door election: Unlike the public Speaker of the House vote, where lawmakers cast ballots on the chamber floor, the Senate leadership contest takes place behind closed doors. Senators vote by secret ballot, and individual votes are not disclosed.

The winner only needs a caucus majority: The Democratic leadership race is decided solely by members of the party’s Senate caucus. If Democrats hold 55 seats, a candidate would need 28 votes to win. If Democrat Mary Peltola wins in Alaska and the party reaches 56 seats, the threshold rises to 29 votes.

The vote is scheduled for next month: As things stand, the election is set for 1 December. Both incumbent senators and incoming Democratic members will participate, while retiring and outgoing senators will not vote, despite many having publicly weighed in on the race.

Republicans choose their leaders separately: Republicans hold their own closed-door leadership elections in both chambers. Earlier this month, House Republicans selected hardliner Jim Jordan as their leader after Mike Johnson opted not to run for minority leader. In the Senate, Republicans unanimously backed John Thune to continue leading the conference.

The US lost its measles elimination status, global health authorities announced, an official recognition that the infectious disease is endemic in the US after being eliminated for 26 years.

The announcement comes as the US endures the worst measles outbreak since 1991, with more than 5,400 cases and eight deaths in 2026.

“Measles is resurging globally amid the persistent gaps in vaccination coverage, and this global trend is also affecting the Americas,” said Dr Jarbas Barbosa da Silva Jr, the director of the Pan American Health Organization, a regional branch of the World Health Organization. “The endemic measles transmission has now been re-established in Bolivia, Mexico and United States.” Silva made the final decision on US elimination status.

Measles is one of the most contagious viruses known to medicine and requires 95% of the community to be vaccinated to prevent outbreaks. The measles vaccine is safe and highly effective. However, pockets of low vaccination have grown in the US following pandemic-era disruptions and widespread misinformation.

The nation’s largest doctors group, the American Medical Association, described loss of measles elimination status as a “deeply troubling and entirely preventable setback for our nation”.

The loss of measles-free status comes after Donald Trump and his health secretary, Robert F Kennedy Jr, spent nearly two years sowing confusion about the safety of vaccines. In just one recent example, Trump called for the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) combination vaccine to be split into separate shots, arguing without evidence that “together, there could be a possibility they’re quite lethal”.

“As a pediatrician, I saw firsthand how decisions made in Washington hurt our children and families,” said Dr Annie Andrews, a Democratic senator-elect. “Nothing makes that clearer than the loss of our measles elimination status.” The loss of elimination status has already heightened criticism of Kennedy. High-profile epidemiologists called Kennedy’s work at the health department “malpractice” and equivalent to a “crime scene”.
