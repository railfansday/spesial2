With just days to go until the U.S. midterm elections, many Europeans and Canadians are watching closely to see if Democrats will retake Congress and perhaps begin to rein in U.S. President Donald Trump for his final two years in office.

Canadians, who have been on the receiving end of some of Trump’s harshest trade actions, are so hungry for news about the U.S. midterms that they are searching for information at a higher rate than even Americans, according to Google search trends.

“Normal, average Canadians know so much more about the United States and care so much more about the United States,” said retired Royal Canadian Air Force Maj. Gen. Scott Clancy, a former director of operations for the North American Aerospace Defense Command. “Whereas Canada doesn’t even come into the daily [consideration] for the vast, vast majority of Americans in any given day.”

Europeans are also gripped by midterm fever. After Canada and the United States, midterm election information searches were the highest in Denmark, Qatar, and Norway—three countries that have also been rocked by Trump’s foreign-policy actions, from his aggressive claims on Greenland to the war against Iran.

Damian Murphy, the senior vice president for national security and international policy at the liberal Center for American Progress think tank, said that European officials he’s met with lately are “laser-focused on the midterms.” Murphy said he has been struck by the granular level of knowledge his European interlocutors have shown for the state of play of individual House races.

“What I’ve been telling them is that you shouldn’t have super high expectations for what any Congress can affirmatively do,” Murphy said. “In our system, the executive has a lot of power when it comes to the conduct of foreign policy. But what is important is the political message that it will send to the rest of the world: that the American people repudiate all of this—ICE [Immigration and Customs Enforcement], the Iran war, the treatment of allies.”

Other foreign observers have cautioned against high expectations of a U.S. reversion to pre-Trump foreign policies even if Democrats win control of Congress.

“Let us not overestimate the consequences of a defeat for the Trump administration in the midterm elections,” said former French Ambassador to the United States Gérard Araud in a recent post on X. “An American president holds considerable powers, particularly in foreign policy, and Trump can rely on the Supreme Court to bypass Congress.”

The experiences of Trump’s surprise 2016 and 2024 presidential election wins also continue to act as ballast against runaway hopes of a blue tidal wave.

“My philosophy of life is to have low expectations, and I apply that to the midterms as well. My mission is to stamp out any glimmer of hope of a return to normalcy after the midterms,” said one European diplomat, who requested anonymity to give an unvarnished take on the U.S. political situation.

One outcome with a strong chance of occurring in November, though, is a vote on impeaching U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over allegations of violating war powers and war crimes laws. A vote on the defense chief’s impeachment is likely to happen the week after the midterms.

Even if Republicans find the votes to protect Hegseth in the impeachment vote, a new impeachment inquiry may be launched in January if Democrats win control of the House, as is predicted.

Like the midterms, Canadians are likely to closely follow any impeachment process against Hegseth.

The former Fox News host “deeply offended” many Canadians this fall, Clancy said, when he separately appeared to disparage Canadian troop cadets and denigrated the contributions of NATO allied troops who joined the U.S.-led mission in Afghanistan.

“Personal friends of mine perished during that conflict,” Clancy said.
