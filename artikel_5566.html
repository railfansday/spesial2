6 reef materials and plans, and how little of each is proven

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Six artificial reef materials and plans from one developer, from SynCoral to Compound 7, sorted by what each has shown.

Around the world, people who care for coral have stopped waiting for damaged reefs to recover on their own and have started building new ones. That work has become urgent. Coral is dying in Malaysia's marine parks, where sea-surface temperatures are now 32°C to 35°C, according to government monitoring figures reported by The Star. Knowledge of what lives on built structures over time has not kept pace, and long-term studies of the marine life that settles on them are still needed, according to the University of Aberdeen. New reef materials are meant to help in the space between that urgent need and the research still to come. Each one promises a reef that does more good and less harm than the concrete and steel people have sunk in the sea before. No one can judge a reef material until it has spent years in the water, and for the newest materials those years have not yet passed.

The companies behind the newest reef materials make striking claims about recycled content, coral survival and carbon capture, and they are making those claims faster than anyone outside can check them. All of the materials and plans that follow belong to one developer, Fizzy Transition Ventures, a private company describing its own products. Its figures are claims to be tested, not settled results, and readers should weigh them that way. That caution does not turn the materials into hype. It means the useful question for each one is what it has shown so far, and what it still has to show over years in open water.

United Nations DESA described one company's artificial reef materials on its partnerships page, along with how they were being tested and what the company planned next. Its account ranged from trial waters off Malaysia's islands to a coating meant to help coral take hold and a mostly recycled stand-in for concrete. A few of these ideas already have results from the water behind them, while others remain stated goals. The question for each is which kind it is. If even part of the promise holds up, people will rebuild reefs in a different way. If it does not, readers will know which of the company's figures no longer deserve their trust.

Anyone who sinks a structure at sea is expected to pull it back out one day, and this material is designed so that no one has to. Its developer made the material entirely natural and designed it to last 10 to 100 years, according to United Nations DESA. Owners of old offshore structures could then meet their duty to remove them by letting the reef break down in place instead of hauling it up. That lifespan is a setting chosen in the formula, not a measured result, and the developer gives no measurements of how fast the material breaks down. The trait that sets this reef apart, leaving the seafloor on a set schedule, is the one thing it has not yet shown.

The case for a reef that removes itself depends on the rule that old structures must come out, and researchers now question that rule. The duty to remove comes from the OSPAR Convention. Other waters already take a different approach, and old rigs in the Gulf of Mexico are handled case by case under a rigs-to-reefs program, according to the University of Aberdeen. Megan Squire, a postgraduate researcher at the University of Aberdeen and lead author of a review of offshore structures, wants their habitat value to count in removal decisions. "The potential value of these structures as 'artificial reefs' should be considered when creating and enacting policy around decommissioning in the North Sea," Squire said. The developer is therefore competing with more than concrete. It is also competing with the option of leaving existing steel in the water. The material has its strongest case wherever removal is still required.

The developer aimed the material at waters covered by OSPAR but plans to test it off Malaysia, outside them, so early results will not show how it holds up where it is meant to be used. Field test results were scheduled for Dec. 1, 2025. That date falls early in a life designed to run for decades, so those results can show how the material starts out in the water, not whether it breaks down on its intended timetable.

This is a promising answer to a question regulators may be rewriting, and it still owes proof of its timing in the seas it was built for.

SynCoral is an attempt to replace ordinary concrete with a material made almost entirely from waste. About 97% of SynCoral is recycled content, according to Fizzy Transition Ventures figures reported by United Nations DESA. A reef built from it would mostly reuse material that already exists rather than material made new for the job. By the company's account, SynCoral also carries close to zero carbon cost. If that holds, adding a structure to the water would not mean adding much to emissions. Anything placed in the sea stays in contact with the water for years, so what the material releases matters as much as what goes into it, and the company calls SynCoral non-toxic. If that claim holds, a reef built from SynCoral would carry nothing harmful into the water around it. The test the material still has to pass is plain strength.

Recycled content and a low carbon cost make SynCoral appealing, but strength decides whether builders can use it in place of ordinary concrete or only for reef blocks. Fizzy Transition Ventures said SynCoral is "a satisfactory replacement for most Portland-based concrete structures having similar compressive and flexural strengths." The words "most" and "similar" set the company's own limits on that claim. SynCoral is offered as comparable to ordinary concrete in most uses, not as equal to it in every use, and readers should not treat it as a claim of equal strength. The company has stated its environmental credentials and made a strength claim with care. It has not yet shown anyone outside the company that SynCoral structures remain intact after years in the water.

Compound 7 is aimed at the coral itself rather than the structure beneath it. Fizzy Transition Ventures designed it as a coating for the surface that young coral first touch, on the theory that coral larvae take hold more readily where the chemistry is right. The coating is meant to help larvae settle and transplanted coral survive, with a gain in coral cover of about 12%, according to United Nations DESA. That gain is the measure the coating should be judged by. If the gain holds, the same planting effort would leave more living coral on reefs that are losing it to heat.

The 12% figure rests on the maker's own belief. Fizzy Transition Ventures credits chemical cues on treated surfaces for the faster settlement it saw in early trials, and it presents both that explanation and the coral-cover gain as its belief rather than as a finding. Chemical cues are a reasonable explanation for faster settlement, but no one has yet shown that they are the reason.

The coverage gain is a different claim from transplant survival. Keeping transplanted fragments alive is one result, and coral spreading across more of a reef over time is another. The 12% speaks only to the second.

The formula itself is unsettled. The company is still comparing versions of Compound 7 on how well coral settles on each, with that comparison due by Dec. 1, 2025. The coating behind the early 12% may not be the one that ends up on reefs.

Compound 7 passes the test of a plausible idea with an early edge, but it has yet to show a measured, repeatable gain in living coral.

On the island of Perhentian, off Terengganu, Malaysia, Fizzy Transition Ventures tested its Compound 7 coating in open water on real transplanted coral, and the coated fragments died far less often than transplants elsewhere. About 0.9% of the coated transplants died each month, against 4.3% elsewhere, according to United Nations DESA. The company kept its yearly estimate cautious: "Survival appears to have been ca. 40% per year better with “Compound 7”," the company said. Its words "appears" and "ca." mark an estimate rather than a verdict. A result counted on a real reef still carries more weight than a lab claim. The open question is whether it holds when someone other than the maker does the counting.

Perhentian's reef is now in decline, which makes the island a hard place for any coral restoration. Fisheries Department monitoring reported by The Star places Perhentian among the islands where reef health is getting worse, along with Redang, Kapas, Tenggol, Tioman and Payar. Agriculture and Food Security Minister Mohamad Sabu says that monitoring found less live coral and more coral dying after bleaching. The survival figures come from the company's own testing, not from that monitoring. Nobody has yet shown how coated coral fares on reefs in their current state, so whether the coating keeps its edge in hotter water remains untested.

Keeping planted coral alive is a different achievement from bringing a reef back. The trial's survival figure covers only the transplants. Malaysia has set aside RM4mil under the 13th Malaysia Plan for its fisheries climate plan, The Star reports, and that plan includes habitat restoration programs, but none of the money is stated as going to reef materials. A good transplant record does not yet show whether Perhentian's reef as a whole is recovering, and for now, wider use of the coating depends on the maker.

Perhentian has shown that coated transplants can outlive transplants elsewhere in open water, but it still owes independent confirmation and evidence that the coating changes the reef, not only the corals placed on it.

ECO2Reef Systems is a plan for what a built reef should accomplish once it is in the water, not a new material for building one. Fizzy Transition Ventures BV presents it as the next step after its reef materials: a system that links restored coastlines with reefs to produce food, support biodiversity and capture carbon, according to United Nations DESA. That design packs three separate promises into one, a far larger claim than a sturdier reef block. Each of the three will need its own evidence before anyone can judge the system as a whole.

ECO2Reef remains a set of plans rather than a record of results. The gains in food, biodiversity and carbon storage are aims the developer has stated, not outcomes it has recorded. A reef that feeds people, shelters wildlife and stores carbon remains a goal for the company's work. Readers should judge ECO2Reef as that goal rather than as a product with results to weigh.

The company's ambitions extend beyond the reef to the people who live beside it. "Creating viable coral reefs in new locations can reduce poverty for local communities while offering significant support to this threatened ecosystem," Fizzy Transition Ventures BV said. The word "can" presents lower poverty as a possibility rather than a result, and the sentence makes that possibility depend on the reefs being viable first. Any gain for local incomes would have to follow proof that the reefs themselves survive and grow.

ECO2Reef has shown a goal, and it still owes evidence for every benefit it names.

Fizzy Transition Ventures has set a date to show how its reef materials perform in the warm tropical water where coral grows. The company planned lab and field tests in tropical conditions from late 2022 to 2025, with its findings due for publication in June 2026, according to United Nations DESA. Results from that water will count for more than any claim in a pitch or a product sheet, and the schedule allows several years of testing before the company must report them. Pairing lab work with field work gives those findings their value. Researchers can check whether a material that held up under controlled conditions also holds up in open water off Malaysia, where conditions are far harder to control. With a publication month set, readers will know when to look for an answer on whether these materials work.

A publication date commits the company to disclosing results, not to good ones. The published tests could confirm the claims made for the recycled concrete, the coating and the dissolving reef, or they could show that some of those claims do not hold. Until the company publishes, each material remains somewhere between promising and proven. Publication is the scheduled point at which that could change, and the outcome may differ from one material to the next.

The coming results will be the first chance to weigh each material on evidence from the water rather than on its maker's word.
