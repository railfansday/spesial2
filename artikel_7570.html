Rope bridges can help animals cross specific obstacles, Morera said, but long-term conservation requires protecting existing forests, restoring biological corridors and ensuring new development does not further fragment habitats.

But Avey-Arroyo said replacing a mother is nearly impossible. Young sloths spend about a year learning which leaves are safe to eat, which plants to avoid and how to navigate the forest along familiar routes. Mothers also teach them which leaves to seek out when suffering from parasites, she said.

Without that knowledge, even apparently healthy orphaned sloths can struggle to survive in the wild.

For Avey-Arroyo, whose work began after she and her husband rescued an orphaned sloth named Buttercup, knowing that some animals will never return to the forest is the hardest part of running the sanctuary.

Climate change could worsen the effects of fragmentation, as rising temperatures and prolonged droughts alter the conditions sloths depend on.

Small forest patches are more exposed to heat, wind and lower humidity than continuous forests, Morera said, reducing habitat quality even where trees remain standing.

Sloths rely on their surroundings to regulate body temperature and metabolism, moving between shaded and sunlit areas of the forest. Cliffe said prolonged periods of extreme heat can deplete their limited energy reserves, while cold conditions can disrupt digestion.
