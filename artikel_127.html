French officials eye social media’s role in promoting unrest.

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

The interior ministry estimated that 320,000 people had demonstrated nationwide, a smaller number than the previous two Saturdays. The police and protesters clashed in Paris and other cities.

Hundreds of thousands of protesters took to the streets in cities across France again on Saturday, the latest expression of national grievance that has led to clashes between protesters and the police and kept the country on edge.

As dusk fell and official rallies wound down, unauthorized marches splintered off in Paris, stoking fresh fears of chaos as night approached.

Adding to those fears: Halloween, which falls on Saturday. The French authorities, citing calls on social media for people to revolt that encouraged them to wear masks and costumes, warned that the risk of a new outbreak of violence was high.

In Paris, tensions began flaring between protesters and the police in the late afternoon, as officers fired tear gas at people who refused to disperse. The Paris police estimated that 70,000 people marched in the demonstration, compared with 110,000 last Saturday. After the main demonstration ended, hundreds of protesters, many masked and clad in black, formed an unauthorized procession in eastern Paris.

The interior ministry estimated that nationwide, 320,000 people demonstrated, compared with 410,000 last week. Protesters and the police also clashed in Marseille, Lyon, Toulouse, and Rennes.

Corentin Schnell, a 19-year-old engineering student, took part in the unauthorized march in Paris because, he said, he was angered by accounts of the police using tear gas and stun grenades against protesters in past weeks. “I’ve come to demonstrate and to try to provide medical care — to limit the damage, regardless of which side it’s on,” he said.

Earlier in the week, France’s interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, promised to deploy 70,000 police officers, matching the numbers on Oct. 24. That operation helped keep a lid on outbreaks of arson and looting, although then, too, there were clashes as the police tried to disperse the crowds in the evening.

There was ample fuel for a repeat of those combustible scenes. Students, who are returning to school on Monday after a two-week break, were not mollified by the government’s initial response to their demands. The Education Ministry agreed to cap some class sizes, offer low-interest loans to rebuild school buildings and phase out an unpopular online college admissions platform.

The students vowed to go on protesting. Their two-month-old movement has grown to include university students, public workers and veterans of the “yellow vest” movement that convulsed France from 2018 to 2020.

The authorities have said they are most worried about trouble breaking out after nightfall.

“We are seeing numerous calls to gather coming from the far left, the radicalized extreme left, and from certain radicalized ‘yellow vests’ websites, which are urging people to attend rallies or public gatherings starting at 8 p.m. in many cities across France,” Mr. Nuñez said at a news conference.

Some of the messages were violent, Mr. Nuñez said, calling for “revolution” and “insurrection.” Those gatherings will be forbidden, and any attempt to gather despite the ban will lead to arrests, he said.

Demonstrators have been encouraged on social media to turn up wearing Halloween costumes. Mr. Nuñez warned that it was a criminal offense to conceal one’s face during a demonstration that disrupted public order.

The authorized demonstration in Paris has come to an end, with some clashes with the police. In its place, a spontaneous procession of several hundred protesters took shape along Avenue Daumesnil in eastern Paris. Many were dressed in black and had their faces covered. Part of the crowd was singing and dancing to songs blasted on speakers. As the crowd got closer to a square with bars and restaurants full of customers, some waiters hurried to close outdoor seating areas as police officers arrived on motorcycles. Even with the heavy police presence, no clashes erupted and the protesters marched into the night.

Among the protesters in the unauthorized demonstration were Morgane Voisin, 22, and Corentin Schnell, 19, both of whom were wearing white outfits indicating that they were “street medics,” or those able to deliver first aid. Ms. Voisin, a sales consultant, said she came to demonstrate because her younger siblings had faced harsh conditions in high school. Mr. Schnell, an engineering student, said he was prompted to join when his little brother told him about getting tear-gassed and almost hit by a stun grenade at a protest earlier this month. “The mere fact that people are being injured by the police makes me furious,” Mr. Schnell said. “So I’ve come to demonstrate and to try to provide medical care — to limit the damage, regardless of which side it’s on.” On Saturday, the pair said they saw one person who had been injured in the head, but aid arrived quickly. “Long live youth!” Ms. Voisin said.

I can hear loud bangs at the end of the demonstration route and I can see many people running backward, as well as tear gas in the distance. Some protesters are throwing mortar-like fireworks.

The mood at the Paris protest has definitely changed. Though I can still hear some protesters singing, the protest has turned violent.

Police officers are now blocking many of the roads in the neighborhood at the end of the demonstration route. I saw a young woman surrounded by medics — she was bleeding from the face. I saw flames at the end of one avenue. I also saw police officers charge toward the crowd. Some residents are trying to make their way back home quickly, covering their faces to avoid tear gas.

Tensions in Paris continue to rise. Police are skirmishing with 200 to 300 rioters who have refused to disperse at the end of a rally. The Paris police said the rioters threw Molotov cocktails and fireworks at officers. A car was set afire.

Tensions are now flaring between protesters and the police in Paris. Police are firing rounds of tear gas at people, who are refusing to disperse. The Paris police estimated that 70,000 people marched in the demonstration, compared with 110,000 last Saturday.

The Interior Ministry estimated that 320,000 people took part in protests across the country today. That is somewhat fewer than last week (410,000) and the week before (550,000).

The demonstration in Paris remains peaceful, even festive. But police and protesters have clashed in Marseille, Lyon, Toulouse, and Rennes. Tear gas and water cannons have been deployed as the police try to disperse crowds. Violence typically breaks out at the end of these rallies, when people refuse to leave the scene.

I am walking along the authorized route for the demonstration in Paris. For the most part, the mood has been festive. Someone even dropped candy from his balcony, drawing cheers from the crowd.

After, I watched a half-dozen protesters dressed in black and wearing masks push trash bins in the middle of the crowd. One was already burning as they made their way, and they set the second on fire not far from where a police unit was stationed. Other demonstrators used water to put out the fire, causing a small clash with the ones wearing in black. “That’s no way to protest,” one of the demonstrators who put out the fire said.

The protest in Paris has a joyous and playful atmosphere. Brass bands are playing music, and many protesters have come dressed in Halloween costumes. Many said they wanted to help reverse the narrative set on Oct. 17 when riots spilled out of marches across France, leading to destruction and looting.

“I’m really worried about how the riots have been instrumentalized by the far right,” said Helen Olive, 50, who was among a group of parents who came dressed as “super parents.” “We want to turn that narrative around.”

Literature student Arthur Fleury, 20, arrived as a prisoner riding a dinosaur and wore a mask of the far-right politician Marine Le Pen. “Protests are about making demands but also having fun,” he said. “I was going for an absurd costume to capture the spirit of the movement.”

Early estimates from the French interior ministry suggest crowds are smaller today than the past two Saturdays. At 2 p.m., the ministry estimated that 15,000 people were taking part in 65 protests. That compares to 37,000 people last week at that time and 67,000 on Oct. 17. These numbers can be expected to grow as the day progresses. The ministry said it had arrested 27 people, compared with 217 by this time last Saturday. Protest organizers have yet to issue a crowd estimate; their numbers are typically far higher.

I’m at the Place de la Bastille. It is normally a bustling square, particularly on a beautiful, sunny Saturday. Now, police officers with shields and helmets patrol in groups. A loudspeaker blares a warning that gatherings and protests are forbidden to take place here by a police decree and that anyone who breaks it will be fined 135 euros.

Union and student organizers had again hoped to start their march here today, and again it was denied by the police and moved to the eastern edge of the city.

As France prepares for another round of protests, officials have grown increasingly concerned about the role played by social media in attracting people to demonstrations and inciting them to violent behavior.

Speculation that the government considered temporarily suspending several popular sites drew a denial from President Emmanuel Macron himself.

But in an interview on YouTube, Mr. Macron said that social media platforms would be required “to remove illegal content, and to be very vigilant about the use of certain features to prevent them from being misused by people who want to engage in looting or other such activities.”

That followed a report by a left-leaning online news site that Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu had sent a letter to leading telecommunications operators, asking them to be prepared to block Snapchat, TikTok and Instagram. Mr. Lecornu’s office declined to comment on the authenticity of the letter.

Of the four reported recipients, one did not respond to questions and two declined to comment. A spokesman for the fourth, Orange, the only one in which the state is a shareholder, declined to confirm that a letter was received but said that “like any other operator, we are required to comply with the obligations legally imposed on us by the competent authorities, in accordance with applicable law.”

The nationwide unrest in France began with a handful of student demonstrations at high schools in suburban Paris last month.

Now, in cities across the country, antigovernment marchers are protesting expensive living conditions, high fuel prices and conditions in schools. Using the unrest as a smoke screen for vandalism, rioters and looters have also joined in.

With the protests in their second month, the police have arrested thousands, and politicians are divided over how to respond.

Here is a breakdown of what has happened.

Soon after returning from the summer break, students at a high school in the working class Parisian suburb of Créteil staged a protest, according to videos posted to social media on Sept. 18. The high schoolers objected to inadequate resources and high workloads.

Within days, students elsewhere around Paris initiated similar demonstrations. At schools in the capital’s poorest suburbs, students blocked entrances to protest crumbling infrastructure, overcrowded classrooms and what they say are flaws in the admissions system for higher education.

By Sept. 29, the unrest had spread to around 400 schools around the country, according to rally organizers.

The union of high school students said the blockades were a protest against a “lack of teachers and staff, dilapidated schools, overcrowded schedules and classrooms.”

Students barricaded the entrances to some schools with obstacles including e-bikes, while others threw projectiles and set fire to trash cans.

The French education minister, Édouard Geffray, said that some of the students’ demands were “legitimate” but condemned the confrontational nature of their protests. He said that students had chased teachers and targeted school principals with firework-launching tubes.

By the end of September, nearly 80 people had been injured, and the authorities had detained more than 440, including many minors.

Within days, the number of schools engulfed in the protests had doubled. According to the Interior Ministry, by Oct. 2, they had spread to more than 1,000 schools across France, including 220 that were blockaded. University students also began taking part.

Around this time, the authorities said that the protests were mutating from legitimate expressions of educational grievances into something more dangerous, accusing people of shooting mortar-style fireworks, burning public buildings and looting.

By Oct. 2, nearly 400 police officers and over 230 school employees and students had been injured. The number of arrests climbed to 3,000.

The actions widened in October to encompass labor unions, parents and other activists, as different grievances converged into a mass protest.

On Oct. 6, around 256,000 people marched through Paris and 40 other French cities, according to the Interior Ministry. A union of high school students said protesters had occupied some 1,800 high schools.

The protests were mostly peaceful, but a fire was ignited in Lyon, and police officers used tear gas, including in Paris. The total number of arrested rose to 6,000, the authorities said. A major union of French judges and prosecutors and a human rights group accused the police of using disproportionate force, including against children as young as 14.

Two days later, on Oct. 8, smaller demonstrations were staged throughout the country, rallying around 71,500 people. The following week, the Interior Ministry said that 160,000 people joined marches on Oct. 13 across France.

More than half a million people joined 110 antigovernment marches throughout France on Oct. 17, according to an official estimate. A major union, the C.G.T., put the number even higher, at 800,000. Using either count, it was the largest day of protest since the demonstrations began in September.

Many of the marchers were peaceful, but some were not. Around the country, rioters vandalized around 1,500 shops, the authorities said. In Paris, where over 100,000 people marched peacefully, a few hundred rioters faced off against officers. According to the police, the rioters burned cars and smashed storefronts.

In the large town of Limoges, in central France, rioters set fire to the 19th-century City Hall. In a moment captured on video, the building’s spire collapsed in flames.
