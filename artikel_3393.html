6 Greatest Classic Books To Read in the Winter

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The end-of-the-year holidays are some of the best days to go back to a beloved classic book for an overdue revisit. After all, some books have a truly timeless quality to them, the kind that makes revisiting them not only possible but outright necessary. You know the type — these books may be familiar, and you might know the plot by heart, but there's something about re-experiencing them that makes it worth the time.

Everyone is different and likes different things, but some books are proper classics, with a universal quality to them that makes them ideal to experience at the end of the year, when the temperatures drop and the holiday cheer takes over us all. Here, we list some of the best classic books to read during the winter, the proper masterpieces that never get old and make for perfect companions as we race towards a new year. To keep things interesting, we'll try to only include non-Christmas books, so no Christmas Carol.

Out in the wily, windy moors, one of literature's greatest passions takes shape. Emily Brontë's classic of the Gothic genre has become one of the most universally acclaimed and beloved books of all time. Wuthering Heights tells the multigenerational story of the Lintons and Earnshaws, two families in the Yorkshire moors whose fates become intertwined. In particular, the book concerns the complicated affair between Catherine Earnshaw and Heathcliff, a bond that transcends time.

Wuthering Heights is among the most singular works of Gothic fiction ever written. Equal parts tragic and ruthless, the book is a tale of obsession and trauma, particularly how resentment and emotional violence pass from one generation to the next. Few books are as haunting or atmospheric, with Brontë crafting a singular, evocative portrayal of the Yorkshire countryside where the moors become a character themselves. Thanks to its supernatural, wistful atmosphere and timeless central story, Wuthering Heights is the perfect book to read come the winter season.

From one Brontë sister, we go to the next. Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre is a bildungsroman with one of literature's most compelling romances at its center. The book tells the story of the titular heroine, chronicling her life from her youth at her cruel aunt's mercy to her stint at the unforgiving Lowood Institution for girls, all the way to her young adulthood as the governess of a French girl at Thornfield Hall. There, she meets and eventually falls for the brooding owner, Mr. Rochester.

Along with Wuthering Heights, Jane Eyre might just be the foundational text for the Gothic romance genre. Brontë depicts a vivid, complex, and enthralling psychological portrait of the heroine, crafting one of literature's most evocative and relatable figures in the process. For his part, Mr. Rochester is the Byronic hero par excellence, an aloof and sardonic man who is as off-putting as he is alluring. Jane Eyre is an incredibly complete reading experience, offering everything you could possibly want from such a tale: the beats might be familiar, but the story is so alive that it's impossible to resist, no matter how many times you've experienced it before.

Louisa May Alcott's Little Women is one of the most endearing and beloved works of the 19th century. The story is well-known, centering on the March sisters, particularly Jo, a spirited, tomboyish, staunchly independent girl who longs to be a writer. Across the novel, Jo and her sisters — Meg, Amy, and Beth — go from childhood to young adulthood, falling in love and discovering what the world can offer while experiencing joy, sadness, grief, and love.

There's a reason why Little Women is often taught in schools. There is such a universal quality to the writing, with Alcott so accurately capturing the feeling of endless possibility and sheer enthusiasm that comes with being a child. Alongside that, the novel also captures the nostalgic quality of growing up and leaving behind the certainty and safety of home and family to venture out and discover oneself in pursuit of one's ambitions. Little Women is perhaps the ultimate coming-of-age story, yet it feels so wholesome and familiar — coziness in literary form. Also, Christmas plays a small but pivotal part in the overall plot, to the point where many consider Little Women a holiday classic.

The whodunit genre is a favorite of readers, and very few whodunits are more masterful than this Agatha Christie gem. Murder on the Orient Express follows ingenious and resourceful detective Hercule Poirot as he goes from Istanbul to London aboard the prestigious, titular train. His pleasant trip is derailed when a murder occurs in the middle of the night in a locked compartment. With the train stopped due to heavy snow, Poirot realizes they are now trapped with a murderer on board.

The premise is so well-known that it has practically become archetypal, and the famous twist has similarly entered the cultural lexicon. Yet, none of that diminishes the experience of revisiting Murder on the Orient Express. Perhaps it's Christie's command over the genre, her ability to craft a twisting, thrilling mystery seamlessly. Perhaps it's the cold, remote setting that makes everything feel more urgent and strangely welcoming. Perhaps it's just the inherent power of the whodunit. No matter the reason, and much like the train itself, Murder on the Orient Express is a classic that never goes out of style. The snowy atmosphere is so powerful that you might even feel a chill as you turn the pages.

You need at least one proper holiday classic to read this time of year, and what better book to go with than The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe? C. S. Lewis' charming classic is set in World War II and centers on the four Pevensie children, who relocate to a remote manor in the English countryside during the Blitz. There, they discover a wardrobe that transports them to Narnia, a magical land of magic and talking animals, stuck in perpetual winter and ruled by the cruel White Witch.

A pillar of the portal fantasy genre, The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe is a beloved piece of children's literature that has somehow matured with the years without changing anything about its text or message. It's also a proper Christmas classic, the only one on the list, because of its setting and strict adherence to many of the common tropes of the holiday, including the appearance of Father Christmas himself. Besides, if you're also eagerly awaiting Greta Gerwig's upcoming adaptation of The Magician's Nephew, there's no better way to get ready.

Finally, let's end with a more unconventional choice. Stephen King's The Shining tells the story of Jack Torrance, a struggling writer and recovering alcoholic who accepts the position of caretaker for the Overlook Hotel during the off-season, bringing along his wife, Wendy, and young son, Danny. However, the Overlook is plagued by dangerous, all-consuming supernatural forces that begin corrupting Jack. Meanwhile, young Danny can sense the hotel's evil because he has "the shining."

The Shining has perhaps been somewhat eclipsed by Stanley Kubrick's 1980 movie adaptation, which King famously detests. However, the book is far more intimate and equally terrifying despite dealing with other themes. The author drew from his own process of recovering from addiction, using the Overlook's powerful influence as a metaphor for countless, very human flaws. Thus, the book can either be seen as a traditional tale of haunting or a more elaborate study of the human psyche and how one's demons can easily take over and destroy one's personal connections in the real world. No matter the way you interpret it, The Shining remains a highly effective chiller with a freezing atmosphere of dread, perfect for those who like their holiday reading with a bit more bite.
