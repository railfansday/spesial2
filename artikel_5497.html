5 tech support scam red flags the FTC says to watch for

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The FTC describes how tech support scams work, the disguises they use, and how to avoid, recover from and report them.

People who see a warning that their computer is infected usually want someone to fix it fast, and tech support scammers count on that wish. The scammer shows up with a fix for a problem the victim didn't know they had, and the victim, grateful for the help, lets the scammer in. Older Americans reported far larger fraud losses of all kinds in 2025 than five years earlier, with adults 60 and over reporting about $3 billion in losses that year, up from $600 million in 2020, according to the FTC. Tech support fraud is one of several familiar schemes aimed at older people. The National Institute on Aging lists government impersonators, fake prizes and grandparent scams among the other common ones. A reader who learns how tech support fraud works learns a pattern that scammers use again and again. Whatever disguise they wear, the scammers need one thing from their target: payment before the target stops to check.

Tech support scammers rely on hurry, and they push victims to act before they have time to think or call someone else. The pressure itself is the clearest warning sign. The National Institute on Aging lists threats and pressure to act immediately among the tactics scammers use. A caller who won't let you wait has revealed the fraud before saying anything technical. That means spotting the scam takes no computer expertise, only a refusal to be hurried by someone who contacted you first. A person who expects that pressure can recognize it whenever it appears, whether with the first warning or with the final demand for money.

Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Consumer Advice set out to explain how tech support scams operate and what consumers should do about them. The guide goes beyond fake pop-up warnings to scams in which the "helper" claims to work for the government, and to fake renewal bills that carry the names of familiar security brands. It also speaks to people who have already paid a scammer or handed over access to their computer. Someone who has lost money or control of a machine to a fake technician still has steps to take, and the response to these scams continues well past the moment of loss.

A tech support scam starts by convincing someone that their computer has a problem it does not have. Scammers send a fake warning, get remote access to the computer and then demand payment, according to the FTC. They present the fee as the cost of removing a supposed malicious program, so the victim pays to get rid of a threat the scammer made up. Remote access is the turning point. The victim gives a stranger control of the same computer the warning said was in danger, and from then on the scammer decides what the victim sees on the screen. When the scammer finally asks for money, the victim has already said yes twice, first to the warning and then to the stranger's access, and paying feels like the next step.

People can't screen out these scams by watching for an unusual source, because scammers use the ordinary ways people get in touch every day. The first contact can be a call from a supposed computer technician, a text message or an online display ad. That covers the phone, text messages and the web browser, and no single channel is safe to trust. The real warning sign is the message: an unprompted claim that something is wrong with the computer, from someone ready to fix it.

The payment request is where the scam is easiest to recognize. A real repair business has no reason to insist on payment that is hard to take back, but a thief does. These scammers ask for wire transfers, bank transfers, cryptocurrency, payment apps and gift cards. The National Institute on Aging, part of the National Institutes of Health, advises people not to pay a phone caller with a gift card they buy. Anyone asked to cover a computer repair with gift cards has good reason to hang up.

Whatever the screen says, people can learn more from how a "helper" wants to be paid than from any technical explanation.

Learning to distrust a frightening pop-up does not protect a person from tech support fraud, because scammers also run the same scheme under other disguises. Some pose as government workers offering help, and others send bogus renewal invoices that appear to come from familiar security companies, according to the Federal Trade Commission. In each case, the scammers borrow the name of an authority the target already trusts. Checking a caller's identity does little good when the identity is the part the scammers keep changing. What the caller asks for stays the same, and that is the thing to judge.

A scammer who poses as a government worker claims the authority of an office so the target will obey instead of doubt. The details that make such a call sound official, including a badge number and a case number, are fake, and the scammer invents them on the call. A precise-sounding credential proves nothing about the person reciting it. Any caller who mentions a "federal safety locker" is lying, because no such thing exists. The surest test requires no knowledge of agencies or badges. "Someone who works for the government will never demand payment. Not in cash. Not in gold," the FTC said.

The fake renewal invoice carries a security brand's name, such as Geek Squad, McAfee or Norton, and it gives the target 24 hours to call and dispute the charge. That deadline pushes people to pick up the phone before they stop to ask whether they ever signed up for the service. The call itself is the trap. Once the victim enters financial details to get the promised refund, the scammer claims to have refunded too much money. The victim set out to stop a charge and is now told they owe the scammer money.

A refund that leaves the customer owing money is not a refund.

Beating a tech support scam takes far less than its many disguises suggest. A scheme that can pose as a computer technician, a government worker or a familiar security company might seem to call for a long checklist of costumes to memorize. Instead, the Federal Trade Commission offers two rules for spotting the fraud before any money or information is lost. Those rules are meant for the first moments of contact, while the scam is still only a phone call, a pop-up or a text message and the target has handed over nothing. The earliest moment is therefore the cheapest one to stop the fraud. Once a person has paid or let a stranger into the computer, that person is no longer preventing the scam but recovering from it.

The short length of this defense is what makes it useful against a scam that depends on hurry. Advice that a person has to look up does no good in the middle of a pressured call, with a supposed technician on the line insisting that the computer is in danger and the problem must be fixed now. The FTC presents its advice as two things to remember, and keeping them in mind can help a person avoid the scam. A person can bring the rules to mind in less time than the scammer needs to deliver the pitch. That takes away the time advantage the whole scheme relies on, and it leaves the decision with the person who was called rather than the person who called.

Even rules this simple do no good if a person remembers them a moment too late, so the FTC also has advice for people after a scam succeeds.

Scammers make the end of the fraud feel final, with the money sent and a stranger already inside the computer, but a victim does not have to accept that the matter is closed. The FTC advises people who have already paid a scammer or shared information to take action. The useful question for a victim is what the scammer actually got. A victim may have paid a scammer, given out personal information, or let the scammer into the computer. These are three separate problems, and a victim needs to know which ones apply before deciding what to fix. Someone who never paid a cent may still have something to secure, because letting a stranger into a computer is a loss of its own even when no money changed hands.

The scammer set the pace for the whole fraud, and after the loss the victim can use speed for their own protection. A person who gave a scammer a username and password should change that password right away, according to the agency's advice. Speed alone is not enough. A hurried switch to an easy password replaces one weak point with another, and the new password needs to be strong. A victim can change a password at once and without help, before making any other decision, which makes it a sensible place to start.

A new password protects an account but does nothing about money already sent, personal information already given out or a computer a stranger has been inside. The FTC lists specific steps for each of those three situations on a separate page, and anyone facing them should follow those steps rather than treat a password change as the end of the job.

Before a scam, the defense is refusing to be rushed; after one, it is refusing to wait.

Hanging up on a fake technician can feel like the end of the matter, but the call is still worth reporting once it's over. The Federal Trade Commission asks people to report a tech support scam when the scammers contact them, whether or not they lost any money. A person who recognized the scam and hung up has as much reason to report it as someone who lost savings. Someone who avoided the trap still knows how these scammers are working right now. That knowledge is most useful to the agency before the scammers' next target pays.

The agency can count only the fraud that people tell it about. Its figures on fraud against older Americans include only the losses that older adults reported, according to the FTC. A loss that nobody reports never enters those figures, and each scam that goes unreported makes the problem look smaller than it really is. Congress received the agency's account of how it protects older people, and lawmakers see the same reported numbers. When people stay quiet, the version of the problem that reaches Congress is smaller than the real one.

A tech support scam begins with a stranger offering help that nobody asked for. It can end with the person who received that call helping others. Anyone who files a report makes the agency's record of these scams more complete.
