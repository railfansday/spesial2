One of the best-known stories of the Bible is that of Jonah, a man who gets himself swallowed by a whale (actually, a “great fish”). Why this happens is probably less popularly understood. The reason is that Jonah has defied God. The Lord wanted him to go to the city of Nineveh and preach to the people there about their sins. He refused and sought escape to the city of Tarshish via a ship leaving from Joppa.

As you might imagine, God is not pleased. A storm arises from the sea, and the ship gets dangerously tossed about. When the crew draws lots to determine who is at fault, Jonah’s number comes up. He admits he is Hebrew and has defied God. He volunteers himself as a sacrifice, and they toss him overboard. As expected, the waters become calm.

What happens next is the part most of us know about. Jonah is swallowed by a sea monster. He suffers in “the belly of hell” for three days. Finally, God forgives him and talks to the fish, who “vomited out Jonah upon the dry land.” Jonah goes on to do what he was supposed to do, now wiser from the experience.

This story has burrowed deep into our cultural psyche. Famed psychologist Abraham Maslow found the tale so compelling he came up with a term: the Jonah Complex. To suffer this complex is to fail to follow one’s true calling. You deny yourself, and you will descend into a dark place, where, hopefully, you will realize your errors and return to your true life path. You’ll emerge from the belly of the beast you’d so recklessly volunteered for and find the bright light of day.

In a new movie, "Whalefall," a young man takes Jonah’s original harrowing route. He denies the grief he should be feeling over the death of his father by suicide. This boy has lived a tortured life, marked by a tough relationship with his father. A gruff old salt who’d led scuba tours, dad constantly berated his son for a lack of fortitude and insufficient love of the sea. Father and son fail to understand each other. It is a classic story of the alienation that can arise between “tough” dads and their sensitive sons. The sons feel they are not good enough, and the fathers know they are missing something, but can’t see through their own pain to do anything about it.

The idea that fathers no longer have the tools to raise their sons properly has long been a popular topic in pop psychology. Fathers, we have been told, are emotionally unavailable and do not know how to inculcate in their sons the proper virtues of self-sufficiency and human connection. Sadly, this argument often winds up blaming domineering “viper” mothers who suffocate their sons and diminish their husbands’ role.

"Whalefall" taps the ongoing cultural conversation of finding the father with a wild take on the Jonah story. The boy, on a solo scuba mission to recover remnants of dad (who chose to drown himself rather than wither away from lung cancer), gets swallowed by a sperm whale. The event, wild on its face, is filmed realistically.

Inside the whale, the boy experiences a trip to hell. The space is claustrophobic, the air is poisonous, and his wounds become more and more grievous. In this surreal space, we experience a series of flashbacks that sketch out the father’s failures and the son’s desire to connect with him. Ultimately, the whale itself becomes the father, and through conversation, the boy realizes that his dad did, in fact, love him, even if he could not show it. The whale-father ultimately supplies the boy with the tools to help him escape. Like the Jonah story, he is expelled from the beast to reach the light of day, transformed by his experience.

"Whalefall" offers the metaphorical father redemption arc that delivers a novel take on Maslow’s Jonah Complex. What dad was unable to do in life, he succeeds in doing through a dying sperm whale on the Pacific coast. The boy takes responsibility for his own life success, and the father can finally be properly grieved over. This film, while shot realistically, is in fact a mythic treatment of an enduring social problem. Does it succeed? That depends on the viewer.
