Ryan Serhant’s warning to brokers: Stop protecting the business you used to have

Tanggal: 2026-11-09

Speaking at NAR NXT 2026, Ryan Serhant argued that brokerages should flip their model, deliver leads and support first, then recruit. He said AI should increase production and that office costs must be justified by revenue.  AI Summary

When Ryan Serhant, founder and CEO of SERHANT., launched his brokerage six years ago, he wasn’t interested in following the traditional brokerage playbook. In fact, he believed many of the industry’s largest firms were too focused on protecting their existing businesses rather than preparing for the future.

“The thing that scared me the most was they were very, very focused on protecting the business that they had instead of building for where they’re going,” Serhant said during a conversation at the National Association of Realtors‘ (NAR) 2026 NXT conference.

Now, as his firm expands across the country, Serhant, whose brokerage is RealTrends Verified, is betting on AI, lead generation and a lean operating model to drive growth. He shared what he believes brokers are getting wrong about everything from recruiting and office space to technology investments and what they should be doing instead.

Here are seven takeaways from the conversation.

Serhant says that the script should be flipped when it comes to how brokers think about agents. Rather than ask what agents can contribute to the brokerage, they instead should focus on what the brokerage can deliver to its agents.

For SERHANT., that means lead generation, brand awareness and an operating structure designed to remain efficient as the business grows. “It’s like I want to build a company for me, the way I would want to be led, my number one job is to generate leads.”

He compared the brokerage’s role to that of a power plant, providing the infrastructure agents need to grow their businesses. “The brokerage’s business is to provide electricity. For me, SERHANT. is a power plant, and we provide electricity to our agents and to our leaders in every market that we’re in.”

That value proposition, he said, must be compelling enough to help agents reach higher price points, increase transaction volume and attract customers. “Agents want leads more than they care about split,” Serhant said.

While brokerages are investing in AI tools, Serhant questioned whether most of those investments will generate meaningful returns. “But they won’t. I mean, most of them won’t, to be honest, a tool is only as good as the person who will use it.”

Rather than introduce another technology platform for agents to learn, SERHANT. initially deployed its AI product, S.mple, to support staff to automate tasks and free agents to focus on sales and relationships.

The objective was not simply to save time. It was to increase transactions per agent and improve the brokerage’s operating margins. “And what we saw was the agents who were using S.mple grew their production by 44% on average in one year. But the super agents, the ones who used it and broke it every day, doubled production in the first year in a declining market.”

Serhant said the next evolution involves using AI to handle increasingly complex workflows with less human intervention. For brokers evaluating AI investments, his approach raises a question: Are those tools actually increasing production, or are they simply adding another expense?

Serhant doesn’t believe the growing availability of AI will eliminate the importance of real estate agents. Instead, he sees an opportunity for agents to spend more time on what technology cannot easily replicate.

“If intelligence is cheap, trust is expensive. Trust is now a luxury item.” His strategy is to use AI to take administrative work off agents’ plates, allowing them to focus on building customer relationships.

“How can I develop more trust with more agents, and how can the agents develop more trust with more customers by buying back their time?”

For brokerage leaders, the distinction is important. Technology may make agents more productive, but Serhant believes trust remains a competitive advantage.

SERHANT. initially built its business without physical brokerage offices, choosing instead to invest in lead generation and technology. As the company expanded, Serhant said it established guidelines for when opening an office would make financial sense, including reaching $1 billion in annual transactions.

“Anyone who says they need an office is clinging to a business that used to be, and I don’t want to work with them,” he said. His point is that brokerage expenses must be tied to revenue and productivity rather than traditional expectations of what a real estate company should look like.

Serhant said one of his priorities is to “create the leanest, meanest efficiency model, so that the support, the expense, doesn’t outrun revenue in any market we happen to find ourselves in.”

As SERHANT. expands into new markets, Serhant said the company recruits founding agents who help establish its presence and attract additional agents. He said the brokerage does not rely on financial incentives to persuade agents to join.

“We don’t pay anybody. They all come to us organically. Every agent who’s come to us leaves money on the table because they come for the opportunity and they stay for the results,” he said.

Serhant argued that agents recruited primarily through financial incentives are likely to remain focused on those incentives. “If they come for the money, they stay for the money. Just like a bad marriage, right?”

His preferred approach is to build a brokerage with a recognizable brand, lead opportunities and tools that enable agents to produce more than they could elsewhere.

Asked how he maintains SERHANT.’s culture as the company expands, Serhant offered one an observations about leadership. “I define culture as the behavior you allow. It’s not policy guidelines. It’s not the employee handbook. It’s [not] the people you bring in, but culture is defined by the behavior you allow to exist, not the behavior you encourage.”

Serhant acknowledged that he made mistakes early in the company’s growth by allowing high-producing individuals to remain despite behavior that negatively affected the organization. “It’s the hardest thing to do. Ah, this person’s so good at what they do, but they just suck at it as a human. It is always so much better to chop out the poison and let the rest rise to the top.”

For brokers facing pressure to retain productive agents, his message was that short-term production should not come at the expense of the company’s culture.

During the audience Q&A, a small brokerage owner asked Serhant how firms with fewer than 25 agents should approach a changing housing market. His advice was to stop relying on the business strategies that worked under different conditions. “Sometimes there are markets that reward us. Sometimes there are markets that punish us.”

Serhant suggested that brokers look for opportunities in other segments, including multifamily, leasing and commercial real estate, when their traditional sources of transactions decline. “We get frustrated because we get stuck trying to do things that used to work instead of being a little uncomfortable and pushing forward to do the things that could work.”

The message applies beyond small brokerages. Throughout the conversation, Serhant returned to the importance of challenging established business models, keeping expenses in check and building a company that can adapt. For brokerage leaders, the takeaway is that surviving a difficult market may require more than waiting for sales to rebound. It may mean reconsidering how their companies generate business, support agents and define success.
