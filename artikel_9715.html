CompTIA Network+ N10-009: The Exam That Rewards Troubleshooters, Not Memorizers

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Most people study for CompTIA Network+ the way they studied for a school exam: flashcards, acronym lists, and a quiet hope that the test asks them to define things. Then they sit down in front of N10-009 and discover the exam has other plans. It hands you a broken network and asks what you would do first.

That is the whole exam in one sentence. Network+ is not a vocabulary test. It is a troubleshooting test that happens to use networking vocabulary. Once you study with that in mind, the exam gets dramatically easier — and the knowledge actually sticks for the job afterward.

The current Network+ exam, N10-009, is 90 questions with a passing score of 720 on a 100–900 scale. CompTIA splits it across five domains, and the split tells you where to spend your time:

Read that list again. Troubleshooting alone is worth nearly a quarter of the exam, and it bleeds into the other four domains too — a security question is often a troubleshooting question wearing a different coat. If your study plan is 80% memorization and 20% practice, flip the ratio.

Every Network+ candidate memorizes the seven OSI layers. Almost none of them practice thinking in layers, which is what the exam tests. When a scenario says users on one floor lost connectivity after a switch was replaced, the exam wants you to work the layers: is it physical (cable, port, power), data link (MAC, VLAN, duplex mismatch), network (wrong gateway, bad route), or higher up (DNS, DHCP)?

Here is a drill that takes ten minutes and pays for itself many times over. Take any symptom — "the printer works for some people but not others," "Wi-Fi is fine in the morning and dies at noon," "one website will not load but everything else does" — and force yourself to name one plausible cause at each layer, then pick the most likely layer to check first and say why. Do that for twenty symptoms and you have trained the exact muscle the exam grades. Flashcards cannot do this. Scenarios can.

The same applies to ports and protocols. Knowing that DNS is UDP/TCP 53 is table stakes. The exam asks what happens when DNS fails but IP connectivity works, or which port a firewall rule is blocking when HTTPS works and SSH does not. Learn each service as a story — what breaks when it breaks — not as a row in a table.

Subnetting is where Network+ candidates lose the most points for the least reason. It feels mathematical and intimidating, so people postpone it, then cram it the night before, then burn exam time doing long division under pressure.

The fix is boring and effective: practice subnetting for fifteen minutes a day, every day, for three weeks. Not an hour once a week — fifteen minutes daily. By the end of week one you can find a network address and broadcast address without thinking. By week two you can carve a /24 into the right-sized subnets for a scenario. By week three you can answer a subnetting question in under a minute and move on, which is exactly what the clock demands.

Two rules make this work. First, always practice with scenario wording, not bare math: "a branch office needs 28 hosts, which subnet fits?" rather than "subnet 192.168.1.0/24 into /27s." Second, never use a calculator while practicing. The exam gives you no subnet calculator, and your hands need to know the powers of two the way a musician knows scales.

CompTIA publishes a troubleshooting methodology, and N10-009 tests it directly: identify the problem, establish a theory, test the theory, establish a plan, implement, verify, document. Candidates who skip the methodology questions lose easy marks because the answers feel like common sense — until four options all sound sensible and only one follows the methodology's order.

The trap is always the same: an option that jumps to a fix before testing a theory, or one that skips documentation at the end. When a question describes a technician's next step, ask yourself which methodology step they are on. If the step order is wrong, the answer is wrong, no matter how reasonable the fix sounds.

You cannot learn cabling, VLANs, and wireless standards purely from a book. The implementations domain rewards people who have touched the technology — even virtually. A free network simulator and an afternoon are enough: build a small network, break it deliberately, and fix it. Configure a VLAN and watch traffic stop flowing to the wrong ports. Set a duplex mismatch and watch the interface counters climb. Misconfigure a DHCP scope and see clients fail in exactly the way the exam describes.

Each of these experiments takes twenty minutes and creates a memory that a chapter cannot. When the exam later describes the same symptom, you will not be recalling a paragraph — you will be recalling the afternoon you caused it yourself.

Weeks 1–2: Foundations. Networking fundamentals and implementations. Learn the OSI model as a diagnostic tool, master IPv4 addressing and subnetting basics, and get comfortable with cabling and wireless standards. End each study session with ten scenario questions.

Weeks 3–4: Operations and security. Monitoring tools, documentation habits, backup and recovery, and the security controls that show up on the exam — segmentation, ACLs, VPNs, and wireless security. Start your daily fifteen minutes of subnetting now if you have not already.

Week 5: Troubleshooting immersion. This is the biggest domain, so give it a full week. Work through scenario after scenario: read the symptom, name the layer, pick the tool (ping, tracert, nslookup, netstat, cable tester), and justify the next step. Time yourself.

Week 6: Practice exams and review. Take full timed practice tests under exam conditions — 90 questions, no pauses, no notes. Review every wrong answer by tracing it back to the domain and the concept, not just memorizing the right letter. Two or three full simulations in this week will tell you honestly whether you are ready.

Ninety questions sounds generous until the scenario questions start eating the clock. Flag long questions and move on — answer the quick ones first, then return with a fresh brain and a known time budget. Read the last sentence of a scenario question first: it tells you what is actually being asked, and the paragraph above it is often full of distractors.

And when two answers both look right, pick the one that follows the troubleshooting methodology or the one that is least disruptive. Network+ loves the least-invasive correct fix. Rebooting the server is almost never the exam's answer; checking the cable, the port, or the configuration usually is.

N10-009 rewards a specific kind of candidate: not the person who memorized the most definitions, but the person who can look at a broken network and reason their way to the fault, layer by layer. Study like a troubleshooter — scenarios over flashcards, labs over lectures, fifteen minutes of subnetting a day — and the exam stops being a memory contest and starts being a demonstration of something you can actually do.

If you want to test where you stand before exam day, work through realistic Network+ practice questions and pay attention to why you miss the ones you miss. The pattern in your misses is your study plan.
