ESP32-P4 and 2-Lane MIPI DSI: How to Pick a Display That Actually Lights Up

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Posted on Nov 4 • Edited on Nov 4 • Originally published at lcdhc.com

The ESP32-P4 looks like an easy win for a smart display: a fast application processor, LVGL support, and a built-in MIPI DSI host. Then the panel arrives, the firmware compiles, and nothing lights up — or the image is snowy, torn, or the wrong colour.

In most cases the panel is not "broken". It was selected against the wrong assumptions. The ESP32-P4's DSI host has two hard limits that decide everything else:

In command mode, the host sends a partial update only when content changes, and the panel stores the frame in its own GRAM. In video mode, the host sends complete frames continuously, synchronised to the refresh cycle, and the panel needs no video memory.

The ESP32-P4 only does the second one. That has a direct consequence many first-time designs miss: the frame buffer lives on the ESP32-P4 side, in PSRAM, and is read by the DSI controller via DMA.

As a rule of thumb, an 800 x 1280 RGB565 frame is about 2 MB, so double buffering is about 4 MB and triple buffering about 6 MB. A 16 MB PSRAM module is usually enough for display work; 32 MB leaves room for a camera or AI workload alongside it.

A panel that only offers a command interface, or whose firmware depends on partial updates, should be ruled out for the ESP32-P4.

Almost every MIPI panel on the market is labelled 4-lane, including common JD9365 and ILI9881C designs. That label is often misleading, because lane count is usually a configuration item, not a property of the glass.

Most of those driver ICs can be switched from 4 lanes to 2 lanes with an initialisation register. The register addresses and commands differ by IC, so the right question to ask a supplier is not "Is this a 2-lane panel?" but:

"Do you supply a 2-lane initialisation sequence for this panel, with the matching register configuration?"

A supplier who can answer that in writing, with code, can save weeks. A panel that is factory-locked to 4 lanes, with no 2-lane sequence available, should be excluded.

The full selection guide, including the size table from 3.5" to 10.1", is here: ESP32 MIPI DSI Display: 2-Lane MIPI LCD Guide.

The ESP32-P4's per-lane range is roughly 480 Mbps to 1,500 Mbps, with 2 lanes giving about 3 Gbps in total. A useful screening rule is:

480 Mbps ≤ final per-lane rate ≤ 1,500 Mbps, after a ~20% margin.

Worked example, 800 x 1280 at 60 Hz, RGB888, 2 lanes: the pixel clock works out to about 62.4 MHz, the raw rate to about 1,498 Mbps, and the per-lane rate after protocol overhead to about 1,077 Mbps — about 1,293 Mbps with margin. That fits, but only just.

Switch the same panel to RGB565 and the requirement falls to roughly 862–930 Mbps, with plenty of headroom. That is why RGB565 is the practical default for medium and large panels on this chip, even though it gives 65K colours instead of 16.7M.

Note the floor as well as the ceiling: a small 800 x 480 panel in RGB565 can calculate below 480 Mbps. In that case, clamp the configured rate to 480–500 Mbps, or use RGB888 at about 550 Mbps.

All of these sizes are available as 2-lane MIPI panels from 3.5" to 10.1", with touch options and custom FPC designs. The detailed guide is here: ESP32 MIPI DSI Display: 2-Lane MIPI LCD Guide.

Pick a panel that is configurable to 2 lanes, confirm video mode, run the bandwidth formula before fixing the resolution, and check the FPC pin by pin rather than by pin count. Do those four things, and driver bring-up, LVGL integration and touch debugging become routine work instead of a weeks-long hunt.
