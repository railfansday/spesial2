Job Crafting at 25

Posted November 2, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Twenty-five years ago, Amy Wrzesniewski and Jane Dutton published a paper that asked readers to look at work a little bit differently. Rather than viewing jobs as something organizations designed and employees simply carried out, they argued that employees could reshape their work in ways that make it more meaningful, engaging, and sustainable. They called the process job crafting.

I recently spent several days sifting through two special issues of the Journal of Business and Psychology, 41(3) and 41(4), marking the 25th anniversary. Together, they contain 19 papers. Individually, each paper tackles a fairly specific question. Collectively, though, they tell a much bigger story. I've whittled that bigger story down to three takeaways that really stood out to me.

The first thing that struck me was how much the questions have evolved over 25 years.

The original paper introduced job crafting as a new way of thinking about work. Unsurprisingly, much of the early research focused on understanding whether job crafting was associated with outcomes like engagement, well-being, performance, and job satisfaction. That was an important first step. Researchers first needed to establish what job crafting was, how it could be measured, and whether it was linked to meaningful workplace outcomes.

Reading these special issues, though, it became clear that the conversation has moved forward. Very few papers were still asking whether job crafting was generally associated with positive outcomes (we’ve established that), and the ones that did were mostly examining the issue through the lens of contextual factors. Instead, researchers were asking much more specific questions about how the process actually unfolds.

For example, Parker et al. (2026) examined whether the relationship between work characteristics and job crafting is reciprocal rather than one-directional, arguing that employees shape their jobs while their jobs simultaneously shape their opportunities to craft. Another, Rudolph et al. (2026), explored whether the effects of autonomy and workload follow nonlinear patterns, suggesting that too little or too much of either may actually reduce employees' ability to craft their work.

Other papers asked equally nuanced questions. Does job crafting operate differently for younger and older employees (Schepp & Boehm, 2026)? What role does job crafting play for interns transitioning into the workforce (Kim et al., 2026)? How might employees with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder use job crafting to better align their work with their strengths and challenges (Weinhardt et al., 2026)?

The broader message from all these papers is that there doesn't appear to be a single formula for effective job crafting. What works depends on the person, the job, and the situation. That may not sound particularly exciting, but I actually see it as a sign that this research domain has matured. Once researchers become reasonably confident that an idea is important, they start asking more interesting questions about how people can make it work. After 25 years, job crafting research seems to have reached that point.

The second thing that struck me was how much the idea itself has expanded.

The original paper described three broad ways employees could craft their work: by changing the tasks they performed, the relationships they developed, and the way they thought about their jobs. Twenty-five years later, researchers are applying that same basic idea in a variety of new directions.

These special issues included papers on career crafting, which extends the concept beyond one's current job to the broader arc of a person's career (Li et al., 2026); team job crafting, which examines how groups of employees collectively reshape their work (Wang et al., 2026); and time-spatial job crafting, which explores how employees intentionally structure where and when they work in hybrid environments (Demerouti, 2026). The introductory article also positioned playful work design in the same context as job crafting, another bottom-up approach to proactively shaping work (Bakker & Slemp, 2026).

None of those ideas felt like a stretch to me. In fact, they all seem to build naturally on the original insight that employees aren't simply passive recipients of the jobs organizations create. Instead, they can actively shape their own work experiences.

What I found most interesting wasn't any single extension. It was realizing how pliable the original idea has proven to be. Twenty-five years later, researchers are still finding new ways to apply the basic insight that employees don't simply have to adapt to work—they can actively shape it. More importantly, that insight is no longer confined to an individual employee crafting an individual job.

The third thing that stood out to me was how much more nuanced the conversation has become. Early job crafting research often focused on demonstrating that employees who crafted their jobs tended to experience better outcomes. The anniversary papers paint a more complicated picture. They increasingly ask when job crafting helps, when it doesn't, and what factors influence whether it succeeds.

For example, Rudolph and colleagues argued that more job crafting isn't necessarily better. Like many workplace behaviors, there may be a point where additional crafting provides diminishing returns or even becomes counterproductive. Additionally, Parker and colleagues suggested that the relationship between job crafting and positive work outcomes may not simply run in one direction. Rather than job crafting causing engagement or well-being, employees who are already engaged or thriving may also be more likely to craft their jobs, creating an ongoing cycle of mutual influence.

Other papers highlight the importance of context. Schepp and Boehm explored how job crafting changes across the lifespan, suggesting that what works for a younger employee may not be the same as what works later in a career. Weinhardt and colleagues examined how ADHD shapes job crafting, reminding us that individual differences matter as well.

To me, this is perhaps the most important evolution. Rather than treating job crafting as a universally beneficial phenomenon, these papers recognize that its effects depend on the circumstances. That's ultimately a much more useful way to think about job crafting because it shifts the focus from encouraging more crafting to understanding what kind of crafting is likely to help, for whom, and under what conditions.

Reading through these special issues turned out to be far more interesting than I expected. Many of the papers were less about introducing new ideas than about taking stock of how job crafting has evolved over the past 25 years. That evolution resulted in researchers asking better questions, applying the concept in new contexts, and developing a more nuanced understanding of when job crafting actually helps.

If there's a practical lesson from all of this, it's that work is probably more flexible than we often assume—but not infinitely so. Most of us have at least some ability to shape how we experience our jobs. The trick, though, isn't simply to craft more. It's to understand which changes are actually likely to make work better for you in your particular role and circumstances.
