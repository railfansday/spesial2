How Audi’s Digital Matrix LED Lights Bring Adaptive Driving Beams to the U.S.

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

NHTSA’s regulations make it exceedingly hard to offer Europe’s best lighting tech in the States, so Audi designed something new.

Adaptive driving beams, or ADB, are finally tunneling away into the dark and lighting roads in the United States. Europe and other markets have enjoyed this headlight tech for well over a decade, but U.S. regulations have kept these superior lights off our streets until the last few years. Even when we thought there was some daylight (no pun intended) in 2022, when U.S. regulators cleared a pathway for adaptive driving beams, cars with these headlights didn’t flood the market. In fact, it took years for the first applications to even come out, with Rivian and Tesla launching first.

There are some real reasons—which I’ll get into later—that adaptive driving beams aren’t on every car already after the 2022 regulation change. It would’ve been peachy if the U.S. regulations in National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) rule FMVSS 108 simply harmonized our headlight rules with Europe, but it didn’t; unfortunately, all the adaptive driving beam headlights used elsewhere don’t comply with our rules, forcing automakers to engineer both separate hardware and software to bring adaptive driving beams to U.S. roads.

Leave it to tech leaders like Audi and Mercedes-Benz to be among the first to breach the American market: the new Mercedes-Benz S-Class is one of the first of this new wave, as is the Audi Q9, the brand’s new flagship SUV. The Q9 is the first vehicle with Audi’s new Digital Matrix LED headlight technology, and it’s completely compatible with America’s arcane headlight regulations—ABD and all. I just returned from Audi’s launch of the SQ9, which included a night drive to experience the tech.

In case you were wondering why these lights are so great, the Cliffs Notes are relatively straightforward. Instead of a static high-beam, adaptive driving beams continuously shape the pattern of light they emit on the road to maintain a long high-beam-like projection of light; to prevent blasting oncoming drivers in the eyes, it creates “tunnels” of darkness as depicted above that track other cars via cameras behind the windshield. It’ll even dim the brightness of the headlights on road signs to ensure the reflection doesn’t bother a driver. (However, you can still put it in dummy high-beam mode to force full brights; plus on the system only works above 25 miles per hour or so, and turns off if it senses you’re in an urban area with street lights or other sources of light.)

To get a handle on what’s so special about Audi’s new Digital Matrix LED adaptive driving beams, I spoke with Michael Kruppa, head of front light development at Audi and the engineer leading Audi’s charge to bring adaptive driving beams to the U.S. Interestingly, it wasn’t a guarantee that Audi was going to pursue these headlights. “It really was a question whether we should do it or not, because it's really not straightforward,” Kruppa said. “It's not following exactly as we do it in Europe or the rest of the world. There are special requirements: how to create the tunnel, how to fulfill testing.”

Once Audi decided to go for it, the new regulations made a huge impact on how the brand engineered the headlights for the Q9. The effect was so strong, the Digital Matrix LED hardware used in the U.S. and Europe is identical; in other words, the regs prompted a completely new headlight system compared to the one that powered the adaptive driving beams on other Audis for years. The previous one used mirror-based tech, with the headlight housing 1.3 million micro-mirrors that could change their orientation in an electrostatic field to reflect light in the desired way. This new system, however, creates its light using 25,600 micro-LEDs.

“To be frank with you, we had to change not only the light source to be [LED], but we also had to build the complete headlamp differently,” Kruppa said. They require a totally new housing that allows for two LED modules stacked on top of one another. Audi integrates it into the Q9’s front end in a rather slick fashion, largely hiding the lights in bodywork to highlight the fancy new DRLs at hood height. The Matrix LED system is also lighter from a packaging perspective than the previous system, and supplies more powerful light, making for something of a win-win.

How Audi met the regs with these new lights is equal parts impressive and frustrating, because even though these headlights are truly adaptive driving beams, they’re still not as good as what’s offered in Europe. And, strangely, the performance required of low beam headlight in NHTSA’s regs and Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) testing is the culprit of many annoyances.

“[The problem was] was not only finding a proper light distribution of the ADB active or the ADB tunnels, but also in that situation, we had to change our low beam pattern,” Kruppa said. “And this was really the core topic—why not [all] hardware is feasible in doing that.”

The Audi Digital Matrix LED headlight has both a static low-beam-only matrix LED module and a micro-LED module that’s responsible for part of the low beams as well as the high beams / adaptive driving beams. Where the light is coming from when only the low beams are on remains static, which is part of what Audi needed to fulfill NHTSA requirements—ensuring it doesn’t move and change its pattern. Plus, it needs to perform well on IIHS ratings, where maximum low beam illumination and minimal glare is paramount. Kruppa says the low beam setting of Audi’s previous ADB headlights would instantly fail NHTSA’s test; now, with a dedicated light for the adaptive beams and a low beam synthesized in a pattern (and static without ADB activated) specifically for the U.S. market, Audi can pass all tests with flying colors. It doesn’t interfere with NHTSA, and supplies the visibility that should score well with the IIHS.

“Even if you do not have an ADB system on, if you're just using a regular car out on the street, and you're trying to pass the NHTSA regulation [with Euro-spec headlights], you will not be successful, because the low beam is already giving you a negative result,” Kruppa said. But the low beam is also important, he added, “because if you look at IIHS Safety Pick ratings—the range of the low beam, the brightness of the low beam, it's very important to get an ‘excellent’ or ‘good’ rating. So if you want to fulfill the one thing, you're not allowed to do the other thing.”

This need to be successful in both the NHTSA test for legality and the IIHS low beam test for its safety ratings forced Audi to design the separate low beam and high beam digital system. But then came the challenge of meeting the U.S. regulations for adaptive driving beams, which are, of course, slightly different than Europe’s. The main point of differentiation (and irritation for engineers) is what Kruppa calls the one-degree rule.

“Normally, we are trying to bring the light closer to the car, [but] with the NHTSA regulation, we really had to create quite a huge light tunnel,” Kruppa starts. “If you have the car in front of you, and you have the European ADB system creating the tunnel, it's more or less roughly hitting the outer mirror of the car. So, very close. As [in] comparison, for the U.S. system ... you could place a wild pig next to the car, and the tunnel will include the car plus the wild pig.”

Porcine thought experiments aside, the point is that the tunnels created for oncoming traffic in U.S. vehicles with Digital Matrix LED headlights are considerably larger than the tunnels European motorists will experience with the same systems. Blame America’s conservative rules that try to create a buffer to ensure drivers never blast glare into oncoming traffic. It’s a frustrating, perhaps worthless guideline that only makes headlight performance worse for Americans and a software headache for OEMs. The technology to bring the light all the way up to the limit of glare exists, and by not allowing Audi and others to exploit this tech to its fullest, we’re leaving part of the road dark.

The performance difference is obvious in practice if you’ve driven vehicles with European adaptive driving beams, as it becomes clear that Audi is forced to leave big chunks of the road unlit to comply with NHTSA’s rules. Yes, this solution is better than not having adaptive driving beams at all, but the compromise feels like yet another chapter in NHTSA’s long-running reluctance to allow modern lighting technology onto America’s roads.

“It’s frustrating because what you experience when driving is, you always see some more shadowy areas next to the car,” Kruppa said. “From the point of view to bring as much light as possible on the road to give you additional information, we would like to get closer to the [other] car, without creating glare.”

Despite this compromise, Audi is still convinced it has the best adaptive driving beams on the market. Kruppa talked a little smack against the solutions offered by other brands who simply tweaked their software to comply with regulations.

With the dual light generating setup of the Q9, “you have a different source of light distribution on the road” depending on mode, Kruppa said. “And this was helping us to solve a puzzle. [Another] solution would be, you could apply an ADB system and cut away a lot of light. Some of our competitors are doing that. But then in the end, the question you're asking yourself is, is the high beam really on if you're losing more than fifty percent of the light on the road?”

I can’t speak to how Rivian’s and Tesla’s adaptive driving beams work compared to the Q9, as I haven’t experienced them, but Audi is so passionate about its lights that Kruppa even stressed a need to offer the classic “Audi DNA” with its headlight performance. What he means by that is a no-compromise and distinctively tech-forward approach that pushes the industry forward, which these Digital Matrix LEDs absolutely nail. This is the company that has long stood by the slogan “Vorsprung durch Technik,” after all.

Funnily enough, much of this effort could ultimately be a short-lived triumph if a recent announcement from the NHTSA results in progress being made. There’s no guarantee yet, but U.S. regulators are talking harmonization with international standards, which would be a godsend for automakers everywhere.

“I'm super-happy that they are now opening up the discussion,” Kruppa says. “This is like Christmas and birthday in the same day.”

If the U.S. does harmonize its rules with Europe’s, it would allow many vehicles on the road already to be updated to run adaptive driving beam software. (The hardware has been installed on cars for years now, but no automaker is legally allowed to turn it on.) This would allow even more features to be unlocked from the new Digital Matrix LED headlights, too. Kruppa specifically calls out the problem of keeping the low beam light static to meet U.S. regs; this means Audi can’t tweak the digital pattern to help in fog, rain, and snow, all conditions Kruppa says the headlights perform better under on European roads.

In the end, having these headlights here in the U.S. feels like taking five leaps forward, but then a couple of steps back. NHTSA finally taking headlights seriously could be the step needed to get us to the lights we deserve, so fingers crossed regulators finally see the light. Pun intended.
