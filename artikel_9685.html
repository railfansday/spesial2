How a Manual QA Team of Three Automated 200 Test Cases Without Writing Code

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

"We can't hire an automation engineer. The budget is gone, and the good ones take five months to find." That was the CTO's answer. The QA team had a different idea.

Let's start with a number that every small QA team recognizes:

Neha, Tom, and Arun were the entire QA department at a logistics software company. They knew the product better than anyone: every edge case, every customer complaint, every "oh, that thing breaks when the warehouse has more than 500 SKUs." What they didn't have was automation code, and nobody had the time to learn a framework while also running 205 manual tests every two weeks.

This post walks through how they closed that gap: the plan, the mistakes, the scenario format, and the numbers. If you lead a small QA team, or are a small QA team, you'll leave with a concrete blueprint. Estimated reading time: 6 minutes.

The usual advice, "just learn Selenium," ignores the real constraint. Learning a framework takes months. Maintaining a scripted suite takes ongoing engineering effort. For a three-person team already saturated with release work, that advice is a polite way of saying "do nothing."

Instead of asking "How do we learn to code?" Neha reframed the problem:

What is the knowledge that makes our tests valuable, and does it actually live in code?

A small rule saved them from a classic trap: every converted test had to be run side by side with its manual twin once before the manual version was retired. If the automated test passed while a human saw a defect, they treated that as a gap in the scenario's assertions, not a success.

Something unexpected happened with team morale. The testers spent less time on repetitive regression and more time on exploratory testing, which is where the weird, valuable, human-found bugs live. Arun described it as "finally getting to test like a detective instead of a checklist."

If you're a manual tester, this isn't a threat to your role. It's the opposite. The scarce skill was never typing locators. It was knowing what to test and what "correct" looks like, and that expertise is now directly convertible into automation.

Open your own test case list (wiki, spreadsheet, or test management tool) and run a quick version of the triage script above. Find your top ten by manual hours times risk. If you can write those ten as plain-English scenarios today, you're closer to automation than you think.
