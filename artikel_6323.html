Octopus suckers hold a surprising clue to how evolution builds new body parts

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Cuttlefish and octopus arms use the leg-building genes of insects, but their suckers run on a gene from the embryo's first days.

Insects and mice lay out their legs with the same small set of genes.

The genetic instructions used to grow an octopus arm are different from the ones used to grow the little suction cups on that arm.

None of the limb-patterning genes the team examined were active in forming suckers.

Instead, the researchers identified an active gene also involved in shaping the early embryo.

A team at the University of Tokyo mapped where two dozen genes are active in the developing arms of cuttlefish and octopuses, and in the foot of a pond snail.

Ryosuke Kimbara led the work at the Misaki Marine Biological Station, part of the university’s School of Science, with Hisanori Kohtsuka, Kohei Oguchi, Daisuke Kurokawa and Toru Miura.

He is now at the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology.

Kimbara noted that the search didn’t go where the team expected it to.

“At first, we hypothesized that appendage patterning genes would be involved both in arm and sucker patterning. But we ran into a challenge in identifying the genes expressed in suckers,” Kimbara said.

“Therefore, we changed the method into a more comprehensive one and continued screening until we found a gene with a distinctive expression pattern in developing suckers, and it wasn’t where we expected to find it.”

The main animal was the golden cuttlefish. Adults were netted off the Okusu fishing port in Yokosuka, on Sagami Bay, each spring from 2020 to 2023.

Their fertilized eggs went into seawater tanks held at 66°F to 70°F (19°C to 21°C). Hatchlings were fed small shrimp and crabs caught on the shore outside the station.

A cephalopod‘s arms are thought to come from the flat, muscular foot that its snail-like ancestors crawled on.

So the team also raised embryos of the gold-spot octopus, from adults collected in the Seto Inland Sea and Tokyo Bay.

To compare the arms with a molluscan foot, they also raised great pond snails from a laboratory strain.

To see where a gene is working, the researchers stained embryos with a probe that colors only the cells where that gene is switched on.

They focused on the second pair of arms, at the stages when new suckers were budding along them.

The first genes they checked have names that give away their history.

Distal-less, Dachshund, Homothorax and Extradenticle were discovered in fruit flies, where they help mark off a leg from base to tip. Their counterparts do similar work in a mouse limb.

In the cuttlefish arm, each one colored its own zone. Distal-less lit up the middle and tip of the arm. Homothorax and Extradenticle stayed near the base, where no suckers grow.

A fifth gene marked the back of the arm, opposite the sucker side. The same zones showed up in the octopus arm.

In the snail’s foot, the same genes were on, but spread across the whole foot with no zones at all.

The patterned version, the team argues, is what turned an undivided foot into an arm with a base, a tip and a sucker side.

The team found no activity from those limb-patterning genes in the sucker buds.

A second family of signals, known as BMP, draws the line between the sucker side of the arm and the back.

A gene called Bmp2/4 was active on the back, while Chordin, which blocks Bmp2/4, was strongest on the sucker side and in the newest buds at the arm tip.

In the snail’s foot, those signals were absent, though they were active elsewhere in the larva.

So the front-to-back layout of a cephalopod arm looks like something cephalopods invented.

Chordin has a second known job, in starting nerve tissue, and the staining fit that.

Nerves branching off the arm’s central cord had already grown into a sucker bud before the bud began to dimple inward into a cup.

With none of the leg genes in the suckers, the team changed tactics. They cut the second arms of young cuttlefish into tip, middle and base, and separately into sucker side and back.

Then they sequenced every gene active in each piece, with three animals per piece.

Thousands of genes differed between the pieces.

Only nine were high in both the tip and the sucker side. Just one of those nine, Ets1, was known to encode a protein that regulates other genes.

Ets1 normally works during gastrulation, the stage in which a hollow ball of embryonic cells folds inward to form the body’s layers.

In mollusks and other animals, it is tied to a process in which cells in a sheet loosen their grip on each other and migrate.

In the cuttlefish arm, Ets1 was on across the ridge at the arm tip where new suckers form and in the youngest buds.

As a bud dimpled inward, the gene’s activity narrowed to the dimple. In finished suckers near the base, it was almost gone. The octopus showed the same pattern.

A dye that outlines cell membranes showed the cells doing something that matched the gene. In a young bud, a neat single layer of surface cells covered a loose inner mass.

In a bud forming its cup, the surface cells had stretched into columns. At the deepest point of the dimple, the border between the surface layer and the inner mass had blurred.

Kimbara’s team reads that as cells changing shape and moving, the way they do when an embryo first folds. In the snail, Ets1 was scattered across the foot rather than concentrated anywhere.

Cephalopods have only one copy of Ets1, so the gene was not duplicated and freed up for a new role.

Its sucker job more likely came from changes in when and where it is switched on, or from mutations in the gene itself.

“Our findings suggest that cephalopod arm development involves conserved patterning genes also used in appendage development in other animals like insects and mammals,” Kimbara said.

“But suckers on their arms have a unique developmental mechanism in which genes used in early embryogenesis, Ets1, are reused.”

Everything here is a map of where genes are active, and it isn’t a test of what they do.

Nobody has switched Ets1 off in a cuttlefish embryo to see whether the suckers fail to form, and the team names that as the experiment the finding needs.

Whether sucker cells really repeat the embryo’s folding process isn’t settled either.

A second gene from that process, FoxO, was on in cuttlefish sucker buds but not in the octopus’s, and tracking single cells as a bud folds has not been done. The snail was examined at only one larval stage.

Cuttlefish and octopus suckers also end up built differently and grip differently, Kimbara said.

So the molecular machinery may have evolved separately in each lineage, even though the early steps look alike.

Next, the researchers want to study the nautilus, a member of an ancient cephalopod lineage that grips with sticky, tongue-shaped pads on its tentacles instead of suckers.

Whether Ets1 is active in those pads is the first thing the team wants to check.

The full study was published in the journal iScience.
