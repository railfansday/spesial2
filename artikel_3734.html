Karen Bass on Nithya Raman, Her Radical Past, and the Fight to Remain LA Mayor

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Karen Bass was showing me her three iPhones when I visited her at Getty House, the official residence of the Los Angeles mayor, late last month. One was her personal phone, another was for government business, and the third had been hacked so many times that she wasn’t supposed to use it anymore. But Bass couldn’t bring herself to ditch the number, which she’s had for 20 years, because friends and colleagues still call it. So she kept the compromised phone around, monitoring its incessant ringing during our interview.

Bass, 73, has the warmth of a grandmother who wants to make sure you’re hydrated, and, as I discovered, the authority of a disappointed matriarch who expects you to know better. The intimidating part is that she can vacillate between those two sides of herself rather quickly.

With a disarming smile, she offered me a glass of water and invited me to meet her daughter and grandson, who live with her at Getty House.

Bass photographed at the Getty House in Los Angeles, September 2026.

But when I suggested that Bass, a lifelong progressive who has traveled to Fidel Castro’s Cuba to study alternatives to capitalism, was borrowing from the conservative playbook to portray her opponent Nithya Raman as a dangerous extremist, a different side of Bass came out. Suddenly I was sitting across from a stern political veteran who had little patience for people challenging her judgment.

“That’s not true,” she shot back defensively.

I mentioned a campaign email that I thought implied Raman might be antisemetic. Bass and an aide disputed my characterization, insisting that the campaign has simply criticized Raman for skipping a Jewish heritage event at City Hall.

Did she believe Raman was antisemetic? “I don’t know what she is,” Bass replied. “It depends on the time of day and day of the week.”

At one point when I asked whether she would warn her successor about the city’s dysfunctional homelessness bureaucracy, Bass took another swipe at Raman.

“Well, if she’s my successor, no, she knows it all,” Bass said with a sarcastic undertone.

Just four years ago Bass was embraced by the local progressive movement in LA. But now she’s getting flak from both sides of the political spectrum and finds herself courting conservative voters to defeat Raman, who is running to the left of Bass.

For over a year, wealthy donors have been trying to find her replacement. Rick Caruso, the billionaire developer who spent more than $100 million trying to defeat Bass in 2022, says he was encouraged by Ari Emanuel and former Governor Jerry Brown to consider another run for elected office. Spencer Pratt, the reality television villain whose house burned down in the Palisades fire, transformed his personal tragedy into an insurgent mayoral campaign, attracting wealthy Hollywood supporters and massive amounts of attention on social media that was driven by his atypical political ads and AI-generated promotional videos made by his supporters.

But neither man became Bass’s 2026 general-election opponent. Instead, the challenge came from the left, in the form of Raman, a Harvard-educated democratic socialist who even endorsed the mayor’s reelection before entering the race. Now Bass finds herself vying for Pratt supporters on the right. She’s made public safety—an issue Pratt harped on—central to her reelection campaign, and has tried to paint Raman as soft on crime. Raman is also making a play for Pratt supporters, even invoking Ronald Reagan at the beginning of the only televised debate between the two candidates since June’s primary.

Bass is even showing up with Caruso on the campaign trail now. The two appeared together this month at the developer’s luxury shopping mall in the Pacific Palisades in what amounted to an unlikely reunion given Caruso’s unflattering assessment of her leadership. “People feel let down by the city,” he told me in August. Caruso also argued that LA mayors have more power than they care to admit. “They do have enough power and they don’t use it right,” he said. But perhaps Caruso sees Bass as a more palatable option than Raman, who carries the weight of a DSA association. Caruso called the socialist organization’s platform “dangerous” and said he would be voting for Bass in the upcoming election.

Karen Bass and Rick Caruso talk while on a tour of his shopping center, Palisades Village, in the Pacific Palisades, October 20, 2026.

A September UC Berkeley Institute of Governmental Studies poll conducted for the Los Angeles Times found Raman leading Bass 39% to 28% among likely voters, with another 33% undecided. Nearly two thirds of respondents viewed Bass unfavorably. The numbers were hardly conclusive (no polls are these days), but they laid bare the fact that a politician who entered City Hall with considerable goodwill is now facing a difficult fight for another term. Bass could very well become the first Los Angeles mayor in 21 years not to be given a second term. If she wins, it’ll likely be because a lot of Pratt primary voters decided she was the lesser of two evils.

It’s a bit of an ironic situation that Bass finds herself in. Before she was mayor, before she was a congresswoman, before she became Speaker of the California state assembly, she was a teenage radical who considered running for public office an act of ideological surrender.

“Running for office was selling out,” she recalled when we spoke at a Croatian community center in San Pedro.

Has she changed? Or, like Reagan before her, has the increasingly left-leaning Democratic Party left her behind?

Bass was 14 when she signed her mother up to serve as a precinct captain for Robert F. Kennedy’s presidential campaign, she tells me. Her mother didn’t actually do the work. Bass did, riding her bicycle to campaign headquarters and immersing herself in the senator’s bid for the White House.

She was supposed to attend Kennedy’s primary-victory celebration at the Ambassador Hotel in June 1968, but her parents wouldn’t let her go because it was a school night.

“So I listened to the assassination on the radio,” she says.

The murder was devastating to Bass and her parents. Just two months earlier, Martin Luther King Jr. had been assassinated and, to Bass’s parents, it seemed like anyone involved in progressive politics would suffer the same fate.

“I was like, ‘It’s over.’ I was done,” she says. “I became much more interested in antiestablishment work.”

Her parents were reluctant to let her dive into politics. But the young Bass had already been politicized. Growing up in Los Angeles, she encountered the contradictions of establishment liberal politics. She attended Hamilton High School, which she says was predominantly Jewish, with roughly 300 Black students among its 3,000 pupils, and witnessed white flight as Black families moved into previously segregated neighborhoods.

Bass surrounded by supporters during a press conference to kick off the next phase of her reelection campaign in Los Angeles, June 9, 2026.

“It was painful,” she said. “Very confusing for a child because, you know, I’m talking Martin Luther King and all this stuff, and they’re like, ‘Oh no, not here.’”

She went on to become deeply involved in the anti-apartheid movement and learned how to organize from older activists. But by the late 1980s, she was growing increasingly frustrated with the distance between the political debates consuming her fellow activists and the violence engulfing South Central Los Angeles. The crack epidemic devastated neighborhoods, gang violence was killing young people, and elected officials only seemed to be interested in filling prisons. On the other end of the spectrum, she felt that progressive activists were more interested in relitigating the Iran-Contra scandal than they were in providing concrete relief to those suffering.

“I felt like what [progressive activists] were doing politically was theoretical and aspirational, but people were dying,” Bass says.

She quit her secure job teaching nursing at USC and founded the Community Coalition, an organization devoted to confronting addiction, violence, and poverty.

Her career change pissed off a lot of her compatriots. Bass was entering the nonprofit world, where she would have to negotiate with government agencies and accept money from the very institutions she had spent years protesting.

“I did the ultimate sellout move,” she jokes. “I took federal funding to start the organization.”

“I wanted to make a difference in people’s lives,” she says, “and my trajectory since then has been pragmatic, which has gotten me on the outs of some folks on the left. But that’s okay. As long as I’m delivering for poor people, I’m okay.”

Homelessness was Bass’s first priority when she was elected mayor. She left a secure seat in Congress to run for mayor in 2022, promising to confront the crisis with the urgency of a community organizer. It wasn’t hard to sell that vision to voters. She had experience tackling the crisis from her time at the Community Coalition.

Her signature program, Inside Safe, was designed to move people out of encampments and into temporary housing, where they could receive services and eventually secure permanent homes. For Bass, the program is evidence that her administration has made progress on an issue that befuddled her predecessors.

But the numbers tell a more complicated story. Street homelessness has declined over the course of her administration, but the city’s unsheltered population increased nearly 8% in the most recent annual count. In Raman’s council district, meanwhile, street homelessness fell roughly 49% during the same year.

Mayor Karen Bass during a debate with City Councilmember Nithya Raman with moderators from NBC4 and Telemundo 52, October 13, 2026.

Bass claims that the deeper problem is a fragmented homelessness bureaucracy. Under the current system, the city, county, and Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority share responsibilities, making it difficult to track spending and hold anyone accountable.

She wants to give the city greater authority over its own homelessness programs. But one might ask why she wasn’t able to give the city more power to fight the problem during her first term? She says her time was monopolized by moving people off the streets, and that she couldn’t simultaneously overhaul the bureaucratic inefficiencies at the homelessness agencies.

“I had to build the plane and fly it,” she tells me.

When I asked her to grade her performance on homelessness, she gave herself a B-plus. It was a confident assessment for a mayor whose signature issue became one of her most glaring political vulnerabilities during the primary. Pratt flooded the internet with videos and pictures portraying a homelessness crisis that was out of control. Whether it’s true or not that the problem got worse under Bass’s leadership was irrelevant. Pratt convinced large swaths of the city that it had.

Nothing has damaged Bass’s political standing more than the January 2025 wildfires.

When the Palisades fire erupted, the mayor was in Ghana as part of a presidential delegation. By the time she returned, the disaster had become a defining crisis of her administration.

The fire killed 12 people and destroyed thousands of homes. It also transformed the city’s politics, elevating figures such as Pratt, who lost his home and made the government’s response central to his mayoral campaign.

Bass has acknowledged that leaving Los Angeles was a mistake.

“My biggest regret was not being here,” she told me.

Bass maintains that the emergency notifications she would ordinarily have expected from public safety officials did not reach her before she left for Africa. But in the week leading up to the fires, weather forecasters were warning that the city could experience an extreme fire event. Companies were telling their employees to be safe. And news organizations were already planning how to cover the potential fires.

Bass knows that the mayor bears ultimate political responsibility, but she resists the idea that every institutional failure can be reduced to her negligence.

“Any time I made any attempts to explain, it was viewed as I was giving excuses and pointing fingers,” she says.

And yet Bass says she understands the anger of fire survivors.

“I understood and understand people’s anger because when you grieve, it is helpful if there’s somewhere that you could say, ‘This wouldn’t have happened except for that.’”

Bass is deeply familiar with the anger that comes with grief.

In 2006 her daughter Emilia and son-in-law Michael Wright were killed in a car accident. Bass told me about the painful impulse she felt to imagine how things might have turned out differently. For some time she wondered if she could have prevented their deaths.

“That was the absolute positively worst thing that could ever happen to me,” she says. “So consequently, everything since then, I feel like the worst has already happened. Nothing else could happen worse to me. Nothing.”

At Getty House, Bass tells me that she’s not going to read the state report on last year’s wildfires until it’s released to the public. She’s been accused of meddling in the LA Fire Department’s after-action report, published in October of last year, and she explained that she doesn’t want to be accused again of influencing or watering down the findings. For what it’s worth, she also said she hasn’t read the fire department’s report either.

“I have not seen it, and you know what? I don’t want to see it,” she says of a preview of the state report.

Bass’s relationship with Hollywood has soured a bit since she first took office. Meanwhile, it appears that much of the city’s creative class is siding with Raman, whose husband is an accomplished television writer who worked on 30 Rock and Modern Family.

Bass pushed for a settlement between state attorneys general and Paramount Skydance over an antitrust lawsuit that threatened to derail the company’s acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery after Paramount Skydance chief David Ellison indicated that he would move Paramount’s operations out of Los Angeles. She insists she wasn’t asking the AGs to approve the transaction. She says she just wanted to end the uncertainty hanging over the entertainment industry, which was already struggling to prevent runaway productions.

Bass says she spoke with Ellison while the lawsuit was active. She told me she was dissatisfied with the assurances he offered about preserving jobs over the next five years. But she worried that prolonged regulatory uncertainty would only compound the industry’s troubles.

The distinction mattered to Bass: She wasn’t necessarily taking the billionaire’s side, she argued. She was trying to protect the workers whose livelihoods depended on the outcome.

Bass’s budget management has also been a hot topic during campaign season, particularly the raises she negotiated for Los Angeles Police Department officers.

“She gave the police more money than they asked for, and then we had a $1 billion budget deficit [at the end of the 2024-2025 fiscal year],” says comedian and Raman supporter Adam Conover.

When Raman attacked her on this issue during the debate, Bass responded by saying her opponent was being disingenuous. “You know that some of these [fiscal management] problems have taken place over many decades,” Bass snapped back. “Frankly, it’s dishonest.”

Bass, then a Congresswoman, at a rally with then-mayor Antonio Villaraigosa in Los Angeles, California, March 23, 2011.

Then there is the downtown convention center.

The city is undertaking an approximately $2.6 billion expansion project intended to attract conferences and revive economic activity downtown.

Jason Oppenheim, the luxury real estate broker and Selling Sunset personality, sees the convention center project as an effort by the Bass administration to satisfy the unions—a very influential constituency in this city.

Oppenheim is hardly a natural ally of the DSA, but he has found himself increasingly intrigued by Raman.

“She strikes me as pragmatic and willing to go against her political base when necessary,” he tells me. “But I think a lot of people are more scared of the DSA than they are frustrated with Karen Bass’s tenure,” he says.

Oppenheim was getting a manicure when we spoke. He had not settled on a candidate yet, but he seemed at peace with the possibility of taking a political gamble.

“I don’t take risks on my nails,” he said. “I will on the mayor, but not on my manicures.”

The mayor wakes up between four and five in the morning and reads the Los Angeles Times, The New York Times, and international news outlets. The teenage activist who followed liberation movements around the world still has a fascination with foreign policy. She tells me she’s not too big a fan of her local paper these days because of how much ink it’s spilled criticizing her administration.

Bass spoke with unhoused people at an encampment during an Inside Safe operation in Los Angeles, September 26, 2024.

The mayor regularly attends Black churches, especially during campaign season. At a Pratt campaign event in South Central LA held in May, two women sitting in lawn chairs told me they were likely voting for Bass because she had recently attended their church for a Sunday service. Two more women at a community trash cleanup in the same neighborhood this October told me they were also voting for Bass because of her relationship to the neighborhood and its faith institutions.

When she was 14, Bass believed the system had failed. At 73, she believes the system can work if someone with sufficient experience and determination is running it.

Bass is not particularly impressed by the younger generation of progressive activists in Los Angeles.

“I don’t feel like there’s a real appreciation or thirst for history of past movements to learn past lessons,” she said.

She sees the Democratic Socialists of America as having the arguments that consumed earlier generations, particularly over whether ideological compromise is a betrayal or a necessary condition of achieving anything.

“At this point I stand for getting stuff done,” she told me. “I’ve always been a progressive, always supported social justice. That is a through line in my life from childhood to now.”

Bass also has a theory about what happens to young radicals as they get older. They have children and parenthood tempers their ideological purity with the practical demands of raising a family. The activists who once demanded radical change begin worrying about their children’s safety, their schools, and whether the neighborhood is a decent place to grow up. Their priorities change.

Of course Bass doesn’t see her own evolution as a retreat from her youthful convictions. She says the goals remain the same. But these days she’s working from within the system to achieve them.

The hard part for Bass will be convincing the voters who believe the system has failed them to choose her. And it leaves Bass in an uncomfortable position for a former radical: she’s defending the virtues of experience against a younger, more ideological candidate who believes Los Angeles needs something different.

Bass knows she might not get another four years to implement her policies. Even her staffers seem genuinely unsure of how election day will play out.

“Losing an election would be devastating,” she told me.

It felt like a big admission from a politician who usually projects confidence. However, Bass has endured worse than political defeat.

“There is no comparison of a parent losing a child to losing an election.”
