Here's an exercise to illustrate what I was trying to say above: Plug a withdrawal strategy into a site like https://ficalc.app or a Monte Carlo analysis.

Have a closer look at the scenarios that run out of money before death and test if taking SS at 62 or another age reduces the failures.

For example, I plugged in a $1M portfolio, 80% stocks, 15% bonds, 5% cash, $64k expenses, SS of $24k annually, 40 year retirement.

Starting SS at age 62 ($24,000) had a 94.2% success rate (145/154)
Starting SS at age 70 ($42,514) had a 98.7% success rate (152/154)**
Here's another example, this time using an amortization-based withdrawal calculator (TPAWPlanner set to the tpaw strategy).

Assumptions: female age 62, retired, 1MM portfolio; social security PIA of $2000 (age 67); living to age 85 (average life expectancy in the US for this person); default settings in TPAWPlanner for stock and bond returns and the inflation rate

Claiming SS at age 62 (monthly benefit of $1400): https://tpawplanner.com/link?params=4QV ... rjxve8t196

Claiming SS at age 70 (monthly benefit of $2480): https://tpawplanner.com/link?params=Nfr ... 1h09xrzv9a

In comparing these two plans, the outcome our hypothetical female used to set the "risk" settings was to maintain (throughout retirement) a constant 5th percentile lower bound on the Monthly Spending During Retirement graph.

Compared to claiming SS at age 70, claiming SS at age 62 resulted in about $400 less spending/month during retirement at the lower bound (5th percentile in the Monte Carlo analysis). This strategy also results in lower monthly spending at the time of retirement (age 62).

With typical market returns (50th percentile in the Monte Carlo) or extremely favorable market returns (95th percentile in the Monte Carlo), claiming SS at age 62 results in more monthly spending as the person ages.

So, this person has a choice to make...
claim at age 62 for potentially higher spending later in life if typical or favorable future market returns show up...
or
claim at age 70 to increase spending at the time of retirement and increase the lower bound of future spending should poor future market returns show up

Tweak the settings and assumptions in the planner. It's fascinating (at least to this Boglehead) to look at the interplay of these on expected retirement spending or legacy amounts.
