Probing the Depths of a Drive-by

Posted November 2, 2026 | Reviewed by Ekua Hagan

"Imagination is more important than knowledge"—Albert Einstein

Imagination has an assortment of meanings and often works in different and perplexing ways.

Merriam-Webster defines imagination as “the act or power of forming a mental image of something not present to the senses or never before wholly perceived in reality."

So imagine the term “drive-by.” It has an assortment of definitions. One of the more established is an assault where a perpetrator fires a weapon from a moving vehicle, then flees the scene.

“Drive-by” is also the name of a celebrated rock band—the Drive-By Truckers.

But the most relevant connotation to me, sadly, is when one pretends to care about a person in need or a highly emotive issue, then quickly moves on for various reasons. Among them: Perhaps they simply don’t understand or are overwhelmed by the circumstances; they can’t embrace emotional trauma or disease; or they’re shallow individuals caught up with self-aggrandizement; or they flat-out don’t really care about the health or well-being of others.

Words like callous and self-centered come to mind.

So, they often punt, in words that have no inner meaning.

I find this true in my journey with Alzheimer’s and an advancing prostate and chest cancer diagnosis—diseases embedded in my family. I’m one of millions.

The gut response often tends to be: “But you look so good!”

The truth here is that Alzheimer’s and cancer don’t affect, at the start, how one looks. It’s the inner core of the diseases that torments. And so, in their great uneasiness about discussing the specifics, many punt.

I’ve pondered this over time, working hard not to feel irritated about it. Seems to me, there’s a proclivity these days for many to be absorbed with self. We see it in advertising; we see it in movies; we see it in social conversations about the size of one’s home, investments, money in the bank, or worldly accomplishments and possessions.

With such individuals, a common response to those in need is to espouse in a drive-by at best: “I’m here for you!”

Well, what the heck does that mean? Unless one embraces the need or at least wants to be educated about it, the response comes across as shallow as low tide on Cape Cod Bay, which runs out to the horizon for miles,

Writes Archie James: “The people who don’t really care about you are so difficult to identify. They are present. They are warm enough. They are soft enough on the surface. They say the right words. They are not cold. They say anything like: ‘That’s amazing' and 'I’m so proud of you!’

“On the surface,” adds James, “if you would describe this conversation to someone else, it might sound like support. It would pass the test. But something doesn’t land. You hang up the phone—or leave the room—and you feel vaguely emptier than you did before.”

Many say the perception today that people in general have become more superficial is driven perhaps by advancing technologies, shifts in culture, addiction to social media, or simply checking out from the whirlwind of modern life. We collectively absorb a 24/7 news cycle of wars, world crises, staggering suffering, and crushing violence. And so many of us suffer at times from compassion fatigue, and race to hit the pause button—a numbing defense mechanism worldwide.

Pump the brakes on this. I now realize that I’m in the driver’s seat at times as well—neglecting the needs of others. Research is enlightening.

Commenting on a worldwide scale, Paul Slovic, an academic expert in human judgment, says that many people are generally caring “and will exert great effort to rescue individual victims whose needy plight comes to their attention. These same good people, however, often become numbly indifferent to the plight of individuals who are one of many in a much greater problem.”

Says the Arithmetic of Compassion: “Psychic numbing is a psychological phenomenon that causes us to feel indifferent to the suffering of large numbers of people. The quote attributed to Joseph Stalin—‘One death is a tragedy; a million deaths is a statistic’—is an illustration of psychic numbing.”

Notes Bessel van der Kolk, a Dutch American psychiatrist and researcher, in his book, The Body Keeps the Score: “Traumatized people (worldwide) chronically feel unsafe inside their bodies. The past is alive in the form of gnawing interior discomfort. Their bodies are constantly bombarded by visceral warning signs, and, in an attempt to control these processes, they often become expert at ignoring their gut feelings and at numbing awareness of what is played out inside. They learn to hide from themselves.”

Author Jessica Sorrensen had it right when she reflected, “I just let the pain take over, allowing it to numb the pain of being left behind.”

And observed Bethany Griffin, author of Masque of the Red Death, “I’ve perfected the art of the fake smile. It’s not so difficult when you are completely numb.”

The fake smile… mea culpa... We’ve all used it.

And so, many caring individuals have become somewhat detached these days, and in many ways for understandable reasons.

So, how do we know what others really feel deep inside? That’s often a crapshoot in these days of political correctness, keeping to party lines, not baring the soul in reflective ways.

Perhaps with oxygen tanks. Perhaps not, for now.

Thus, one needs to use imagination to climb out of a box that people place you in when they say they want to help; they want to sympathize, but they don’t.

Fully noted: As an Irish Catholic, I’m thinking now that I need to be back in the confessional on this.

One often needs imagination, I’ve learned the hard way, to walk in knowledge—for knowledge is limited and imagination is vast, as Einstein notes.

Even more so on the lip of holiday times.
