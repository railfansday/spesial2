Inflation in 2026: core PCE never fell below 3% from January to October

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

CNBC's monthly record shows core PCE inflation at 3.0% or higher from January through October 2026, with headline at 3.8% in October.

The inflation problem that defined 2026 was already in place when the year began, before any spring jump in food or energy prices. In January, core prices, which leave out food and energy, were up 3.1% from a year earlier, according to CNBC. The BEA reports that the full basket of consumer prices, food and fuel included, was up a smaller 2.8%. The pressure came from the everyday goods and services that make up most of a household budget rather than from the grocery aisle or the gas pump. Cheaper gas or groceries could not bring the rate down, because they were not what was pushing it up. Whatever happened to food and energy later in the year happened on top of a base that was already too high.

Households had more real income in January, but they bought only a little more with it. The BEA reports that real disposable income, meaning income after taxes and inflation, rose 0.7% in the month, while consumer spending grew just 0.1% once price increases are taken out. For that one month, income outpaced prices, and households had more real money to spend even as their carts held little more than before. Higher income helped families keep up, but it did nothing to lower the prices underneath it. The core rate that set the year's starting point remained in place.

February's inflation came from the everyday core of household budgets, not from the gas pump or the grocery store. The full price index climbed 0.4% over January, according to Bureau of Economic Analysis data reported by CNBC, and the BEA reports the same monthly gain for the core measure, which sets food and energy aside. Leaving out the most volatile items removes none of the month's increase. Food and fuel prices tend to jump and then settle back, but pressure that runs through the rest of the household budget does not fade on its own.

Households paid for that broad rise by spending more money on barely more goods. Consumer spending rose 0.5% in dollars in February, according to the BEA, but it grew just 0.1% once higher prices are taken out. Almost all of the extra money went to cover price increases, and people came home with close to what they had bought in January. Their buying power shrank at the same time. Disposable income after taxes and inflation fell 0.5% for the month, so households kept up their spending on a smaller real income. Steady spending on falling real income leaves a gap that families have to cover somewhere.

The spring's jumps in food and fuel prices had become, by April, the level households were living with rather than one-off shocks. Core inflation, which leaves out food and energy, ran 3.3% above a year earlier, according to CNBC. BEA reports the same rate in its first estimate of the month, so there is little doubt about where the underlying base stood. In that same estimate, the full price index was up 3.8%. Core leaves out only food and energy, so the gap means households paid extra for groceries and fuel on top of a base that would not come down. They faced pressure from two directions at once: a stubborn base, and a costlier layer of essentials above it.

Prices that high left households with less buying power, and the loss held up when the figures were revisited. Real disposable income, meaning take-home pay after taxes and inflation, fell 0.5% in April, BEA reports, and BEA shows the same drop in its May release. At first glance, that looks like a budget under strain from both sides, with prices rising while income went nowhere. Total personal income did not grow at all in April, but the weakness came from falling income for farm owners rather than from paychecks across the workforce. The income dip is more likely a one-off than a sign that jobs or wages were weakening. The lasting problem was prices.

June brought a monthly drop in prices but little relief for households. Prices slipped 0.1% from May, according to the first estimate from the BEA. Over 12 months, though, prices were still up 3.5%, according to CNBC. One cheaper month did little to undo a year of increases. For a family, June's bills stayed well above those of the previous summer, even though they stopped rising for a moment.

The first count made June look hotter than the later figures do. In its first estimate of the month, released July 30, 2026, the BEA puts the annual rate at 3.7%, a figure it later replaced. Neither reading comes close to the pace policymakers want. The open question is how much prices eased over the summer, not whether inflation was beaten.

The June drop most likely came from food and energy rather than from underlying prices. The BEA reports that in the same first estimate, core prices, which leave out food and energy, still rose 0.1% while the headline index fell. Underlying inflation did not pause in June. Cheaper groceries and fuel offset it for a single month, and the stubborn base underneath kept rising.

By late summer, households got a break from rising underlying prices, but the break only returned them to where the year began. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, were 3.0% higher in August than a year earlier, according to CNBC. That cooled rate held until inflation picked up again in the fall. Core inflation had already sat at that same low in winter, so households ended the summer no better off on underlying prices than they had been in February. A lull that puts prices back at the starting line is a pause, not progress.

The overall cost of living stayed higher than the core reading suggests. With food and fuel included, everything households bought cost 3.4% more than a year earlier, still above the cooled underlying rate. Prices also went on rising during the quiet month, up 0.3% from July, and BEA reports the same monthly increase in its own count. Even in the calmest stretch of 2026, households paid more.

Inflation was still running hot in October, the most recent month with data. Everything households bought cost 3.8% more than a year earlier, according to Bureau of Economic Analysis figures reported by CNBC. That pace is far from what the Federal Reserve aims for. Setting aside food and energy offers little comfort, because core prices were up 3.3% over the same 12 months, and the underlying rate had climbed back up after its cooler stretch in the summer. With the full measure above the core, food and energy added pressure on top of an already high base rather than causing the problem on their own.

October itself brought no pause. Overall prices rose 0.3% within the month, and core prices climbed at the same pace even with food and energy left out. For households, the squeeze did not ease as the year drew toward its close, and the goods and services they buy became more expensive again.

Americans kept spending through 2026, and a year of steady shopping can look like a sign that households were doing well. Their savings show something else. Americans set aside 4.5% of their after-tax income in January, according to the BEA, which was a modest margin even before the year's price increases set in. The BEA reports that the share was down to 2.6% in April, which left families that month with far less money put away for a lost job or an unexpected bill. Stubborn inflation can show up in a household budget this way. People keep buying what they need, and a smaller share of what they earn goes into savings.

The strain on household budgets came from prices that stayed high all year, not from one bad month. Core inflation, which leaves out food and energy, tracks that underlying pressure. Even in February, one of the months when core inflation ran at its lowest of the year, core prices were up 3.0% from a year earlier, according to the BEA. The best months still left everyday goods and services costing noticeably more than they had the year before. Food and energy prices rose sharply in some months and eased in others, and those changes moved the overall inflation rate up and down. The underlying rate moved within a much narrower range and never fell far. Inflation in 2026 did not rise steadily, and it never cooled enough to give households relief. It stayed high.

CNBC tracked how U.S. consumer price inflation changed across each month of 2026. Over those months, underlying inflation cooled during the summer, and prices rose again in the autumn as energy and transport costs climbed. The October figures are the most recent. The Federal Reserve aims for inflation of 2.0%, and underlying inflation did not come close to that goal in any of those months. Read one month at a time, the year looks less like a steady climb than a series of ups and downs that never brought prices near the Fed's target. The question for the months ahead is whether the autumn rise is a new problem or the same one returning.

In March, the part of inflation that leaves out food and energy stayed high and showed no sign of easing. Core prices were 3.2% higher than a year earlier, according to CNBC, and the BEA reports that they rose 0.3% from February alone. March brought a sudden jump in overall prices, but the more lasting problem was that the base underneath that jump kept going up every month. Whatever happened to groceries and fuel that spring, households already faced an underlying rate above 3%.

The jump that made March stand out came from food and energy, not from a broad new wave of price increases. Overall prices were 0.7% higher than in February, according to the BEA, far faster than the core measure. Core leaves out only food and energy, so those two categories account for the difference. That was the spring pattern of a stuck year, with sharp increases in the cost of essentials on top of a base that kept rising at a steadier pace. A narrow increase was still no comfort to a household, because it hit the purchases people can't put off.

When groceries and fuel cost more, the same paycheck buys less right away. Households ended March with less buying power than they had at the start of the month, as real disposable personal income, which is income after taxes and adjusted for prices, fell 0.1%, according to the BEA. That drop is small on its own. But it came while the underlying rate stayed above 3%, and that combination is how a stuck year costs families: not through a single shock, but through a steady loss of buying power that one bad month makes larger.

Households in May faced a broader rise in prices than the underlying rate alone would suggest. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, were 3.4% higher than a year earlier, according to CNBC, while the BEA reports that the full basket of consumer prices was up 4.1%. The underlying rate did not break loose in May. Core leaves out only food and energy, so the higher overall figure means those two categories had become more expensive than the rest of the budget over the previous 12 months. For a family, the higher number is the one that counts, because groceries and gas are purchases no one can skip.

Shoppers spent more freely in May, but much of the extra money only covered higher prices. The BEA reports that consumer spending rose 0.7% in dollars during the month and only 0.3% once price changes are taken out. The busy checkout lines of May reflected inflation as much as demand. Households paid noticeably more and came home with only modestly more.

Incomes kept pace with spending in May, though part of the gain came from federal aid to farmers. Personal income rose 0.7% in dollars, matching the rise in spending, according to the BEA, and farm income in the month was tied to payments under the American Relief Act of 2025. May's income figure combines ordinary earnings with government aid to a single industry. Only the earnings show how most households were doing as prices kept rising.

In May, households paid more for essentials while the core rate kept creeping up.

The relief households got in June depended on the most volatile prices they pay, and those prices can rise again as quickly as they fell.

Whether underlying inflation cooled in July depends on which count of the month a reader trusts. By the later count, core prices, which leave out food and energy, were 3.0% higher than a year earlier, according to CNBC, down from 3.3% in June. In its first estimate for the month, BEA puts the annual core rate at 3.3%, the same as June's. By that first count, underlying inflation held still from June to July rather than easing. One count shows a real summer dip, and the other shows no change at all.

The gap between the two counts does not have to mean that one of them is wrong. The 3.3% reading was the first estimate for July, and BEA reports that an annual update of its figures was still to come after that release. The 3.0% reading comes from a record of the full year compiled months later, so revisions may account for the cooler number. Nothing in the figures shows that they do. That leaves the summer cooling an open question. Anyone who reads 2026 as the year inflation eased in summer is relying on the more favorable of the two counts.

Under either count, July gave households a pause rather than relief. Americans bought almost nothing more than they had in June. Spending adjusted for prices rose by only $1.3 billion in July, according to BEA, which is close to no growth at all in an economy the size of the U.S. Households did not buy more even in a month when the underlying rate, by the later count, had cooled. A cooler number on paper and flat buying in stores add up to a pause, not a recovery.

Whatever July's true reading, the problem had not ended when autumn arrived.

What set August apart was that households bought more with the money they spent. Spending adjusted for prices rose 0.5% in the month, according to BEA, which outpaced the month's rise in prices. Americans came home with more goods and services rather than just bigger bills. That is what a real lull looks like for a family, which gets more for its money instead of only paying more for the same things.

August was the last month of 2026 so far in which households got more for their money.

Prices picked up again in September. The overall price index climbed 0.5% from August, according to Bureau of Economic Analysis data reported by CNBC. Much of the increase came from energy and transport costs, which Americans pay to fuel their cars and get around. Households can't easily cut back on those costs.

The underlying rate of inflation rose more slowly than the headline rate. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, were up 0.3% in September. The core measure leaves out only food and energy, so those two categories account for the gap between the core and overall readings. The underlying rate still moved in the wrong direction, and its annual pace edged up from its late-summer low. Volatile food and energy costs added to a base that was rising again. The autumn increase looks more like the year's old problem returning than a new one starting.

Shoppers paid the full rise in prices at the checkout, not the slower core rate. Once price increases are taken out, consumer spending grew by less than 0.1% in September, according to the BEA. Nearly all of the extra money households spent went to higher prices, and they brought home about the same goods and services as the month before. Their income fell at the same time, with disposable income after taxes and inflation down 0.3% for the month.

Americans kept spending more dollars while their real income shrank, which left them less room to absorb the next price increase.
