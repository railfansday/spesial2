The average American household is sitting on $4,500 or more worth of items they no longer use. Old electronics in drawers, clothes that haven't been worn in two years, furniture from a previous apartment, exercise equipment doubling as coat racks. That's not clutter — it's unrealized capital.

From an FI perspective, selling used items is one of the highest-impact one-time actions you can take. It's tax-free (personal items sold at a loss aren't taxable), requires no ongoing commitment, and the proceeds can go directly into investments that compound for decades. Sell $3,000 in unused items, invest it in index funds at 7% average returns, and it becomes roughly $11,500 in 20 years.

But where you sell matters enormously. Post a $500 designer handbag on Craigslist and you might get $150 from a lowballer. List it on The RealReal with authentication and professional photography, and you could get $300 after fees. Choose the wrong platform for electronics and you'll pay 20% in fees instead of 5%.

This guide compares the 15 most popular platforms for selling used items, breaks down their fees, and tells you exactly which platform works best for each category of item. We've organized everything so you can quickly find the right marketplace, list efficiently, and convert unused stuff into invested capital.

Not all selling platforms are created equal. Some charge listing fees, some take a percentage of sales, some handle shipping, and some are local pickup only. The right choice depends on three factors: what you're selling, how quickly you need to sell, and how much effort you're willing to invest.

Choosing the wrong platform is the most expensive mistake sellers make. A $200 pair of Nike sneakers listed on eBay nets you about $173 after fees. The same sneakers on Poshmark, where shoe buyers actively browse, might sell for $220 before the 20% fee — netting you $176. But on StockX or GOAT (sneaker-specific marketplaces), rare colorways can command $300+.

Here's the definitive guide to matching items to platforms.

For recent-model phones and tablets, Swappa offers the best combination of low fees and buyer trust. Their flat-fee structure ($5-$35 depending on device value) beats eBay's percentage-based fees on higher-value items. A $600 phone on Swappa costs roughly $20 in fees versus $79.50 on eBay.

For older electronics you want to offload quickly without negotiation, Decluttr and Gazelle give instant quotes and free shipping labels. You'll get 10-30% less than marketplace value, but the convenience is worth it for low-value items.

Amazon Trade-In pays in gift card credit, not cash — only use it if you regularly shop on Amazon. The quotes are competitive for recent items but drop sharply for anything more than 2-3 years old.

Clothing is where platform choice matters most. Poshmark has the largest active buyer base for used clothing, but the 20% fee is steep. It's worth it for brands that command premium resale value — Lululemon, Patagonia, Nike, Anthropologie — because Poshmark buyers expect and pay higher prices.

For everyday clothing, Mercari charges only 10% and has a broader audience. ThredUp is best for large-volume cleanouts — send a bag of 30+ items and they handle pricing, photography, and sales. The per-item payout is low (often $2-8 for common brands), but the time savings are significant.

The RealReal is the only platform worth using for genuine luxury items — Chanel, Gucci, Hermes, Rolex. Their authentication process means buyers pay premium prices, and the consignment model handles everything.

Facebook Marketplace dominates furniture sales because of local pickup — no shipping hassle, no fees, and buyers can see the item before purchasing. Price furniture at 30-50% of retail for items in good condition. Solid wood and mid-century modern furniture holds value best.

Chairish targets higher-end furniture and vintage pieces, handling professional photography and reaching interior designers. The 20% fee is justified by higher sale prices.

OfferUp is a strong alternative to Facebook Marketplace for local sales, especially in areas where Facebook adoption is lower.

Selling $4,500 in unused items and investing the proceeds in index funds at a 7% average annual return produces roughly $17,400 in 20 years — from stuff that was collecting dust. Even better, selling personal items at a loss is not a taxable event. This is one of the few ways to generate investment capital with zero tax consequences.

Product photos are the single biggest factor in whether your listing gets clicks. You don't need a professional camera — a modern smartphone in good lighting produces excellent results. Follow these rules for every listing.

Shoot near a window during daylight hours. Natural light makes items look accurate and appealing. Avoid flash, which creates harsh shadows and washes out colors. Use a clean, neutral background — a white sheet or blank wall works perfectly.

Take at least 5 photos per listing: front, back, close-up of brand label, any flaws or wear, and an in-context shot (clothing on a hanger, electronics powered on). Listings with 5+ photos sell 30% faster and for 15% higher prices than listings with 1-2 photos.

Search your item on the platform you're using. Filter by "sold" or "completed" listings (eBay makes this easy). This shows you what buyers actually paid — not what sellers are asking. Price 5-10% above the average sold price to leave room for offers.

For items you want to sell quickly, price at or 5% below the average. For rare or in-demand items, price at the average and wait for the right buyer.

Local pickup is ideal for large, heavy, or fragile items — furniture, appliances, exercise equipment. No shipping costs, no packaging, no damage risk. Facebook Marketplace, OfferUp, Craigslist, and Nextdoor are built for local transactions.

For everything else, shipped sales reach a much larger buyer pool and typically command higher prices. Platforms like Poshmark and Mercari provide prepaid shipping labels that simplify the process.

Meet in public, well-lit locations — many police stations now offer designated safe exchange zones. Never invite buyers to your home for initial meetings. Accept cash or instant digital payments (Venmo, Zelle) only. For high-value items ($200+), meet inside a bank lobby during business hours.

Start with a single closet, drawer, or room. Pull out anything you haven't used in 12 months. Separate items into three piles: sell (worth $10+), donate (functional but low resale), and trash (broken or worn out).

Pro tip: Start with your highest-value category — electronics and designer clothing generate the most return for least effort.

Search your item on eBay (filter by "Sold Items") or the platform you plan to use. Note the average sold price — this is what buyers actually pay, not what sellers hope for.

Shoot near a window on a clean background. Get every angle: front, back, label, flaws, and an in-context shot. Listings with quality photos sell 30% faster.

Include brand, model, size, condition, and any flaws. Use the exact words buyers search for — "Lululemon Align Leggings Size 6" sells faster than "black workout pants." Honesty about wear prevents returns and builds seller reputation.

Pro tip: Apps like Crosslist and List Perfectly can automatically cross-post to multiple platforms at once, saving significant time.

If you sell personal items for less than you originally paid (which is almost always the case for used clothing, electronics, and furniture), there is no tax owed — it's considered a personal loss. However, if you sell collectibles, art, or items for MORE than you paid, the profit is taxable as a capital gain. Starting in 2024, platforms like eBay and Poshmark report sales exceeding $5,000 on Form 1099-K. Keep records of your original purchase prices to prove no gain was realized.

Selling used items is one of the simplest ways to accelerate your FI timeline — convert depreciating clutter into invested capital that compounds for decades. Match each item to the right platform: Facebook Marketplace for furniture and local sales, Swappa for phones and electronics, Poshmark for premium clothing, eBay for collectibles and niche items, and The RealReal for luxury goods. Take quality photos, price based on completed sales, and invest the proceeds in low-cost index funds. The stuff in your closet today could be worth tens of thousands in your investment accounts 20 years from now.
