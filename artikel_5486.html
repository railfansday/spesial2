GTA VI made an estimated $2.36 billion on day one. Now 1,600 Rockstar workers are voting on a union

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Vice City has opened its doors. Rockstar Games released Grand Theft Auto VI on Thursday, and by the end of its first day the game had sold an estimated 24.6 million copies worth about $2.36 billion, including preorders, according to tracking firm Sensor Tower. Take-Two Interactive, Rockstar’s parent company, has not confirmed sales figures.

If the estimate holds, that is nearly triple the roughly $800 million GTA V took on its first day in 2013, and it would make GTA VI the biggest entertainment launch on record. But beyond the sales figures, cultural conversation, and think pieces, the people who built the game are asking for eyes on what lies beyond the pixels.

More than 1,600 Rockstar employees in the U.K. are voting through Nov. 26 on whether the company should formally recognize the Rockstar Game Workers Union, part of the Independent Workers’ Union of Great Britain (IWGB). The proposed recognition agreement follows four months of negotiations between Rockstar management and staff, and a yes vote would make it the largest recognized union in the U.K. games industry, according to the IWGB.

The outcome will test whether the studio behind the biggest launch in entertainment history will give the workers who made it a formal say, at a moment when the rest of the industry is cutting jobs and betting on AI.

In an op-ed for The Guardian published Wednesday, the day before the game’s release, Rockstar North animation programmer and union representative Nestor Uriarte laid out the stakes.

“Winning union recognition would be a huge step forward in securing a meaningful say over our conditions at Rockstar, but its significance would reach far beyond that,” he wrote.

Games like this take years and hundreds of people to make. GTA VI arrives 13 years after its predecessor. Those long development cycles bring constant reshuffling of teams, which can leave workers exposed to layoffs.

That anxiety is not abstract at Rockstar. In late October 2025, the studio dismissed 31 employees in the U.K. and three in Canada, and the IWGB accused the company of union-busting. Rockstar said it fired the staffers for leaking confidential information and that the dismissals were “in no way related to people’s right to join a union or engage in union activities.” The dispute is before an employment tribunal in Glasgow, where closing submissions were scheduled for this week.

There has also been a long-running debate about working conditions at the studio. In a 2018 Kotaku investigation into the making of Red Dead Redemption 2, employees described a culture of “crunch,” the long hours and intense pushes that come before a deadline.

One former employee recalled the development of the original Red Dead Redemption: “If you left early on a weekday or weekend, you’d get dirty looks. You’d feel the stare down, and sometimes you’d see it as you were leaving. There was this culture of, if you don’t put in the hours, you’re not worth working here.”

In a Fortune profile of Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick, Zelnick said delays are sometimes part of game development and that the company’s focus is first and foremost on making a quality game.

The vote reaches beyond Rockstar. Uriarte argues a union would also give workers a voice on AI, warning that “the skills that workers spend years developing risk being devalued as their creative work is replaced by AI.”

Former Take-Two CEO Ben Feder made a related argument in Fortune this month, calling GTA VI the last pre-AI blockbuster video game of its kind.

The cuts are already visible elsewhere. In July, Microsoft said its Xbox division would eliminate 3,200 jobs, about 20% of its staff, over the following year and divest five studios, even as the company trims costs to fund its AI buildout.

With GTA VI marking a generational feat, Rockstar’s union hopes to lock in job security and fair treatment for the teams behind one of the most successful game developers of the modern era.

In Uriarte’s words: “It’s something we can, and must, embrace for ourselves. The future of the industry shouldn’t be a game played over our heads.”
