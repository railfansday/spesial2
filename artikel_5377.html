Microsoft, the FAA, Chase and United each failed on their own in one week

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Microsoft, the FAA, Chase and United all failed within five days. Each fault was separate and ordinary, and each spread in its own way.

Within a few days, work email went down, flights across the country stopped, bank payments slowed and a major airline held its departing planes at the gate. When failures arrive that close together, people start to suspect a single attacker. The whole run lasted five days, across a single weekend. Microsoft $MSFT's email outage came on a Thursday, and PBS News reports that United's departures stalled the following Monday. Each company or agency that addressed the question reported no sign of an attack. Microsoft ruled out a cyberattack and its own AI agents as causes, ABC News reports, and the FAA and the Department of Transportation saw no sign of one, according to CNBC. The Associated Press reports that Chase also ruled out a cyberattack. United found no sign that its problem involved cybersecurity. The question, then, is why so many ordinary systems failed in the same few days with no attacker in sight.

The absence of an attacker is less reassuring than it sounds. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin says the failures were unrelated to one another, which suggests their timing was a coincidence. With no sign of an outside attacker, each breakdown appears to have come from inside systems that people and institutions trust to keep running. If that account holds, the larger danger lies in unreliable systems rather than hackers. "We need to know the systems that keep this country running won't simply fall over without notice," Prins Ralston, CEO of the Australian Computer Society, told ABC News. Essential systems did fail without warning in those five days, and no villain has been found. In most cases, the damage also spread well beyond the system where the fault began.

Audacy gathered the unrelated tech breakdowns that struck travelers and businesses within days, from Microsoft's global outage to the nationwide halt on flights and the Chase payments slowdown. Each failure began with a different, ordinary fault. Each also spread in its own way, whether across borders, through an entire industry or to customers of rival banks. Readers should keep one caution in mind as they go through those days. For some of these failures, the all-clear rests on the company's own word, and for at least one, nobody yet knows why it happened.

Microsoft $MSFT 365 went down first, on Nov. 12, and Audacy reports that the fault traced back to an update made in July. That update left a latent defect in a networking gateway, and nobody caught it until services crashed worldwide for about 24 hours. Users in any region lost access, according to ABC News, so one internal fault cut off customers everywhere at once. An update that hides a flaw for months is hard to catch, because nothing looks wrong until the day it fails.

The recovery took longer than the crash itself. Services went down on a Thursday, according to ABC News, and were not fully restored until Saturday morning. Customers who relied on Microsoft spent that stretch waiting for one company to repair its own network, with no way to speed up the fix.

So many institutions depend on the same supplier that an email fault became a problem for governments, health services and shops. Immigration New Zealand halted all visa processing, according to ABC News, which means a national government stopped one of its own services because of a fault in a foreign company's network. Health workers were hit as well. Staff were "unable to refer patient details in the normal way," Rachel Harrison, public services head at the GMB union, told ABC News. Those referrals are the handoffs that patient care depends on, which makes the outage far more than an office inconvenience.

Small businesses also paid for the outage, losing up to $30,000 in one day, according to ABC News, even though they had no part in the fault and no power to fix it. People who never touched the faulty gateway paid for trusting one vendor. Their losses were the week's first sign of how far an internal fault can travel.

For part of one day, no plane in the U.S. could take off. The FAA stopped every departure across the country for under five hours, and about 12,462 flights were canceled that day, according to Audacy. In all, the halt reached tens of thousands of flights, CNBC reports, yet every aircraft already in the air landed safely. The cost was lost travel rather than danger. In that sense the halt itself was a safeguard, even if the fault behind it was not.

Officials traced the halt to a single piece of equipment. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy linked the nationwide stoppage to one router in southern Arkansas, according to CNBC. The router belonged to an air traffic control upgrade, and L3Harris Technologies is the contractor in charge of that system. One device tied to work meant to improve the system was enough to stop flights everywhere.

The reason the router failed is still unknown. "Identifying the device that failed does not establish why it failed," Democratic members of the Senate Commerce, Science and Transportation Committee said in a statement, as reported by CNBC. Until someone finds the cause, no one can promise that the same kind of part, installed the same way, won't fail again. Officials can point to the device, but they cannot yet explain the failure.

Washington is now being asked to pay for the same kind of upgrade on a much larger scale. Duffy asked Congress for $50 billion for air traffic control upgrades, CNBC reports, and Senate Commerce Chair Ted Cruz has said he doubts lawmakers will approve the whole sum. Committee Democrats want any new equipment to keep flights as safe as the old system did. A nationwide halt traced to a device from an upgrade gives that demand real weight.

The risk for travelers is not only that the old system breaks, but that the new one goes in faster than anyone understands how it fails.

The bank failure was the mildest breakdown of the week, at least on its face. On Nov. 16 a software bug at JPMorgan $JPM Chase slowed some transactions, Audacy reports, and the problem was fixed the same day and was not systemic. Next to grounded flights and dead email, the slowdown looked like a nuisance rather than a crisis. Customers also kept access to their accounts and their money, according to The Associated Press. "Not systemic" makes the problem sound contained, but the phrase speaks only to where the fault started.

The slowdown still reached people whose own banks never broke. Bank of America $BAC and Citigroup $C had no trouble with their own systems, The Associated Press reports, yet their customers saw delays whenever a payment passed through Chase. Zelle users were caught as well, even though a spokesperson for Zelle owner Early Warning Services says the payment network was fully operational. The shared network did not fail, yet transfers on it slowed because one large bank using it did. Chase put the scope in its own words. "Other banks may have been impacted by slowness in transactions only to the extent that they were a party to a transaction with us," Chase spokesperson Joe Evangelisti said. By that measure, anyone at any bank who sent money to a Chase account or received money from one was exposed to the slowdown. The fault was confined to Chase, but its effects were not.

The reassuring account of the slowdown comes from the bank that failed. Chase itself described the problem as isolated, and it presented a same-day fix as an expectation rather than a confirmed result, according to The Associated Press. No count of how many transactions or customers were affected has been reported. Without that count, nobody outside the bank can check how small the problem really was.

The week's mildest failure is mild by the measure of the one company with the most reason to call it that.

United Airlines held departing planes at the gate because the problem sat in the tools pilots rely on before every flight. During the Nov. 16 outage, pilots could not reach the applications they use to prepare for takeoff, and departures were delayed roughly an hour, according to Audacy. Losing that one set of pilot software was enough to keep a major airline's planes from leaving, though only for a short time. The problem itself lasted longer than those delays. PBS News reports that 1 1/2 hours passed between the first reports from pilots and the resolution. A delay of about an hour on the ground did not mean United had the applications working again in an hour.

The outage affected only planes that had not yet left, because pilots need the blocked applications while they prepare to depart. Flights already in the air continued unaffected, PBS News reports, and United Express regional flights kept operating as well. The damage stayed with United flights that were still on the ground and went no further than the gate.

It takes very little to stop an airline, and a single failed set of pilot tools was enough to hold United's departures. For travelers, the risk in a failure like this lies on the ground before the doors close, not once the plane is in the air.
