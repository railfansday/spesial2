The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping — release date, cast and plot explained

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Sunrise on the Reaping opens Nov. 20, 2026. See 10 characters, who plays them and the casting bet behind each role.

Sunrise on the Reaping depends on a character fans think they know well, played by an actor few of them know at all. Haymitch is the film's main character, according to Variety, and Rotten Tomatoes reports that Joseph Zada plays the young Haymitch and leads the cast. Zada has no famous name to draw a crowd. His earlier credits include We Were Liars, and ABC News reports that the Sydney-born actor also appeared in the Australian series The Invisible Boys and Total Control. The well-known actors around him can give the film stature, but only Zada's performance can make its central character work.

Audiences will compare this Haymitch with the older one Woody Harrelson played in the original films. Zada follows an actor who has earned three Oscar nominations, according to ABC News, and that is a demanding standard for a newcomer. The film's director, Francis Lawrence, acknowledges that following Harrelson in the role is a heavy task, according to The Associated Press. A copy of Harrelson's performance would only invite the comparison, and Zada has to build the role anew.

Zada plays Haymitch as the opposite of the man fans remember. The character is 16 in this story, according to Parade, and Zada chose to show the boy before the damage. "He's a heartbreaking character so the best job that I could do was to make him loving and caring, so we can see how much this person has been through to reach the cynical, alcoholic, sarcastic person that is Woody's Haymitch," Zada ABC News. Haymitch is one of 48 tributes reaped for the 50th Games, double the usual number, according to . A contest that large and that cruel makes it believable that a caring boy comes out of it broken.

Fans have already watched President Coriolanus Snow at two very different points in his life, and the actor who takes over the role has to answer to both. Most viewers picture the older Snow, the ruler Donald Sutherland played in all four original films, according to Variety. The younger Snow is a recent memory, because Tom Blyth played him in 2023’s The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes. Ralph Fiennes plays Snow in Sunrise on the Reaping, Rotten Tomatoes reports, and his performance has to make one man of the young Snow viewers saw and the old Snow they remember.

Most recasting aims to make a character new, but this casting aims to keep Snow recognizable. "We wanted to honor Donald Sutherland by having one of this generation's greatest actors play President Snow 24 years before Katniss Everdeen entered the arena," Nina Jacobson, a producer at Color Force, told Variety. The Snow in this film already holds power, and Fiennes has to play him in a way that points ahead to the man Sutherland played. That sets an unusual standard for success. Fiennes succeeds if viewers can see Sutherland's Snow taking shape in him, not if he makes the character entirely his own.

Fiennes’s recent work backs up the producer’s praise. His performance in Conclave earned him Academy Award and Golden Globe nominations, according to Variety. His Harry Potter role also means he has already played a major part in a long-running franchise.

With Snow, the film uses star power to serve continuity. Audiences will watch a third actor in the role, and the casting works if they still recognize the president Sutherland played.

The film has given one of its backstage Capitol jobs to a star. Magno Stift is the fashion designer for the tributes, according to Rotten Tomatoes, and Billy Porter, whom many viewers know from Pose, plays him. TheWrap reports that Magno is District 12's designer, a working post rather than a seat of power. A job like that rarely draws much attention on its own, so Magno is a clear test of the decision to put famous actors in small parts. If audiences take to the character, much of the credit will belong to Porter and to the people who cast him.

Magno's marriage explains why the part needed a big name. He is the estranged husband of Drusilla Sickle, TheWrap reports, so he works alongside the tributes while living with a broken marriage to another member of the Capitol staff. Glenn Close plays Drusilla, which sets Porter's scenes against another heavyweight actor. Together, Close and Porter give the Capitol side of the story a relationship for viewers to follow, beyond costumes and ceremony.

The role asks for glamour and grief at once. Magno's job is to create spectacle for the Capitol to put on display, and his broken marriage gives him a private wound. Producer Nina Jacobson told TheWrap that Porter is "one of those rare performers who can dazzle and devastate in equal measure on stage and screen."

The thing to watch is whether Porter plays Magno as a man with a wound or as a celebrity in a costume.

Fans of the Hunger Games films know how Mags died far better than how she lived. In Sunrise on the Reaping, Lili Taylor plays Mags years before that ending, as a former champion of the Games, according to Rotten Tomatoes. Parade reports that Lynn Cohen played Mags in Catching Fire, so moviegoers have met this victor only late in her life. Taylor is filling in the earlier years of a woman audiences remember for a single act.

Mags earned her place in moviegoers' memory without saying a word. She never spoke in Catching Fire, according to Parade, and her defining moment on screen came when she sacrificed herself. That leaves Taylor almost nothing on screen to contradict and nearly everything to invent, including Mags's voice, her temperament and the person who made that choice. This is where the casting carries its risk. A younger Mags who talks and acts in a big way could deepen the woman fans grieved, or she could quietly turn her into someone else.

Taylor’s Mags appears only briefly in the trailer, in a hug with Haymitch, Parade reports. In the books, she serves as a mentor for District 12 even though she comes from District 4. If the film keeps that role, looking after another district's tributes gives her a reason to stay at Haymitch's side. It would also point ahead to the sacrifice that later made her matter to fans, when she put others before herself.

Taylor's Mags will be judged on how much she speaks, and on whether her new voice explains the silent woman fans remember or replaces her.

Effie Trinket returns as a younger version of an established character, according to The Associated Press. The filmmakers can't build her from nothing, because audiences already know the woman she will become. Elle Fanning plays Effie, Rotten Tomatoes reports, and in this story she works as Haymitch's stylist. As his stylist, she is responsible for how Haymitch looks when he appears before the Capitol, which ties her to the lead from the start.

Fanning will be measured against Elizabeth Banks, who played Effie in the original films. A brand-new character would be judged on her own terms. This Effie is meant to grow into the woman Banks played, so viewers will hold Fanning to that earlier performance whether she wants them to or not. She has to meet two demands at once. Her Effie has to read as the same person audiences remember. She also has to feel like a woman with her own life rather than an impression of Banks. If Fanning copies too closely, she invites the comparison. If she strays too far, fans may not recognize the character at all.

Fanning's success depends less on how much she does on screen than on how quickly viewers stop comparing and accept her as Effie.

The Plutarch Heavensbee who returns in Sunrise on the Reaping has no power yet, and he is far from the man who ran the Games in the later films. Jesse Plemons plays a young version of the character, according to Rotten Tomatoes, rather than the older Plutarch of the original films. CinemaBlend reports that Philip Seymour Hoffman played that older Plutarch. At this stage, Plutarch records the spectacle instead of designing it, working as a cameraman on the crew that films District 12's reaping. Plemons has to cover the distance between that job and the man who later serves as Head Gamemaker of the 75th Hunger Games and then becomes a leader of the rebellion. He has to play a minor figure whose power lies years ahead and still let audiences see the schemer he will become.

This recast carries more weight than most because Plutarch was Hoffman's final film role, according to CinemaBlend, and for many viewers it is the last character they watched him play. Plemons played the son of Hoffman’s cult leader in The Master in 2012, so the role passes to an actor who once played his on-screen son. "His Plutarch will be both a tribute to the character fans have already come to know and a portrayal he makes his own," Erin Westerman, co-president of Lionsgate Motion Picture Group, said, as reported by CinemaBlend. Meeting both aims is hard in a role so closely tied to one actor, and viewers will hold Plemons to that standard.

The people making the next Hunger Games film don't have to find an audience. They have to avoid disappointing the one already waiting for it. That audience is large, and it comes from two directions. Moviegoers have followed the franchise through five films, Variety reports, and readers bought 1.5 million copies of the new novel in its first week, according to TheWrap. The novel came out in 2025, according to ABC News, and its readers will reach the theater with the story still fresh. Sunrise on the Reaping faces a double test, with readers holding it up against the novel and moviegoers holding it up against the earlier performances in these roles.

Many characters in the film are people fans have already met, now younger and played by someone else, and each of those casting choices invites a comparison before filming even begins. The setting makes those comparisons unavoidable. The story goes back to the 50th Hunger Games, the Second Quarter Quell, according to Collider. At an earlier Games, known figures have to appear at an earlier stage of their lives rather than as new creations. The producers have met that pressure by putting experienced, well-known actors even in supporting parts. "We've been blessed with very sophisticated actors in our roles," Nina Jacobson, a producer at Color Force, told Variety. The story still belongs to a lead most viewers have never seen before, and the stars around him are there to steady the film rather than to take it over.

Rotten Tomatoes set out to introduce the film's major characters and the actors playing them, from Glenn Close's Drusilla Sickle and Kieran Culkin's Caesar Flickerman to a young Plutarch Heavensbee. Taken together, these casting choices amount to a series of bets, each on whether a new actor can make an old character feel both familiar and new. Audiences can start judging those bets when the film opens in U.S. theaters on Nov. 20, 2026, according to The Associated Press. Each character below represents one of those bets. Some depend on keeping a known figure recognizable, some on rebuilding one, and some on a star in a part that looks small on paper. For each, the thing to watch is whether viewers stop comparing and accept the person on screen.

If Zada succeeds, viewers will see Harrelson's drunk as a survivor rather than a joke, and they will understand a character they thought they already knew in a new way.

On paper, Drusilla Sickle is defined by a job rather than a story. Rotten Tomatoes lists her as the escort for District 12, the Capitol official who accompanies that district's tributes, which is a part most viewers would expect to stay at the edge of the frame. The filmmakers gave that supporting post to Glenn Close, who has earned eight Oscar nominations, according to Variety. The distance between the size of the role and the stature of the actor is the bet. The filmmakers are wagering that a performer with her record can turn a chaperone into someone audiences remember after the credits roll.

The producers expect Close to make a minor part into a defining one. "Glenn Close is a dream Drusilla. She brings so much of her intellect and imagination to each role, creating characters who are unforgettable and iconic," producer Nina Jacobson told Variety. An iconic escort is a large ambition, and it reaches beyond Close's own scenes. Drusilla represents the Capitol to District 12's tributes up close, so the way Close plays her will shape how viewers read the Capitol's treatment of the young people it sends into the arena. A warm Drusilla, a cold one or a careless one would each tell the audience something different about the people who run the Games.

Close's casting pays off if she makes the Capitol's chaperone feel essential to the story, and it falls short if a star of her standing ends up with too little to do.

Wiress, a victor from District 3, mentors the District 12 team in Sunrise on the Reaping, according to Rotten Tomatoes. That puts her beside Haymitch from the start. Film audiences met her much later in her life, and this story shows where she stood years before.

The casting gives a part that filmgoers remember to an actress whom many younger viewers know first from television. Maya Hawke of Stranger Things takes over a character that Amanda Plummer played in Catching Fire, according to Collider. Many viewers will recognize Hawke before they think of Plummer. The question isn't whether she resembles her predecessor but whether audiences accept her as the same woman at an earlier age. Fans remember Wiress mostly for a repeated phrase, "tick tock," and Hawke has to make that habit believable as something her younger character could grow into. Hawke’s casting pays off if viewers see the Wiress they remember in the younger woman on screen.

Beetee comes into Sunrise on the Reaping as a past victor, according to Rotten Tomatoes. That gives him standing before the film’s Games even begin, but it doesn’t leave him on the sidelines. He lends his technical expertise to efforts to sabotage the Games, which puts him inside the story’s push against them. The part carries real weight in the plot, and that makes the choice of actor more important than a supporting credit suggests.

Jeffrey Wright played Beetee from Catching Fire onward, according to Variety. The prequel gives the role to Kelvin Harrison Jr., who plays a younger version. Harrison’s task is to create the earlier man that Wright’s version grew out of, without imitating the later performance.

This Beetee is a father as well as a victor. Variety reports that his son, Ampert Latier, is a tribute in the 50th Hunger Games. That is the contest the film follows, so Beetee works on the victors' side while his own child competes in the arena. That father-and-son thread adds personal stakes to a role that already carries weight in the plot.

Harrison's casting will be judged on whether he can play a technician working against the Games while his son is one of their tributes.

Kieran Culkin is taking over a character people already remember, not introducing a new one. Culkin plays the host Caesar Flickerman in Sunrise on the Reaping, according to Rotten Tomatoes. Variety reports that Stanley Tucci played him in all four original films, and that performance is the one Culkin will be measured against. Viewers will also bring a second memory into the theater, since many of them know Culkin as Roman Roy on Succession. Lionsgate Motion Picture Group co-president Erin Westerman says Tucci left fans a Caesar they won't forget, and she expects Culkin to create a version of the host that belongs to him alone. The studio is openly asking for reinvention rather than imitation. The test is whether audiences can stop seeing both Tucci and Roman Roy when Culkin is on screen.

Caesar can look like the franchise's comic relief, but his job on screen is to sell the Games to the Capitol. That job makes him one of the story's sharpest views of the Capitol's cruelty. "A host like Caesar transforms brutality into entertainment, and an actor as layered as Kieran makes sure we don’t look away," Nina Jacobson, a producer at Color Force, said, as reported by Variety. That puts a double demand on Culkin. He has to be charming enough that moviegoers enjoy watching him the way the Capitol crowd does, and unsettling enough that they notice what they are enjoying.

Culkin's Caesar succeeds if he makes viewers uncomfortable with their own attention. If he plays the part only for laughs, the film loses its satire.

The bet pays off only if audiences can see, behind a camera on reaping day, the man who will later help lead a rebellion against the Capitol.
