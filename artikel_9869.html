In the 20th century we had radio and television programs; a television executive at ABC could program Thursday nights as a comedy block; the television itself became the vessel for playing Video Computer System game programs in the 1970s. By the 1980s one could program their VCR to record television or even program their home computer on that same screen.

Programs have been ubiquitous your entire life. You heard them. You watched them. You played them. You may have even made them. And as broadcasting has given way to narrowcasting and analog signals have given way to digital software, programs are increasingly watching you and using that information to alter everything from prices to our perception of reality.

The arrival of programmable interactive media led to a frenzied interest that crescendoed in the 1990s with the introduction of the DVD and the world wide web. By this point, the information age had become tangible in the everyday life of ordinary people. But people generally engaged new media like they did television: passively. But programmeable media is too powerful to tolerate passive consumption. The internet is truly a different type of media; digital interactivity facilitates the same physical reach to both the institutional power and the distributed peer. The interface to this media is a sophisticated language colloquially known as programming.The name programming elides its expressive power, which is somewhere between mathematics and natural language. And if you do not know how to speak it, the institutional powers will speak it for you.

Take the basic example of RSS/atom. I love RSS and use it to this day. The premise is simple: in a diverse information ecosystem, you can select feeds from major corporate news agencies, collectively funded information providers, and self-funded bloggers and they all get equal space in your reader. The feed is flexible; the sources inevitably change from year to year as you discover new things and get tired of old things. But this intellectual freedom comes at a cost - you must make choices. You must create the program. The alternative is an algorithmically defined news app that makes the program for you. And the organization controlling that program makes tiny, seemingly innocuous tweaks every week which can have profound consequences over time.For example, the person who added the infinite scroll to the Facebook timeline, Aza Raskin, now says he regrets it. When programming was less well-compensated and less highly regarded, it was a job for women. Much has been written about how LLMs will eliminate the entire field of programming - as if people will no longer need to know how to speak for themselves. Programming will still exist. But much will be obscured by a consumer veneer.

The language of programming is actually the language of systems. Self-trained hackers were aware of this language when they were hacking the telephone networks of the 1970s. And freshmen at MIT in the 1980s understood this after reading the Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs. Humans build systems. And systems contribute to our view of the world that we are born into. LLMs can provide a surface-level understanding of a system but only hands-on experience can provide knowledge.

You are constrained by any system that you cannot inspect. This is true of both designed and emergent systems such as cities, forests, and networks. The physical computer is unique because it is the artificial embodiment of a natural science. The theoretical computer has no politics. So how do we fight back against these overwhelming odds? We program. Not as an offense into their territory. But as a defense of our territory - our mind.

Civilization is built on media. You cannot shelter your sons and daughters; you have already seen the impact on your friends and neighbors. It is past time to accept that in a programmable world of digital media, we can no longer blame the television executive or the media baron for their programs. We must support and participate in alternative systems. From food to computing - we must create and participate in systems that minimize cruelty as a bulwark against systems that maximize profit.

The lingua franca of these systems is software. This language is at least as important as the language you speak with your friends. Learning the language of systems - a.k.a programming - will be a mind-opening second or third language that you can use to manifest the world you want to see in everyday media.
