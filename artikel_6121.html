Skift Commerce Summit 2026: Five Decisions in the Room

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

AI agents are here. Travel isn’t ready. Decisions made today will determine who controls the booking, the customer relationship, and the economics of travel commerce.

AI agents are already booking travel. One invite-only booking agent reported more than $1 billion a year in transaction volume, half of which was travel. Investors have already placed their bets on who wins and who loses. Most travel companies are still built for a traveler who searches, scrolls, and clicks.

Direct or third-party distribution used to be the primary choice travel suppliers had to make. Now an agent may decide which brands show up, which system can complete the booking, which card pays, and which loyalty program earns. Hotels alone spend more than $105 billion a year on distribution, and AI companies want a share of it.

The agents are here. Travel is not ready. Travel companies have to connect the dots inside the new travel commerce stack, and Skift Commerce Summit brings senior commerce leaders together around five decisions travel companies have to make now.

AI has moved quickly into the travel customer journey for inspiration and planning, but not booking… yet. According to Skift Research, direct suppliers lead with a 49% net comfort score, followed by packaged tours at 37%, travel agents at 34%, loyalty portals at 31%, and OTAs at 20%. AI and chatbots score -7%. [1]

However, Skift Research describes the discovery path shifting from “search-scroll-compare” toward “ask-shortlist-decide.” As soon as travelers gain confidence in booking through AI platforms, space for brands “on the shelf” is poised to shrink. [2]

Hotels, airlines, or other travel retailers are racing to optimize for AI discoverability, but the more control they give to AI platforms, the more they risk losing direct customer relationships. They have to decide how to prioritize what they value most, and whether distribution alone is worth it if they become interchangeable within someone else’s interface.

Travelers are comfortable booking across a wide range of channels, but that also means today’s marketplace has a wider swath of competitors.

Travel’s AI discussion is moving downstream. It’s not only a customer relationship question, but also a technological one. Even if they’re discovered, booked, and integrated into the loyalty ecosystem, brands have to ensure the infrastructure works when an AI agent starts to handle trip management.

It’s one thing for an AI assistant to drive traffic to a brand’s site. The open decision is whether travel companies find it worthwhile to give an agent the tools to compare offers, change or cancel an order, and fix problems on the traveler’s behalf.

A better conversational front end can’t compensate for fragmented inventory, legacy servicing systems, or commerce technology designed for humans.

Banks, card issuers, retailers, membership programs, and technology platforms have built businesses around the traveler without having to own (or even sell) an airline seat or hotel room. They can make money from payments, rewards, subscriptions, advertising, financing, and the broader customer relationship surrounding a trip.

That changes the competitive economics for travel companies. A bank can use travel to increase card spend. A membership business can use it to make a subscription more valuable. A technology platform can influence the purchase while monetizing the customer elsewhere. These companies can compete for the same traveler without depending on the booking itself for most of their economics.

The decision is how much of that value suppliers and other travel-native companies can capture themselves as more of the commercial relationship moves into ecosystems built outside the industry.

Non-travel companies are profiting more and more off travel, stepping in to sell ancillaries and earn loyalty. 32% of travelers rank credit-card or bank loyalty programs as the most rewarding, ahead of hotel programs at 26% and airline programs at 20%. [1]

The direct-versus-third-party debate has shaped travel distribution strategy for decades. The economics have never been as simple as choosing one side. A direct booking can carry marketing, loyalty, technology, and acquisition costs of its own. An intermediary can bring incremental demand, reach a customer the brand would not have acquired directly, or fill inventory that otherwise would have gone unsold.

The channel mix is also getting more crowded. OTAs remain important, but banks, credit-card programs, membership platforms, social channels, AI interfaces, and other partners are creating new paths to the customer. Each comes with different costs, different access to customer data, and a different ability to generate demand.

The decision is which channels produce the strongest return for a particular customer, market, and trip. The best distribution strategy may use direct channels to deepen valuable customer relationships while paying intermediaries when they can deliver demand more efficiently than the brand can acquire it on its own.

“The competition to be a travel distributor has never been this intense,” says Skift Research, pointing to credit cards, social platforms, AI-native tools, OTAs, and suppliers all competing for a larger role in the booking journey. [3]

Travel companies can find unique growth opportunities by leveraging an existing customer base, brand, asset, or operating capability to create a new revenue stream. The challenge occurs when they find themselves wading into businesses they were not originally built to run.

The decision is how far that expansion should go. Some adjacencies strengthen the core business because they use capabilities the company already has. Others require new technology, operations, capital, or customer behavior that the company has never had to manage before. Growth can create leverage. It can also create complexity faster than revenue.

A Skift and ZS survey found that 75% of senior travel executives have non-core assets they can monetize, but only 32% described their current approach to doing so as “very” innovative. [4]

Discovery no longer guarantees ownership. Distribution extends beyond an OTA or supplier website and, indeed, beyond travel-native companies themselves. Payments and loyalty can generate value beyond the booking. AI is becoming another intermediary, another channel, and eventually the buyer itself.

Every travel company now faces the same question: which parts of the customer relationship, the transaction and the economics do you need to own before an agent stands between you and the traveler, and which can sit on someone else’s platform?
