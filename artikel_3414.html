'A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving' Has a Peppermint Patty Problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Poor Charlie Brown. If he isn’t making life worse for himself, then the world does it for him, like getting rocks for treats at Halloween, as he did in It's the Great Pumpkin, Charlie Brown. He is, for lack of a better word, a magnet for grief and misfortune. But in A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving, the source of his grief at least has a name: Peppermint Patty (Christopher DeFaria). And she's a problem.

It's Thanksgiving, and Charlie Brown (Todd Barbee) and Sally (Hilary Momberger) are getting ready to go to their grandmother's house for Thanksgiving dinner when the phone rings. It's Peppermint Patty, and she has great news after the obligatory "How you been, Chuck?" It turns out that her father is out of town, so, lucky for ol' Chuck, she can come to his house and share Thanksgiving with him. It's a decidedly one-sided conversation, with Charlie Brown unable to get a word in. Not just once, or twice, but thrice, with Patty calling back first to tell him that she's also invited Marcie (Jimmy Ahrens), and again saying that she's now invited Franklin (Robin Reed).

The immediate problem is that Charlie Brown won't even be there, and, secondly, it isn't something he even initiated. He can't call Peppermint Patty back, because, as he says, "You never get to say anything." But dinner isn't until 4:30, so he has time to put together something, so it's all hands on deck as he, Sally, Linus (Stephen Shea), and Snoopy (Bill Melendez) set up a table, chairs, and prepare a meal consisting of toast, pretzel sticks, popcorn, and jelly beans.

It's not turkey, but given the time and resources available, it's a pretty decent, kid-friendly alternative. So when Patty, Marcie and Franklin arrive, everything's set to go. Linus begins the meal by leading them in grace (although closer to a history lesson). The dinner is unveiled and everyone digs in... except for Patty. She is irate, wondering who the blockhead is that came up with the menu when it was supposed to be turkey, mashed potatoes, pumpkin pie, and all the other traditional offerings of a Thanksgiving dinner. As she's ranting, Charlie Brown slinks away, having failed once more.

The thing is, Charlie Brown hasn't failed at all. He coordinated a meal, albeit an unconventional one, invited his friends as well at the last minute, and did so graciously. Peppermint Patty, on the other hand, passive/aggressively dictated that a Thanksgiving dinner was going to happen, and invited others without asking Charlie Brown first. And that's before dinner. She's rude, insulting, and flat-out ungrateful for all of Charlie Brown's efforts. Even for Charlie Brown, the poster child of being treated unfairly by life, her demands and subsequent ingratitude are a kick to the Schulzs'. There's only one word to describe Peppermint Patty: Bully.

It's terrible behavior, yet there is a cleverness to it that ties into Charles M. Schulz's penchant for addressing mental health. It comes as part of a throwaway line when Peppermint Patty first calls Charlie Brown: the fact that her dad isn't in

town for Thanksgiving. Bullying has been tied to a dysfunctional family environment, and while it's not stated outright, Patty's dad not being around for an important holiday certainly suggests the possibility. It would also easily explain Patty's behavior, placing the demands of a perfect Thanksgiving on Charlie Brown because she won't have her own, and when it doesn't meet expectations, she lashes out.

This young philosopher has more to say than "Good Grief."

But just because we now understand the behavior, it doesn't excuse it, which is where Marcie comes in. She is the only one who confronts Patty about her actions, pointing out how she is the one who invited herself, Marcie, and Franklin to a dinner that was never originally intended to take place. And she does so calmly, yet firmly, and it forces Patty to be accountable and take ownership of her own actions. So yes, Peppermint Patty is a problem, but she's given the opportunity to learn and grow from it. That lesson is something that is as relevant as it has ever been, in an age where the actions of social media bullying, which mirror the same traits, are themselves a problem. Even more so: at least Peppermint Patty was visible, whereas more often than not, social media bullies do so anonymously.

What if, then, Peppermint Patty became the face of it? In doing so, the anonymous aren't so anonymous anymore, and standing up to that, in the calm, firm, and self-assured way Marcie did, is the key to turning it around. It may be naive to suggest that such a complex emotional issue can be cured by being like Marcie, but striving for that assertiveness can only be beneficial. And there's a lesson in Patty's about-face, too, one where Charlie Brown not only graciously accepts her apology, but extends the invitation to join him and Sally at his grandmother's for dinner (after asking first, of course). Patty is rewarded for her mea culpa, and Charlie Brown, for once, is the true hero in his own special.
