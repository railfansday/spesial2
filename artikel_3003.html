If I Could Buy Only 1 Dividend Stock With $1,000 Before 2027, It Would Be This One

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

There are several good ones to choose from right now, but the factors holding this one back should begin abating soon.

By James Brumley – Nov 19, 2026 at 2:00PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Realty Income ›

Attempting to perfectly time your entry into a new trade usually isn't worth the effort. If you've got the capital available and are happy enough with the price, in most instances you're better off just diving in once you've made your pick.

Every now and then, though, an overlooked opportunity surfaces. This is one of those times. If you're looking for a new income-generating investment for your portfolio, consider Realty Income (O -1.24%) before the end of the year while its forward-looking dividend yield is 5.6%. Here's why.

Realty Income isn't a conventional company. It's a real estate investment trust, or REIT. That just means it owns a portfolio of revenue-bearing real estate, such as apartment complexes, office buildings, and the like, and passes along the majority of its profits to shareholders as dividends (though sometimes these payments are called distributions). REITs typically yield in the same ballpark as longer-term bonds, although like bonds, these yields can vary widely with quality.

Even by REIT standards, however, Realty Income is unique. Its specialty is brick-and-mortar retailing, with its top tenants including Dollar General, 7-Eleven, Walgreens, and Walmart. It owns 357 million square feet of consumer-facing space, divided across more than 15,000 storefronts, collectively worth $83 billion and generating about $5.4 billion in annual rent payments.

At first blush, it feels risky. The ongoing growth of e-commerce is still chipping away at in-person shopping. Coresight Research expects about 8,000 U.S. store closures this year alone, versus around 5,500 new store openings, extending a multi-year streak of net losses.

Realty Income is largely sidestepping this headwind, though. Give credit to the quality of its tenants. Once outfits like Walgreens, Home Depot, and Walmart incur the time, trouble, and expense of establishing a new location, they tend to stay put, holding out for a return on their investment. And unlike many smaller retailers, these bigger and better-known names have the financial wherewithal to continue making their rent payments.

To this end, this REIT's occupancy rate is consistently right around 99%, supporting a monthly -- yes, monthly -- dividend payment that's not only been paid like clockwork for over 50 years but has also been raised every year for the past 31 years.

It's an impressive pedigree. But shares of this REIT certainly haven't performed as well as you might have expected them to of late. This ticker's barely up year to date and is still barely above its pre-COVID-pandemic peak.

What gives? There's never a single perfect cut-and-dried reason a stock performs as it does. In this instance, though, there are a couple of key contributing factors.

One of these factors is interest rates. They've been up since the current bull market began in late 2022, in an effort to curb inflation resulting from the stimulus injected into the economy during and because of the coronavirus pandemic. Although real estate investment trusts can typically pass their own higher borrowing costs along to tenants, it's not clear how well Realty Income would manage the $26.5 billion in longer-term debt already on its balance sheet while adding more debt to continue expanding. Investors have erred on the side of caution by avoiding the stock.

The other factor at play here is the economic environment. The cost of everything continues to rise, eating away at consumers' confidence and buying power. Assuming this translates into a headwind for Realty Income, the market's been cautious.

The two underpinnings of this caution may have already run their full cyclical course, however, setting the stage for a turnaround sooner rather than later.

As for interest rates, although a quarter-point increase in the fed funds rate is practically a certainty for December, this is largely already priced in. This may also be the last of any rate hikes for a while. As of September, the Federal Reserve's Open Market Committee, along with several analysts, still expect interest rates to begin inching lower again next year, easing some of Realty Income's borrowing pain.

And as for the economic backdrop, sure, everything remains expensive, and unmanageable debt is increasingly becoming a very real problem. The New York Federal Reserve recently reported that overall loan delinquency rates moved up 30 basis points to 5% during the third quarter of this year, led by mortgages and auto loans.

Now take a closer look at all of the data. While there's no denying some consumers are miserable, there's also no denying that the nation's unemployment rate of 4.1% is healthy, and retail spending continues to reach record-breaking levels. Coming up with discretionary spending money isn't a problem for most households right now.

But what about any of this makes Realty Income a buy before 2027? I suspect the market's going to start seeing -- and accepting -- these two realities before 2026 comes to a close. More specifically, I believe investors will start to recognize that, rather than slipping into the recession many people fear is brewing, the economy will be able to navigate past this weird headwind with minimal fuss.

That doesn't mean Realty Income’s shares will only climb from here. Indeed, if the broad market's recent weakness is the beginning of a full-scale (and overdue) correction, this ticker will almost certainly be dragged lower with it. That dip would be your pre-2027 buying opportunity.

Even if that dip doesn't materialize and this stock instead drifts back into bullish mode again with other stocks, the longer-term bullish argument still stands. The market can't ignore that this REIT's top and bottom lines both continue to improve despite the troubling economic rhetoric. I can see this acceptance taking shape before the end of the year.
