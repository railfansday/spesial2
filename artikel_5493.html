3 risks facing SpaceX’s first attempt to catch Starship’s upper stage

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

SpaceX wants to catch Starship's upper stage on Flight 15. Three risks stand in the way, from engine trouble to a return over land.

Rocket companies now bring the bottom half of their rockets home as a routine part of flying. SpaceX grabs its Super Heavy boosters out of the air at the launch tower, and two other companies, Blue Origin and LandSpace, have also brought their lower stages back to the ground. The top half is different: SpaceX has never caught Starship's upper stage, known as the ship, according to SNJ Today. The work that remains sits on the half of the rocket that does the hardest flying. That half travels all the way to orbit and has to survive the trip back, and no catch of it has been proven.

Reusing a rocket has never been enough on its own to make spaceflight cheap. NASA refurbished its space shuttles and flew them again, yet each launch remained enormously expensive. The savings come when a company can recover hardware and put it back on the launch pad. SpaceX has done that with its boosters, catching them and flying two of them again, according to SNJ Today. Catching the ship would extend that approach to the whole vehicle. That is why SpaceX is willing to take the risk, and it is why a first attempt carries so much weight for the company.

CNN set out the main dangers facing SpaceX's first attempt to catch the Starship ship itself. They include engine trouble on the previous flight, a long stay in orbit before reentry and the ship's return over land. Flight 15 is scheduled to launch from Starbase in South Texas, according to SNJ Today. This time the ship is meant to reenter over land and head for the tower instead of ending its flight in the sea. A successful catch requires SpaceX to guide a vehicle returning from orbit to a fixed point on the ground, where earlier ships could finish in open water. The dangers do not add up to a single failure. Each one is a separate way the attempt could end before the ship ever reaches the tower.

The tower catch is the last act of Flight 15, and the ship has to get through a long day in space before it gets there. SpaceX plans to keep Starship in orbit for 22 hours, according to CNN. SNJ Today reports that the ship will circle Earth about 14 times during that stretch before it heads home. Controllers can try a catch only after a clean orbital phase, so the ship has to stay healthy through every one of those orbits. The real test of the flight is whether Starship can last a full day in space and still be fit to come home.

SpaceX has also planned ways to end the mission short of the tower. Controllers can end the flight early while the ship is still in orbit and bring it down in the ocean, which would call off the catch without any return to Starbase. They can also set the ship down in the Gulf, or they can destroy it and give up the vehicle entirely. A flight that goes mostly right could still end in the water, and that outcome would follow SpaceX's plan rather than break it.

People betting on the flight treat the ship catch as the real gamble. Bettors on Polymarket see a booster catch, which SpaceX has managed before, as about 80% likely, while they put the odds of the ship being caught on this first try at about 40%, according to SNJ Today. Elon Musk is more hopeful than the bettors. "Success is not guaranteed on these first flights, but I’d say it’s more likely than not that this succeeds next flight," Musk said on SpaceX's October earnings call, as reported by SNJ Today. The bettors put the ship catch below even odds, and Musk puts it above. Neither side expects a catch on the first try to be close to certain.

On Saturday, the first thing to watch is whether controllers clear the ship to come home at all, before anyone asks whether the tower can catch it.

SpaceX brought its ship through an engine failure on Flight 14, but that recovery offers less comfort for a catch than it seems. The ship continued to orbit after losing an engine, CNN reports, and SpaceX built the vehicle with spare engine capacity so it could keep flying safely if a few engines quit. A climb to orbit leaves some room for an engine to fail. A catch requires the ship to come down at one exact point at the tower, and the Raptor engine failures on Flight 14 show how engine trouble could threaten a landing that precise. SpaceX has shown that its spare engine capacity works during ascent, but nobody has yet shown that it works during a precision return. That return is what Flight 15 will attempt.

How much the engine failure matters for the catch depends on its cause. The shutdown came after the ship separated from the booster, and the cause had not been determined, according to Reuters. Dean Sladen, an aerospace engineer for Accu Components, says the trouble may have come from a single faulty part that failed only once. In that case, SpaceX could make a narrow repair. A deeper cause would likely take longer to find and fix.

The schedule at risk extends beyond SpaceX. NASA aims to land astronauts on the moon by 2028, according to Reuters. "Any engine fix that holds up the next flights, including the planned attempt to catch the ship with the launch tower's arms (after re-entry), eats into that schedule," Sladen said. Before the ship gets anywhere near the tower, SpaceX has to explain an engine failure quickly enough that the fix does not slow down everything that depends on it.

Starship's upper stage has to come home from orbit, and no rocket maker has yet landed a stage moving that fast. The Super Heavy booster that SpaceX has learned to catch travels at about 6,000 km/h (3,728 mph) before landing, CNN reports, while a ship in orbit can move at up to 27,000 km/h. The ship has to shed far more speed than any recovered booster before a tower can reach it. The catch is only the last few seconds of a return that has to go right all the way down from orbit.

SpaceX has no earlier orbital vehicle to copy for this return. The space shuttle flew again, but it touched down on a runway on landing gear, and a vehicle built for a tower to grab cannot use that method. Lars Blackmore, SpaceX's senior principal Mars landing engineer, says the company is building a launch vehicle that must also work as a spacecraft. No vehicle has yet flown home that way.

Bringing the ship back for a catch gives up the safest way an orbital flight can end. CBC reports that Aaron Boley, co-director at the Outer Space Institute, considers a planned ocean finish the best of several poor choices.

The federal limit on danger to the public sounds close to zero, but it has not prevented a failure. The Federal Aviation Administration will let Flight 15 go ahead only if the odds of anyone being hurt or killed stay under 1 in a million. SpaceX had to clear that same bar before its 2025 launch, and that flight still ended in an explosion. According to CBC, Ship 33 broke up uncontrolled. Nobody chose where its pieces came down.

Even specialists cannot say with confidence where debris from a breakup would fall. Efforts to model such breakups are "woefully ill at being able to predict anything with any sort of meaningful accuracy," Moriba Jah, a professor of aerospace engineering at the University of Texas Austin, said, as reported by CBC.

The ship's speed is what makes it worth catching, and that same speed means no one can confidently say where the pieces would land if the return fails.
