Breaking Baz: ‘Bunker’ Writer-Director Florian Zeller Takes Us Behind The Scenes With Penélope Cruz, Javier Bardem, Stephen Graham & Dame Eileen Atkins

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Speaking over Zoom from his home in Paris, Florian Zeller says he could write poems all about Penélope Cruz. Instead, he wrote a thrilling movie called Bunker for her, and her husband, Javier Bardem.

Zeller, the playwright, screenwriter and screen and stage director behind The Father (for which Anthony Hopkins won his second Best Actor Oscar) and plays that have run on Broadway and in London’s West End, emphasizes that he wasn’t interested in Cruz’s radiant beauty. “Of course she’s beautiful,” he says, but, “somehow we don’t care.”

“Of course, she is talented, but she has something more, something unique, and it has to do with the fact that she’s always working with who she is. She’s always working with her deep emotions, and that in my experience is really rare.” Watch on Deadline

We both extol the three performances Cruz appears in this season. First, his Bunker, of course, in which she plays Sofía, an accomplished editor of foreign fiction at an upscale London publishing house. Bardem plays Miguel, her husband, an eminent architect, who’s handy in the kitchen and adores preparing the family’s meals. In addition, in the Los Javis-directed La Bola Negra, Cruz plays Nené Romero, a cupletista, who performs bawdy songs for troops during the Spanish Civil War. Then in Olivia Wilde’s chamber comedy The Invite, she shows off her comedic chops in the role of Piña, one half of a couple with an experimental approach to partnership.

But it’s Bunker that gives Cruz a lead role and center stage.

As to how Zeller connected with Cruz and Bardem he explains that he had a meeting with Cruz four years ago “but the project was not the right one.” Then, one day, Bardem rang him and marveled over Zeller’s plays and films, saying that he’d screened The Father countless times to study Anthony Hopkins’s performance, to understand how he did it. A little while later, they were both in Los Angeles and Bardem visited Zeller and spent an afternoon in his LA garden “talking about life.” That’s when Zeller shared his desire to write something for both Bardem and Cruz.

Zeller says now that part of his motivation was because they are “such an iconic couple then reality would go almost against fiction, and so I thought it would be much more interesting, challenging, and beautiful to have layers to every scene and to blur the line between fiction and reality.” As with Nicole Kidman and Tom Cruise in Stanley Kubrick’s Eyes Wide Shut and Mike Nichols’s screen version of Edward Albee’s seminal play Who’s Afraid of Virginia Wolf? with Elizabeth Taylor and Richard Burton, we’re fascinated when reality meets fiction, or vice-versa, but it’s not, as Zeller puts it, “to put ourselves in a position of voyeurism.”

(By the way, there’s a fabulous revival of Albee’s Who’s Afraid of Virginia Wolf? on now at @sohoplace Theatre in London’s West End starring Gillian Anderson and Billy Crudup directed by Marianne Elliott.)

Sofía and Miguel have been married for 16 years, just like Cruz and Bardem. The fictional couple in Bunker seem happy at first, but you sense in Sofía that there’s a growing distance. There’s a brilliant opening scene where Sofía and Miguel are dining with friends and Miguel begins to tell fellow guests the story of how they met. However, the camera’s real focus is on Cruz’s face. She’s just listening and says nothing in that moment. “You can feel everything that she is feeling,” says Zeller, “and only the greatest actress can do that with almost no words to say and to express so many things, so many layers. And that’s what I love about that scene. Her face is like a landscape. You know, looking at her to see the how intense, complex, and rich she can be in this quieter, more contained exploration of who she is — that’s something of a new journey of performance.”

Zeller observes that he and Cruz talked a lot about that opening scene, “and the fact that it was about not what she says but what she keeps for herself, and because in the film we feel that she’s going through a crisis without knowing exactly what it is. In another scene she is in front of this painting. Suddenly, she feels melancholy, but we don’t know exactly why. She’s not happy with her life without knowing exactly why…You know, she has a beautiful house, she has a beautiful husband, she has beautiful children, and still, she is unhappy. She doesn’t have the words to describe why she feels that way. What is this melancholy? So, it’s not until the moment comes when there’s this long confrontation and this long argument with Javier, when suddenly she can articulate her thoughts and her feelings. But until then, and it was the challenge for her as an actress, it was to make us understand everything that she’s going through without being able to express it.” Zeller says that “everything that matters was behind the dialogue” and that Cruz’s “silent intensity” blew him away. Me too.

Zeller continues, “You know, I’ve been lucky enough to work with so many great actors, and I can say that she’s really extraordinary to work with. She’s extraordinary, versatile, but always truthful, always intense. She’s never cheating. You know, every emotion she is exploring is coming from a true place, and I think that’s why she’s so special in this industry now…It’s really rare.”

Of Bardem, Zeller observes that he usually plays “very powerful men, very masculine, frightening sometimes, sometimes really scary, and when I met him, I was touched by the fact that he’s such a sensitive, such a delicate, and I would say, almost such a feminine man. And that’s what I wanted to explore in this film. Not to say that this is his story. Of course, that’s not his story and that’s not her story. But what I was really interested in was to try to still leave room for who they are in this film. But what you feel about this character is at least what I feel about who he is as a man, this very delicate way of seeing the world and trying always to leave room for the other. You know he has such a delicacy towards Penélope, for example, and so I feel like it’s almost them, even though it’s characters.”

It’s true that the fear is at the core of the film, and because I think that it’s one of the feelings that really defines the world we are in at the moment. It all started, as you might know, with an article that I read about this tech billionaire building a bunker.

Zeller ensured that they read the script several times “to be aware of what would be the exposure,” and calls the couple “very brave as artists.” As soon as they made the decision to do it, they expressed no fear “to be seen the way they wouldn’t want to be seen.” Their commitment,” he adds, had to do with “the trust, of course,” but also, “with the feeling of being safe. It’s true on set but it’s true in every professional situation that you are a better version of yourself when you feel safe, and as an actor, you feel safe when you feel loved. So, part of my job was to love them or to make them feel loved, and it was not that hard because I truly loved them,” he says.

I ask Zeller to talk about the film’s gripping 12-minute fight scene between the couple, that took three days to shoot. He noticed that Cruz “was really nervous about it, days and days ahead of it.” And when it came time to shoot, Cruz had to be “very mad,” so Bardem “was saying off-camera things that would make her mad.” He laughs. “Javier knew which buttons to push.” Zeller remembers Bardem telling him one day that there was no need to rehearse the couple arguing because they knew how to do that, and in any case not rehearsing that scene would save the production money, a point that Zeller, as a producer of the film, acknowledged.

In bringing Graham on board as the neighbor, Zeller says he admired the actor’s performance in Netflix hit, Adolescence, but for Bunker he was looking for an actor “that was capable of putting Bardem at risk. It’s not that easy because usually Javier Bardem is the one to threaten everybody else. And for some reason, Stephen Graham has this menacing presence.” But he was also struck by Graham’s ability “to have something so human.” It’s a small but integral part and Zeller was struck by Graham’s humility. Zeller says, “When he read the script, he said, ‘OK, it’s not a big role, but I know exactly what I can do with that. I know exactly why I want to do it.’”

“It’s not a conscious reference, because that play is not clear in my mind,” says Zeller, “but it’s true that the fear is at the core of the film, and because I think that it’s one of the feelings that really defines the world we are in at the moment. It all started, as you might know, with an article that I read about this tech billionaire building a bunker.” When I suggest that Zeller and I must’ve read the same article in the New York Times about Mark Zuckerberg and his bunker, Zeller responds, “Exactly.”

He was, he says, struck by the idea that this man “who made a fortune from the idea of connecting people together was working at the same time on a plan B to isolate himself.” That’s what gave him the impetus to write the film. “It’s such a strange position in life to have to work on something that would mean that maybe soon it’s the end of the world. The more I was thinking about it, the more I realized that, to some degree, we are all tempted to live in a bunker,” he says. He clarifies, “like an invisible bunker to build things to feel protected, without realizing that it’s another kind of prison.”

Zeller worries that we are all adopting the lifestyle “that these billionaires are promoting, it is a world of illusions where you almost believe that you don’t really need the others. You know, like everything could be delivered to you, like food, love, friendship, social interactions, cinema, as if the world out there was not a place to explore anymore. As if we were like isolated islands and not interconnected continents.”

Speaking of “interconnections,” there’s a reconnection of sorts in Bunker. Several years ago, Zeller penned the West End and Broadway play The Height of the Storm which starred Dame Eileen Atkins and theatrical knight Jonathan Pryce. During pre-production on Bunker, Zeller approached Dame Atkins to ask if she’d take a cameo role of a woman who meets Cruz’s character in a powder room and offers her some sage advice. It’s just a moment on screen, but it holds enormous symbolism, that of one great artist passing the baton to another.

Zeller nods in agreement and speaks of his “respect and admiration” for the classically-trained Atkins, now in her 93rd year. The grande dame had told Zeller that she was retired. So, while the interiors were being shot in Madrid to allow Cruz and Bardem to be close to their children, Zeller was so keen to have Atkins in his film that he offered to shoot the scene in London.

What followed was “a very moving moment” because it was the last scene shot in the film, “so it was the wrap for Penelope and for her, and it was really emotional because the scene is very short and it’s basically her saying, ‘You’re so beautiful, and you should enjoy it because you know things are going very, very fast, faster than what you think,’ which is a very simple and common thought. But that’s also the truth. Not only a character saying that to another character, but it was also a woman of a certain age saying that to Penélope, and it was also an older actress that had stopped working saying that to another actress. So, it was so many layers of transmission between two women. I mean, they both cried at the end, and then, it was so moving, Eileen said, ‘Oh, that was my very last apparition on the screen.’”

Sony Pictures Classics opens Bunker in select U.S. cities on December 11 and then nationwide on January 15, 2027.

StudioCanal will release the film in the U.K. and Ireland on February 19.
