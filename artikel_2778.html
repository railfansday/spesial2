Why Is Nikola Jokic Suddenly Struggling So Much To Score Early In The Season?

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Something's been a little off with Nikola Jokic lately, and I don't think we can just ignore it anymore. The Denver Nuggets superstar is still putting up 23.4 points, 9.7 rebounds, and 9.3 assists per game to start the 2026-27 season, which sounds amazing for pretty much anybody else, but I mean, we're talking about a guy who was averaging almost 30 PPG not too long ago.

And it's not just the scoring. Jokic shot 56.9% from the field and 38.0% from three last season, but through seven games, those numbers have dropped to 50.4% and a pretty ugly 21.9% from deep. For someone who's been one of the most efficient offensive players we've ever seen, that's quite a drop, especially when he's actually taking more shots.

The Nuggets aren't doing much better either, sitting at 4-3 and ninth in the Western Conference, with their offense dropping from first in the NBA last season to 11th right now, not exactly what we expected from a team trying to get back into the championship conversation.

Now, I'm not saying Jokic is suddenly washed or anything crazy like that (he literally just dropped a 32-point triple-double against the Toronto Raptors), but I do think there's something worth discussing here, and it goes beyond his three-point shooting.

His left knee injury from last season, the way he's moving around the basket, his shot selection, and even his workload could all be connected, and looking at the numbers, I'm starting to think his struggles didn't actually begin this season.

What makes this whole situation a concerning issue is that he's actually averaging 18.1 field-goal attempts per game compared to 17.4 last year, so we can't blame his scoring drop on some sort of team scheme or him deciding to pass every time he touches the ball.

Jokic is taking more shots, he's just missing a lot more of them, and when we're talking about someone who shot at least 56.6% from the field in each of the previous six seasons, seeing him down at 50.4% is a pretty noticeable difference.

His true shooting percentage is even more concerning, going from an insane 67.0% last season to just 57.9% right now. That's over nine percentage points, and although 57.9% isn't terrible by normal NBA standards, Jokic has never been a normal offensive player.

Remember, this is someone who averaged 29.6 PPG on 57.6% shooting in 2024-25, and during the Nuggets' championship season, he put up 24.5 points while making an absurd 63.2% of his shots. He basically spent years making difficult shots look easy.

Now? Not so much. The season opener against OKC was probably the clearest example, with Jokic finishing with just 13 points on 5-of-19 shooting, including 1-of-7 from three, as the Nuggets lost 96-84.

He bounced back with 35 against the Pelicans, sure, but then came 19 against Golden State, 21 against the Pelicans again, and 18 against the Suns. Nothing too horrible, but definitely not what we're used to seeing from him, and I honestly think the bigger issue isn't the scoring average itself, but how he's missing some of those shots.

Jokic doesn't seem to have that same little touch around the basket, especially when bigger centers are guarding him, and that's where I want to bring up something I've been thinking about.

It's fair to say that Jokic hasn't looked quite the same since injuring his left knee against Miami last December, and the numbers before and after that injury might prove this theory right.

Before getting hurt, Jokic was shooting an absolutely ridiculous 60.5% from the field and 43.5% from three, but after returning, those percentages dropped to 53.3% and 31.9% across his final 33 games of the season. And things got even worse during the playoffs, when he averaged 25.8 PPG while shooting 44.6% from the field and 19.4% from three across six games.

So now we're watching him struggle again to begin this season, and I wonder if that knee injury took away some of the very little explosiveness he had (because Jokic was never a guy who jumped over defenders), but he always had just enough lift to get his shots off.

Some of his hooks look short, his floaters aren't dropping as easily, and when he's going against bigger centers, he seems to have a harder time bullying them into the rim. He's still getting to his spots, but the finishing doesn't look as automatic as it used to.

There's actually a pretty interesting breakdown from Nuggets fan and analyst Max Moacanin, who studied Jokic's shooting before and after the injury, and the numbers back up at least part of what we're seeing.

According to that analysis, Jokic went from shooting 75.1% in the restricted area before the injury to 71.8% afterward, while his efficiency from the paint outside the restricted area dropped from 62.1% to 53.8%.

His above-the-break three-point percentage also dropped from 42.6% to 30.2%, another change that could be related to his legs, especially when you consider how important balance is for a player who shoots with that kind of unusual form.

Okay, let's talk about the most obvious problem, because Jokic has made just 7 of his first 32 three-point attempts this season, shooting 21.9% from deep after making 38.0% last year and 41.7% in 2024-25.

For a player who turned himself into one of the most lethal stretch weapons in basketball, that's a brutal slump. And he hasn't cut back on the attempts, either, still launching 4.6 per game. The volume is identical, but the bucket quality hasn't been there.

To put it in perspective: if he were hitting his usual 38.0% on those 32 attempts, he'd have five more made 3s right now, adding 15 points to his season total. That alone would bump his scoring average up closer to 26.0 PPG, and the whole narrative around his start would look completely different.

Beyond raw points, missing from deep hurts Denver's spacing. When defenders don't have to respect Jokic at the arc, center help can sag into the paint, which clutters driving lanes for Denver's cutting game.

His game logs show the rhythm: 1-of-7 against OKC, 1-of-6 against Golden State, 2-of-7 against Portland. While 21.9% is bound to trend upward, expecting an immediate leap back to 40% might be overly optimistic given the lower percentages he's posted dating back to last season.

My biggest tactical question right now is why Jokic is spending so much time floating away from the low post. According to tracking data cited by ESPN's Ben Golliver, just 16.0% of Jokic's shot attempts have come directly at the rim this season, a career low. Meanwhile, his average shot distance has drifted out to 12.0 feet, tying his career high.

I really don't love that trend, I mean, Jokic built an MVP legacy by destroying defenders out of the mid-post, using footwork, fakes, and counters to collapse defenses. Those short floaters and turnarounds inside 10 feet are what make him unguardable.

Lately, he's spent more time taking perimeter jumpers or acting purely as a handoff hub at the top of the key. While his passing remains elite, Denver's offense is at its absolute peak when he threatens the basket first and passes off the help.

When Jokic gets deep position, defenses face an impossible choice: give up a high-percentage paint look or double and leave shooters open. Without that consistent interior pressure, Denver currently ranks 26th in paint scoring. Coach David Adelman needs to draw up early post touches to establish that inside-out rhythm right out of the gate.

Another factor in Jokic's lower scoring output is a drop in free-throw volume. He's averaging just 4.7 free-throw attempts per game, down from 7.4 last season.

For an 87.9% free-throw shooter, losing nearly three attempts per night really hurts. He's making 4.1 FT per game compared to 6.1 last year, meaning roughly two points a game have vanished strictly from the line. This connects directly back to shot location. Settling for more perimeter jumpers naturally leads to fewer draw-foul situations than banging down low in the post.

His rebounding shows a similar trend. He's down from 12.9 boards per game to 9.7, with offensive rebounds dropping from 3.0 to 2.3. That means fewer putbacks and fewer immediate second-chance opportunities right around the rim.

Denver got wrecked on the glass in their 118-110 loss to Portland, getting out-rebounded 53-37 while giving up 21 offensive boards. Jokic hasn't lost his instincts, but when paint shots, free throws, and offensive rebounds all drop at once, it suggests he isn't banging and crashing with opposing centers as often as he used to in those glass battles.

Here's another thing that annoys me about Denver right now: after all the roster changes and moves to improve the supporting cast, the Nuggets still seem to need Jokic and Jamal Murray to carry almost the entire offense.

Credit to Murray, who's been awesome, averaging 25.4 points and 5.9 assists while torching nets at a 50.9% clip from 3. Christian Braun has also stepped up, averaging 14.6 PPG on 54.9% shooting. But past those two? The production from the rest of the rotation has been pretty quiet.

Aaron Gordon is averaging 12.3 points and 5.3 rebounds. Cam Johnson has been limited to four games and is averaging 9.0 points. DeMar DeRozan is adding 10.7 points off the bench. Denver's bench currently ranks 28th in scoring and 23rd in net rating, while the team sits 28th in total 3-point attempts. They just aren't getting enough consistent perimeter volume or secondary scoring punch.

Adelman noted after the Portland loss that Jokic and Murray are carrying too heavy a load, and the tape backs him up. Jokic dishing 9.3 assists is incredible, but expecting him to create almost every clean look while also scoring 25-plus and anchoring the glass every single night is a tough ask over 82 games.

Getting Johnson integrated as a volume spacer, getting Gordon active on rim cuts, and getting reliable second-unit scoring from DeRozan would go a long way in balancing this offense out.

Jokic is now 31 years old, and while that's obviously not old to start talking about retirement or anything, he's been carrying a ridiculous workload for years. He averaged 36.8 minutes per game in 2024-25, followed by 34.8 last season, and now he's back at 35.9 through seven games.

That's a lot of basketball for a center who runs everything, takes contact inside, rebounds, defends, and has been doing pretty much all the dirty work for the Nuggets since they became contenders.

And after that knee injury last December, I genuinely wonder if his body is starting to feel some of those miles. Now, I'm not saying Jokic is physically declining, because we really don't have enough evidence to make that claim, and he can still dominate whenever he gets going.

But I wouldn't be shocked if some of his reduced aggression inside is related to fatigue, or maybe he's simply trying to save some energy instead of going all-out every possession in November.

Either way, Denver needs to be careful, because asking him to carry another 82-game season at almost 36 minutes per night doesn't sound like the best plan if the goal is to have him healthy for the playoffs.

Look, I'm not buying the idea that Jokic suddenly forgot how to play. A three-time MVP who averaged nearly 30 a night just two seasons ago doesn't just lose his scoring touch overnight.

At the same time, it's naive to completely brush off what we're seeing right now, especially when you trace this efficiency dip back to that knee injury last season. The shooting numbers from deep are ugly, sure, but that's not even what concerns me most.

I'm way more focused on his touch inside: how he's finishing around the basket, how he's handling size in the post, and whether he still has that subtle lower-body lift on his signature hooks and floaters. That's the real story here. I couldn't care less if he gets back to 38% from three.

Honestly, Denver has a much bigger problem on their hands than whether Jokic averages 23.4 points instead of 27.7. The core issue is that they're still asking him to be practically flawless every single night just to pull out wins. For a team with real championship ambitions, that's a dangerous way to operate over an 82-game grind.

I fully expect Jokic's scoring average to climb back toward that 27 or 28 mark as the season unfolds, especially after the way he dismantled Toronto. But one vintage performance doesn't mean we just toss out the physical concerns entirely.

That's what I'm keeping an eye on over the next few weeks. Forget the raw scoring averages and the three-point percentages for a second. The real test is whether Jokic can still create those high-percentage, easy paint looks against true rim protectors without having to fight so much harder for them.

Because if he's genuinely lost that touch around the rim… Denver has a real crisis on its hands, not just a bad week in November.
