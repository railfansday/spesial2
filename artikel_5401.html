Community property vs. equitable distribution states divide divorce assets differently, and this state-by-state guide explains which rule applies where and what it means

A couple with the same house, the same retirement accounts and the same debts can walk out of divorce court with different results depending on where they file. U.S. states do not share one rule for dividing a marital estate. Each state follows one of two systems by default, and that choice shapes everything from who keeps the family home to how a pension gets split.

The first system is community property. In these states, property acquired during the marriage generally belongs to both spouses, and courts generally lean toward dividing those assets 50/50. The second system is equitable distribution. Courts in these states divide marital property in a way they consider fair, and fair does not always mean equal.

Nine states use community property: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington state and Wisconsin. The rules still vary within that group. California sets a firm equal-division requirement in its Family Code. Texas courts divide the community estate in a manner they deem just and right. Washington state courts can reach separate property as well as community property. Louisiana's regime traces back to the civil-law traditions of its French and Spanish past.

The other 41 states and the District of Columbia follow equitable distribution. Judges there typically weigh factors such as the length of the marriage and each spouse's income and earning capacity. Contributions to the household, including homemaking, can count. So can each spouse's age and health. Some states weigh the spouses' conduct as well.

A few states sit between the two systems. Alaska is an equitable distribution state, but spouses there can opt into community property treatment through a written agreement or a trust. Tennessee, Kentucky and Florida have community property trust statutes that let couples opt in through a trust. Those three states still follow equitable distribution.

Community property is a marital property system in which most assets and income acquired during a marriage belong to both spouses together. In a divorce, courts in the nine U.S. states that use the system generally lean toward dividing that shared estate equally.

Those nine states are Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington state and Wisconsin. Every other state follows a different default rule called equitable distribution, and so does D.C.

The central idea is joint ownership from the moment property is acquired. A paycheck one spouse earns during the marriage is treated as belonging to the marriage, not only to the person who earned it. The same logic generally extends to a car, a brokerage account or a house bought with those earnings.

The name on a title or account does not settle the question by itself. Under community property rules, the timing of the acquisition and the source of the money carry more weight than the paperwork. An account opened in one spouse's name and funded with wages earned during the marriage is generally still community property.

That framing shapes how a divorce case unfolds. Both spouses are treated as owners of the community estate from the start. The court's main task is to identify what belongs to the community and then divide it.

An even split is a starting point, not a single national formula. Each of the nine states applies the concept through its own statutes and court practices. Some give judges more room than others to depart from an even division.

Equitable distribution, the system in the remaining states, takes a different route. A judge there weighs each spouse's circumstances to reach a division the court considers fair. That result can leave one spouse with more than half of the marital estate. Community property instead begins from a presumption that both spouses already share ownership.

The system also reaches beyond divorce. Community property classification can affect how property passes when a spouse dies. It can also affect how married couples report income on separate federal tax returns. The Internal Revenue Service publishes a dedicated guide, Publication 555, for taxpayers in community property states.

Separate property is what a spouse owned before the marriage, plus certain assets received individually during it. Marital property is generally what either spouse acquired during the marriage. Courts sort assets into these two groups before they divide anything.

Classification comes first under both systems. A community property state and an equitable distribution state may divide the shared pool differently. Each must still decide what belongs in that pool before splitting it.

The vocabulary shifts with the system. Community property states use "community property" for the shared category and "separate property" for everything else. Equitable distribution states usually speak of "marital property" and either "separate" or "nonmarital" property. The labels differ, but the underlying question is the same.

Timing drives most of the analysis. A house bought, a savings account opened or a car paid off before the wedding typically stays with the spouse who owned it. Wages earned during the marriage are the standard example of shared property, even when only one spouse drew the paycheck. Assets bought with those wages generally follow the same treatment.

Gifts and inheritances are the main exception to the timing rule. Money or property given to one spouse alone, or left to that spouse in a will, is generally treated as separate. That holds even when it arrives in the middle of the marriage. A gift made to both spouses together is a different matter. It usually lands in the shared category.

Title does not settle the question on its own. A bank account or vehicle registered in one spouse's name can still be marital if it was funded with earnings from the marriage. The source of the money tends to matter more than the name on the document.

The spouse claiming that an asset is separate generally carries the burden of showing it. That means producing records that trace the asset back to a separate origin. Account statements, closing documents, wills and gift letters are common examples. Without that paper trail, a court may treat the asset as shared.

The classification usually shapes the outcome. In most states, separate property stays with its owner. The court then divides only the marital or community estate.

Finer points vary by state. These include when the marital period ends and how courts treat income produced by separate property, such as interest or rent.

Separate property can lose its protected status through commingling, which mixes it with marital money, or through transmutation, which changes its legal character. Either process can turn an asset that one spouse owned alone into property a court may divide in a divorce.

Commingling often happens without any deliberate decision. A typical case involves an inheritance deposited into a joint checking account that both spouses use for household bills. Paychecks flow into the same account. Money flows out for groceries, mortgage payments and travel. After enough deposits and withdrawals, it becomes difficult to say which dollars came from the inheritance.

The spouse who claims an asset is separate usually carries the job of proving it. This process is called tracing. It relies on bank statements, closing documents, brokerage records and other paperwork that connect an asset back to its separate source. When the records are incomplete, a court may treat the mixed funds as shared property.

Transmutation, by contrast, often happens through a deliberate act. Adding a spouse's name to the deed of a house that one partner owned before the wedding is a frequent example. Retitling can signal an intent to share the asset. The change can also run the other way, as when spouses agree in writing that a marital asset belongs to one of them. States differ on the formalities required to prove such a change.

Separate property can also take on a partly marital character when marital money or effort adds to its value. Paying down a premarital mortgage with paychecks earned during the marriage is one route. A business owned before the wedding that grows because of a spouse's work during the marriage is another. Courts in many states treat growth from market forces differently from growth produced by a spouse's labor or marital funds.

These problems arise under both major systems. Community property states call the shared pool community property, while equitable distribution states call it marital property. In either case, a court must sort out what changed category before it can divide anything.

Mixing does not always erase separate status. If the trail of documents stays clear, a spouse can often still trace an asset back to its origin. Spouses who want to keep gifts, inheritances or premarital savings separate often hold them in accounts titled in one name. They also keep the records that show where the money came from.

California requires a court ending a marriage to divide the community estate equally. Family Code section 2550 sets that mandate. It gives California the strongest equal-division rule among the nine community property states.

The statute covers two kinds of cases: a dissolution of marriage and a legal separation. In either one, the court must split the spouses' community estate equally. It can make that division in the judgment itself. It can also make it later, if it expressly reserves jurisdiction over the property division.

The equal-split requirement has stated exceptions. Spouses can agree in writing to a different division. They can also reach an oral stipulation in open court. The requirement also gives way where other provisions in the same division of the Family Code direct a different result. Outside those routes, a judge cannot award one spouse a larger share of community property on the ground that it would be fairer.

That limit sets California apart from states that use a fairness standard. In an equitable distribution state, a judge weighs a set of factors and may give one spouse more than half of the marital property. A California judge starts and ends with equal shares of the community estate unless an agreement or a specific statutory exception applies.

Equal division refers to the total value of the community estate, not to each individual item. A court does not have to split every bank account, car or piece of furniture down the middle. It can award the family home to one spouse and other assets of matching value to the other. When the pieces do not balance, one spouse can owe the other an equalizing payment.

The mandate reaches only community property. Assets a spouse owned before the marriage, along with gifts and inheritances received by one spouse, are generally that spouse's separate property. Those assets sit outside the equal split. Before any division happens, a California court has to decide which assets belong to the community estate at all.

The community estate includes liabilities as well as assets. As a result, the equal-division rule applies to the net value of what the marriage built, not just to the property on the asset side of the ledger.

Texas is a community property state, but its courts are not required to split the marital estate exactly in half. A Texas judge divides the community estate in a manner the court deems "just and right." That standard leaves room for an unequal result.

Texas begins where the other community property states begin. Property acquired during the marriage is generally treated as belonging to both spouses. Texas law calls that shared pool the community estate, and it is the pool the just-and-right standard governs.

The wording of the instruction is what sets Texas apart. Some community property states direct courts toward an equal division. Texas law contains no such mandate. Its "just and right" language gives the court discretion over how the community estate is allocated between the spouses.

Property division in Texas generally leans toward a 50/50 outcome. An even split is the usual direction, not a legal requirement. A judge may decide that an equal division would not be just and right in a particular case. In that situation, the court can award one spouse a larger share of the community estate.

That discretion gives Texas a distinct position among the nine community property states. It carries the same community property label as states with fixed equal-division rules. Its standard for the final split, though, rests on a judge's assessment of fairness rather than a set formula. In that respect, its wording sits closer to the fairness language used in equitable distribution states.

For a divorcing couple in Texas, two questions shape the outcome. The first is which assets belong to the community estate at all. The standard reaches only the community estate. Property that remains one spouse's separate property is not part of the pool the court splits. The second question is how the court applies the just-and-right standard to that estate once it has been identified.

Arizona, Nevada and New Mexico are community property states, and divorce courts in all three generally lean toward splitting community assets 50/50. Property that either spouse acquires during the marriage is generally treated as belonging to both of them.

That rule makes classification the central task in a divorce case in these states. The first question is usually not how much each spouse should receive. It is which assets count as community property at all.

Once an asset falls into the community estate, the general expectation is an equal division of its value. The 50/50 lean is a general tendency, not a guarantee for every asset. Each state applies its own statutes and case law, and the details of how a court reaches its result differ from one state to the next.

All three states sit in the Southwest, next to other community property states. Arizona shares borders with California, Nevada and New Mexico, and each of those neighbors follows community property. Nevada also borders Idaho, another community property state. New Mexico borders Texas, which uses community property as well.

The system stops at several of their borders. Utah, which borders both Arizona and Nevada, uses equitable distribution. So does Colorado, which borders New Mexico. Oregon to Nevada's north and Oklahoma to New Mexico's east follow equitable distribution too.

Those borders outline a community property bloc that stretches from the Pacific Northwest to Texas. Arizona, Nevada and New Mexico sit near its middle. Only two of the nine community property states lie outside that western bloc: Louisiana on the Gulf Coast and Wisconsin in the Upper Midwest.

Some couples move from Phoenix, Las Vegas or Albuquerque into Utah, Colorado, Oregon or Oklahoma. If they later file for divorce there, they file in a state whose default rule is equitable distribution, not community property.

Idaho is one of nine community property states, and its divorce courts generally lean toward a 50/50 split of community assets. Property that either spouse acquires during the marriage is generally treated as belonging to both of them.

That rule sets Idaho apart from most of its neighbors. Six states share a border with Idaho. Two of them, Washington state to the west and Nevada to the south, are also community property states. The other four — Oregon, Montana, Wyoming and Utah — use equitable distribution.

The difference shows up at the state line. A couple divorcing in Idaho starts from a community property default. A couple with the same assets divorcing in Oregon or Montana starts from equitable distribution instead.

The two defaults pose different opening questions. An Idaho court generally treats the community estate as belonging to both spouses equally. A court across the line in Utah or Wyoming weighs a set of factors to decide what division is fair.

The equal-split lean applies to community assets, not to everything a spouse owns. Classification comes first in an Idaho divorce. A court has to identify which assets belong to the community before the general 50/50 approach applies. Property a spouse owned before the marriage sits outside that pool.

Within the community property group, Idaho's general lean toward equal division of community assets puts it closest to Arizona, Nevada and New Mexico.

Louisiana divides marital assets under a community property system built on civil law, not English common law. The system grows out of the state's French and Spanish heritage. Louisiana divorce courts generally lean toward a 50/50 split of community assets.

That legal foundation sets Louisiana apart from every other U.S. state. Its private law is organized around a written code, the Louisiana Civil Code, rather than the judge-made common-law tradition inherited from England. The code spells out how spouses own property during the marriage and how that property is divided when the marriage ends.

The code calls the default arrangement the community of acquets and gains, a term for property acquired during the marriage. Under Article 2336 of the Civil Code, each spouse owns a present undivided one-half interest in community property. That shared ownership exists throughout the marriage, not only once a divorce begins.

Separate property stays outside the community. It generally includes what a spouse owned before the wedding, along with inheritances and gifts made to that spouse alone.

Louisiana handles income from separate property in its own way. Under Article 2339, the natural and civil fruits of separate property fall into the community by default. Rent from a house one spouse owned before marrying is one example. A spouse who wants to keep those fruits separate must execute a formal written declaration reserving them.

Couples are not locked into the default regime. Louisiana allows spouses to enter a matrimonial agreement that establishes a separation of property or changes how the community operates. Such an agreement can be signed before the wedding.

When a marriage ends, the community terminates and must be partitioned. Louisiana Revised Statutes 9:2801 governs a judicial partition. It directs the court to value community assets and liabilities, then divide them so each spouse receives property of equal net value.

That standard does not require splitting every item down the middle. The court can allocate an asset, such as the family home, to one spouse and order an equalizing payment of money to the other.

Washington state is a community property state, but its divorce courts divide both community and separate property under a just-and-equitable standard. That power over separate property sets Washington state apart from the stricter community property model, where premarital and inherited assets usually stay with the spouse who owns them.

In most community property states, the first job in a divorce is sorting the marital estate into two piles. Community property, meaning what the couple acquired during the marriage, is divided between the spouses. Separate property generally leaves the marriage with its owner. In Washington state, that sorting still happens, but it does not lock in the outcome.

A Washington state judge can reach into either pile. A house one spouse owned before the wedding keeps its label as separate property. The court can still award part of it to the other spouse if doing so produces a just and equitable result.

Community assets in Washington state generally lean toward an even split, in line with most other community property states. The difference is that an equal division works as a starting tendency rather than a fixed requirement. A judge looking at the full estate can move away from it.

That approach places Washington state between the two systems covered in this guide. It shares the community property framework used in California, Texas and the other community property states. Its flexibility over the full set of assets resembles the discretion that judges hold in equitable distribution states.

The practical effect is that labeling an asset as separate offers less protection in a Washington state divorce than in a state that keeps separate property off the table. A spouse with significant premarital savings cannot assume those funds are automatically shielded from division.

Classification still matters. Knowing which assets are community and which are separate shapes how a court views the estate and what each spouse brings into the division. In Washington state, that label does not decide the result on its own.

Couples who want more certainty about how separate assets will be treated can address the question in a written agreement. Without one, the default standard leaves the final division to the court.

Wisconsin is a community property state whose system rests on the Uniform Marital Property Act, and its divorce courts presume the marital estate will be divided equally. That presumption is a starting point, not a fixed rule. A Wisconsin court can deviate from an even split.

The Uniform Marital Property Act is a model statute drafted by the Uniform Law Commission. The commission writes proposed laws that states can choose to enact. Wisconsin is the only state that has adopted this one.

That origin shapes the vocabulary. Wisconsin law uses the term "marital property" for what other states call community property. The principle underneath is the same. Assets acquired during the marriage generally belong to both spouses, regardless of which one earned or bought them.

Wisconsin also stands apart geographically. It is the only community property state in the Midwest, and the rest of the group sits in the South and West. Its neighbors Illinois, Iowa, Michigan and Minnesota all follow equitable distribution.

The presumption sets the burden in a contested case. An even split of the marital estate is the expected result. A spouse who wants a different outcome has to give the court a reason to depart from it. The judge then decides whether that reason justifies an unequal division.

Wisconsin's divorce statute lists the factors a judge weighs when deciding whether to depart. They include the length of the marriage, the property each spouse brought into it and each spouse's contributions, including homemaking and child care. Age, health and earning capacity also count. So does any written agreement the spouses made before or during the marriage about dividing property.

Some assets fall outside the presumption entirely. Wisconsin generally keeps property that one spouse received as a gift or inheritance out of the division, as long as its character and identity have been preserved. A court can make an exception to prevent hardship. All other property starts from the equal-split presumption.

Alaska lets married couples choose community property treatment rather than applying it by default. Spouses opt in by signing a written community property agreement or by placing assets in a community property trust.

Without either step, Alaska handles a divorce like most of the country. Its default rule is equitable distribution. Under that rule, a judge divides the marital property in the way the court considers fair.

That opt-in structure sets Alaska apart from the nine community property states. In Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington state and Wisconsin, the community property regime applies automatically to property acquired during marriage. Couples in those states do not have to sign anything for it to take effect. In Alaska, the couple has to act first.

The two opt-in tools work in different ways. A written community property agreement is a contract between the spouses that classifies property as community property. A community property trust holds assets as community property inside a trust. Either route requires a deliberate choice by the couple, documented in writing.

The writing requirement draws a firm line. A spoken understanding between spouses, or a habit of pooling paychecks, does not turn Alaska property into community property. Nor does holding an account in both names.

Once property carries the community label, the core community concept applies to it. The assets are treated as belonging to both spouses, not only to the spouse whose name appears on a title or account.

That label matters most at the start of a divorce. Every property case begins by sorting assets into categories before anything is divided. For an Alaska couple that opted in, the agreement or trust shapes that sorting. The signed agreement or trust document becomes a key record in the case. For a couple that never opted in, the state's equitable distribution framework governs the whole process.

Tennessee, Kentucky and Florida let married couples opt into community property treatment by placing assets in a community property trust. All three states still divide property under equitable distribution.

The trust is a voluntary arrangement. A couple must create it and move assets into it. The state's default rule stays the same for couples who skip that step.

That sets these three states apart from the nine community property states. In those states, community property treatment applies by operation of law. Spouses do not have to sign anything for property acquired during the marriage to be treated as shared. In Tennessee, Kentucky and Florida, the shared model exists only inside the trust.

The practical result can be a split within a single marriage. Assets placed in the trust carry community property treatment. Assets held outside it remain under the state's ordinary rules. A couple could hold a brokerage account inside the trust and a house titled outside it. The two assets would then sit under different frameworks.

For property outside the trust, a divorce court in any of these states follows the fair-division approach. The judge looks at the circumstances of the marriage and decides what division is fair. The result can be an equal split, but the law does not require one.

Alaska is the closest comparison. It also lets couples choose community property treatment rather than imposing it. Alaska couples can make that choice through a written agreement or a trust. The opt-in in Tennessee, Kentucky and Florida runs through the trust.

The trust statutes do not move these states onto the list of nine community property states. In a regional breakdown, all three sit among the Southern states that use fair division.

A key question for couples in these states is whether an asset sits inside a community property trust at all. That answer decides which set of rules governs it.

In equitable distribution states, a judge divides marital property by weighing a set of factors rather than applying a fixed 50/50 rule. The aim is a division the court considers fair to both spouses, which can differ from an even split.

Under this approach, the marital estate is not automatically cut down the middle. An equal division is still possible when the court concludes it is fair.

The factors are considered together. No fixed formula assigns a percentage to each one. The final split depends on how they combine in a particular marriage.

The length of the marriage is a standard consideration. A court can treat a brief marriage differently from one that lasted decades, even when the assets involved look similar on paper.

Income and earning capacity come next. A judge looks at what each spouse earns now. The judge also looks at what each spouse can realistically earn after the divorce. A spouse with lower earning power may receive a larger share of the property as a result.

Contributions to the marriage count as well, and they are not limited to paychecks. Homemaking is a recognized contribution in equitable distribution. A spouse who managed the household or raised children can have that work weighed alongside the other spouse's salary.

Age and health also enter the analysis. An older spouse, or one with health problems, may have a harder time becoming self-supporting. Courts can take that into account when deciding who gets what.

States also differ in how they set out these considerations. Pennsylvania writes its factors into statute, giving judges a defined list to work through. Other states give judges broad discretion over which considerations matter most in a given case.

That structural difference shapes what spouses can expect. A written list tells both sides which considerations will be on the table. Broad discretion leaves more room for an individual judge's view of what counts as fair.

Marital fault can shape how property is divided in some equitable distribution states. Mississippi, Alabama and Georgia are among the states where a spouse's conduct may influence the split. The rules differ from state to state. In many other states, judges divide the marital estate without weighing who caused the breakup.

Fault plays two separate roles in divorce law. The first is the legal ground for ending the marriage. The second is the division of assets once the marriage ends. A state can allow divorce without proving fault and still let a judge consider conduct when deciding who receives what. Comparing state rules means keeping those two questions apart.

Conduct usually enters the analysis as one consideration among several. In states that permit it, a judge looks at behavior during the marriage alongside the financial and family circumstances the court already reviews. Conduct does not replace those considerations, and it does not decide the outcome by itself.

Two kinds of conduct tend to come up. Marital misconduct covers behavior such as adultery or cruelty toward a spouse. Economic misconduct covers actions that shrink the pool of shared assets, such as hiding money, running up debt for personal use or spending marital funds on an affair. Courts often call the second category dissipation of assets.

The distinction matters because economic misconduct has a direct effect on what is left to divide. A spouse who drained a joint account before filing has reduced the marital estate. Some courts respond by crediting the wasted amount back to the other spouse during division.

How much weight fault carries in Mississippi, Alabama or Georgia depends on each state's statutes and case law. A judge in any of the three may consider conduct, yet the result turns on the facts of a specific case. The same behavior can lead to different outcomes in different states.

Retirement accounts, the family home and debts all start with the same question in every state: was the asset or obligation acquired during the marriage? The answer decides whether a court treats it as part of the marital estate before any division takes place.

A 401(k) or pension often holds money earned both before and after the wedding. Contributions made during the marriage are generally marital or community property. A balance that already existed on the wedding day generally starts out as separate property. The division applies to the marital share, not automatically to the whole account.

Splitting an employer plan takes an extra legal step. That order directs the plan administrator to assign part of the benefit to the other spouse. An individual retirement account does not need a QDRO. The marital home raises its own classification questions. A house bought during the marriage is generally marital or community property, regardless of whose name appears on the deed. A house one spouse owned before the wedding can begin as separate property. When marital earnings pay down its mortgage, the other spouse may gain a claim to part of the equity.

Courts have several ways to handle the home itself. A judge can order it sold and the proceeds divided. A judge can also award the house to one spouse and give the other spouse assets of offsetting value. Couples can reach either outcome on their own through a settlement.

Debts follow the same logic as assets. Credit card balances, car loans and mortgages taken on during the marriage are generally treated as shared obligations. Debt a spouse brought into the marriage usually stays with that spouse.

A divorce decree binds the two spouses, not their lenders. If both names are on a loan, the creditor can generally still pursue either borrower after a court assigns the debt to one of them. Refinancing a joint mortgage or closing joint credit accounts is therefore a common part of a property settlement.

A prenuptial agreement lets a couple replace their state's default property division rules with terms they choose themselves. In community property and equitable distribution states alike, a valid prenup can decide in advance which assets stay separate and how shared assets get divided.

The default rules apply only when spouses have not agreed otherwise. A prenup is a contract signed before the wedding. A postnuptial agreement does the same job but is signed after the marriage begins. Both work by settling the classification and division of property ahead of any divorce. That leaves a judge less to decide.

In a community property state, a prenup can take a couple out of the shared ownership model. Spouses can agree that each person's salary during the marriage stays separate, for example. They can also agree that a business owned by one spouse, and any growth in its value, remains that spouse's property. Without such terms, earnings and many purchases made during the marriage would fall into the community estate.

In an equitable distribution state, the agreement narrows the judge's discretion. A court would otherwise weigh statutory factors to reach a fair split. With a valid prenup, the court can apply the division the couple already chose. That makes the outcome more predictable for both spouses.

Common prenup terms include a list of each person's premarital assets, rules for how inheritances and gifts will be treated and an allocation of debts. Some agreements address what happens to the marital home or to income from separate investments. Clear terms on mixed accounts can head off the classification disputes that arise when separate and marital money are combined.

Prenups have limits. They generally must be in writing and signed by both parties. Courts commonly examine whether each spouse signed voluntarily and received fair disclosure of the other's finances. An agreement found unconscionable may not be enforced. Terms that set child custody or reduce child support are generally not binding. Courts decide those matters based on the child's best interests. The exact enforceability standards differ from state to state.
