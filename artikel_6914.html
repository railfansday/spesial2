What El Niño Means for Your Lawn and Garden This Year—and How to Protect Your Plants

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Most regions will experience different impacts from this phenomenon.

You may have heard that this year’s El Niño is likely going to have a significant impact on weather in the United States.

“This naturally occurring phenomenon, known as El Niño Southern Oscillation or (ENSO), influences climate in the U.S. and other parts of the world,” says Pam Knox, Ph.D., agricultural climatologist at the University of Georgia. “Some indications say that this could be one of the strongest El Niños on record.”

This pattern occurs roughly every two to seven years, though not on a regular schedule. “El Niño starts in the tropical Pacific Ocean. Changes in ocean temperatures can shift weather patterns around the world,” says Eric Hunt, Ph.D., agricultural meteorologist for the University of Nebraska Extension Service. “This year we’re seeing record warmth, about 10 to 12 degrees above average, in water along the equator. We’ve never seen temperatures this warm in parts of the Pacific.”

Although you’ve probably heard of it referred to as a “Super El Niño,” that’s not a real meteorological term. “Predictions are broken down into categories,” says Madelynn Wuestenberg, agricultural climatology extension specialist for Iowa State University Extension and Outreach. “So, sea surface temperatures .5 degrees above historical averages indicate a weak El Niño, for example. Anything greater than 2 degrees above average is categorized as a very strong El Niño.”

Interestingly, the name of this phenomenon dates back a few centuries to when South American fishermen noticed warmer-than-usual Pacific Ocean waters and decreases in fish catch in December. This led to nicknaming the occurrence “El Niño,” Spanish for “little boy,” because it typically occurred around Christmas.

Here’s what else you should know about El Niño and its potential impact on your lawn and garden.

Experts are clear that while a very strong El Niño can affect temperature and precipitation patterns, nothing is 100 percent guaranteed. “We can say certain conditions are more probable, but we can’t say exactly what will happen,” says Hunt.

Typically, El Nino brings drier, warmer air to the northern parts of the U.S., with fewer clouds and less precipitation. Meanwhile, the Gulf Coast and South may experience wetter, cooler, stormier, and more overcast conditions, says Knox.

A strong El Niño also shifts the track of winter storms. By increasing the total amount of heat and water vapor in the Earth’s atmosphere, and by shifting global storm tracks, El Niño may contribute to significant regional drought and flood events in places such as the Southeast and West, says Hunt.

It’s important to remember that a very strong El Niño doesn’t mean impacts will be more extreme—only more likely, says Knox. This also will vary by region. But being aware of potential risks to your region may help you prepare.

Here’s what the experts say may be issues due to El Niño this winter.

All of these tips are useful for any growing season, but they may be particularly important this year with a very strong El Niño likely to impact weather across the U.S.

Long-range forecasts can help you plan ahead for freezes and other significant weather events, says Wuestenberg. Check eight- to 14-day hazards here, and three- to seven-day hazards here.

Mulch will protect roots from freeze/thaw cycles. This will also conserve moisture and may prevent frost heave that occurs when new or shallowly rooted plants are pushed up out of the ground by temperature fluctuations, says Wuestenberg.

Heavy rains may be problematic if you have areas in your yard where runoff is an issue, says Hunt. That means it’s also a good idea to ensure gutters are clear so water is carried away from the foundation of your house.

Look for branches that could be potential issues, such as any that are overhanging your house, driveway, or walkways.

“It’s recommended to have a certified arborist inspect your trees every two to three years for health reasons because trees are such a significant investment in your landscape,” says Blake Watkins, master arborist and director of plant healthcare at Monster Tree Service. “Arborists also can evaluate a tree’s structure for potential safety issues.”

Evergreens such as boxwood and arborvitae keep their leaves or needles all winter, which means they keep losing moisture, too. Keep watering them until the ground freezes, especially newly planted trees or if you have no rain, says Wuestenberg.

“We still need to do the same things we normally do to keep our lawns healthy,” says Clint Waltz, Ph.D., turfgrass researcher and extension agent at the University of Georgia. “This means following good agronomics, including irrigating and mowing properly, and applying product as needed to help your lawn be better prepared for spring. It’s also helpful to be aware of what the potential impacts on turf could be, such as a wetter-than-normal winter in the Southeast.”

While it’s too late to apply nitrogen to your warm-season grasses, you can add potassium to help with rooting, and phosphorus, if a soil test indicates it is needed. For cool-season grasses, put down an application of nitrogen, keep irrigating if needed, and consider overseeding and applying a fungicide, if needed, says Waltz.

Don’t let fallen leaves sit on your grass all winter. A thick coating of leaves prevents turf from photosynthesizing and could impact turf health in the long run. But don’t cart leaves off to the landfill; compost them or grind them up and use for mulch, says Waltz.

Inspect plants regularly, especially when the growing season kicks off, so you can treat sooner, rather than later. Keep an eye out for signs of pests that overwintered or diseases that are affecting ornamental or edible plants, says Knox.

Drier-than-normal conditions in some regions may mean you’ll need to water young plants more often after planting. In other areas, it may be too wet to plant at the usual time. You may want to use a cold frame or to wait longer than usual to plant if soil temperatures are cooler than normal, says Knox.

“The number one thing to do is to pay attention to forecasts. But take social media with a grain of salt. Many of these dire predictions are made by people who are not trained climatologists,” says Knox. “It’s really about watching how conditions evolve and thinking about how they may impact your garden.”
