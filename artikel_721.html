Brothers Osborne & Cody Johnson Celebrate Teen Rebellion in ‘Born And Raised Hell’: ‘It’s Kind of Our Story as a Town’

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Billboard's Makin' Tracks looks at the writing and recording behind the Brothers Osborne and Cody Johnson collab "Born and Raised Hell."

The new Brothers Osborne single is a big Deale.

Not just because it features a Country Music Association male vocalist of the year winner, though having Cody Johnson as a guest deserves high fives.

But even more than that, for discerning listeners looking for clues in the song’s parade of youthful misdeeds and rabble-rousing, “Born and Raised Hell” contains shards of the Osborne’s upbringing on the Atlantic Coast in Deale, Md. Osborne. “I feel like we’ve spent so many years writing, and trying to write, songs for everybody [else], as one does. That personal detail arrives in the new single, “Born and Raised Hell,” through two aquatic references: swimming out to the jetty and sitting on the bank of Rockhold Creek. The creek is a real place, and Brothers Osborne wouldn’t be quite the same without the experiences they racked up there.

“Where we grew up specifically was where Rockhold Creek opened up into the Chesapeake Bay,” guitarist John Osborne says. “Right at the end of Rockhold Creek is a very long jetty that helps break up the waves and the tide that comes into the creek, because there are a lot of marinas and a lot of businesses [near] the creek. We grew up right there on the end — almost right at the point of it, where that big jetty is. We would just get into all sorts of trouble right there on the on the banks of the Chesapeake.”

When John wrote the new single at his East Nashville studio, he conveyed the desire to address the Osbornes’ homegrown history to co-writers Kendell Marvel (“Right Where I Need to Be,” “Either Way”) and Lee Thomas Miller (“In Color,” “It Ain’t My Fault”). Marvel scrolled through his phone for an appropriate title and found “Born and Raised Hell,” an idea that Miller assumed was already in circulation somewhere. They couldn’t find any competition online; the only other song with that name in the performing rights organizations’ databases appears to be unrecorded.

“It’s so hard to find one of those,” Miller says. “That’s been there this whole time, and no one’s done it. That’s great, you know, and it’s exciting, and then it’s immediately followed up by, ‘Oh no, don’t screw this up. This is pretty unique.’”

John wheeled into a heartland-rock progression on electric guitar, setting an attitude for the work, and they dug into boundary-testing coming-of-age stories familiar to kids in every corner of American culture: drinking, smoking, driving fast and using fake IDs. They weren’t thinking about Brooks & Dunn’s “Red Dirt Road,” but “Raising Hell” took the same tack as that 2003 single, looking back through a nostalgic lens at the stunts they pulled — and managed to survive.

“That’s exactly what ‘Red Dirt Road’ does,” Marvel notes. “You’re talking about your past [as a] young man, and everybody wants to feel that.”

Most of the images in “Raising Hell” apply to small-town life regardless of the location, but John struck a Deale before the first verse ended when he dropped a line about swimming “out to the jetty when the coast was clear.”

“The one word that I know definitively was not mine is ‘jetty,’” Miller quips. “John said it, and I’m thinking, ‘Are we gonna go with that? Because I’m gonna have to ask what that means.’”

It is, he quickly discovered, a protrusion into a body of water designed to protect a harbor from speedy currents. “I learned a word,” Miller says.

The chorus’ set-up line about “broken hearts and guardrails” was unique, too — who writes “guardrails” into a country song? — but it fit the theme in a way that isn’t necessarily confined to the coastline. “It just kind of popped out, you know, thinking of angst and youth and being reckless,” Marvel says. “This is teenage years, you’re chasing girls, you’re sneaking beers, you’re smoking cigarettes, you’re doing things you’re not supposed to do.”

“Our town,” John adds, “once you got away from the bay, had a lot of back roads, and those guardrails got ripped to shreds by a lot of young teenagers — some, unfortunately, ending tragically — but that’s part of life growing up as a as a teenager. It’s the same story in Deale that it is in Anywhere, Small-Town-America.”

He closed the Deale by weaving Rockhold Creek into the bridge — “The whole time we wrote that song, I thought it was Rock Hole,” Marvel says — and John built a demo around the brash guitar tone he used to write “Raising Hell.”

As the brothers sorted through production ideas, they thought it would be ideal to record with producer Jay Joyce (Eric Church, Lainey Wilson), who hadn’t worked with the Osbornes for a while. He had a hole in his calendar the same week they wanted to come in, and they cut “Raising Hell” at Joyce’s Neon Cross Studio, adhering mostly to the sound of John’s demo. The biggest alteration that Joyce suggested came when he had John extend the guitar solo, becoming an epic-sounding section with blistering technique.

“There’s this big section that builds up with a really fast guitar run, which I certainly am good at the guitar, but I can’t just pull those types of things out of my hat,” John says. “I had to sit and really work on it, and I’m glad that we did because it’s become one of my favorite parts of the song.”

Even before they recorded it, T.J. envisioned Johnson as a vocal partner on “Raising Hell.” “I just kept thinking to myself, ‘Man, this register, Cody’s voice would be so good on this song,’” T.J. says. “Plus, I think it matches his and our vibes, like who we are and where we grew up.”

As luck would have it, Johnson reached out to the Osbornes about collaborating on his song “Fool Proof.” They made a Deale — they would help out if he’d consent to singing on “Raising Hell.” Johnson showed up in person to cut his part with some input from the Osbornes. Johnson found a space to let out a long, forceful note, and he took over for John on harmonies in the closing chorus.

“When he hits that ‘broken hearts and guardrails,’ that high harmony, the power behind it just blows your hair back,” T.J. says.

Their team started talking about “Born and Raised Hell” as a single, and when the Osbornes asked broadcasters for feedback, they agreed on that title. Mercury Nashville released it to country radio via PlayMPE on Oct. 22. It ranks No. 59 on Billboard’s Country Airplay chart dated Nov. 21. It feels personal to the brothers, even though the rebellious teen spirit resonates beyond Deale.

“All of this shit went down here that we all did,” T.J. says. “That’s one of the things I feel in the song, and I like about the song: It’s kind of our story as a town.”
