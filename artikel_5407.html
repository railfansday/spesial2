10 takeaways from Stanford's AI Index show AI use racing ahead of its foundations

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index shows fast AI adoption, a narrowing U.S.-China gap and weak safety reporting. See 10 takeaways.

Americans have taken up artificial intelligence faster than their employers, schools and regulators have adjusted to it. People now use AI on their phones and in their personal lives, while its economic payoff, the rules that govern it and the public's trust in it remain unsettled. About half of American adults use AI outside work, according to the Federal Reserve Board. Their contact with it is also turning into a habit. Pew Research Center reports that the share of Americans who interact with AI several times a day rose from 22% in February 2024 to 31% in June 2025. When people adopt a technology this quickly, the questions change. People stop asking whether anyone will use it and start asking what it is actually worth and who is responsible for overseeing it.

Broad use is easy to count, and depth is much harder to see. A tally of users cannot show how many people are only experimenting with AI, how many produce real work with it, or whether safety checks have kept pace with either group. That gap appears wherever people use the technology. Developers report ever-higher test scores for their models, while users still find errors on simple tasks. Investors commit enormous sums before companies can show gains in productivity. Students adopt chatbots for their coursework before their schools decide what is allowed. The useful way to read any AI statistic this year is to ask what supports it: the chips, the people, the rules and the public confidence it depends on.

Stanford HAI set out to summarize the biggest trends in AI's progress, its economy and its effect on society in its 2026 AI Index. Its takeaways cover the narrowing contest between American and Chinese AI models and the classrooms where students use AI more readily than their schools have planned for. They also cover a chip supply that depends on factories in Taiwan and the distance between what experts and ordinary people expect from the technology. Each takeaway reads like a headline. The slides that follow weigh each one against the money, machines, rules and trust behind it. They show where the speed is real and where the base under a number is thinner than the number suggests.

AI's ability to do hard work improved faster this year than any single product launch could show. Within a single year, the developers of several frontier models took them from fixing 60% of the real software problems on the demanding SWE-bench Verified coding test to fixing near 100%, according to Stanford HAI. A difficult skill went from often useful to close to mastered in one cycle, and a group of leading models made that jump together. That coding leap is why capability leads the 2026 AI Index's takeaways, and it shows that the claim rests on results rather than hype. Once a test nears its ceiling this quickly, the more useful question is who is putting these tools to work, and how much.

Businesses have taken up AI, but nobody agrees on how many, and Stanford's count is at the generous end. Stanford HAI puts organizational adoption at 88%. Federal Reserve Board staff economists cite separate estimates of firm adoption that range from about 20% to 78%. Stanford's figure measures something different, organizational adoption, and it sits above even the highest of those firm estimates. When surveys disagree this widely, calling a business an AI user tells a reader little about whether it is testing the tools or depends on them. The headline count says little about how heavily anyone relies on AI.

Capability has moved well ahead of habit. Federal Reserve Board staff economists estimate that only 10% of workers use AI every day on the job, even as top models approach a perfect score on the coding test. For most workers, a powerful tool is still an occasional one. Stanford's figure also describes organizations rather than individuals, which leaves room for a company to qualify as an adopter while most of its staff seldom use AI.

Take the capability gains seriously, but read "spreading" as a measure of breadth, and watch whether daily use at work catches up with what the models can already do.

The best American AI models no longer hold a clear lead over the best Chinese ones. Stanford HAI finds that in March 2026 the top American model, built by Anthropic, held a margin of only 2.7% over the strongest Chinese model. That margin is too thin for anyone choosing among the most capable models to assume that American means better. This is what it means for the gap to have effectively closed: the two countries' best models are separated by inches, not by a generation of technology.

A tie on model scores is not a tie in the machinery behind them. A model's score measures the finished product and says nothing about the computing power, data centers and money it takes to build and run AI at national scale. The U.S. holds an estimated 74% of the world's high-end AI computing power and China an estimated 14%, according to a note by Alex Haag for the Federal Reserve Board. China has matched American models while working from a much smaller base. The American lead has not disappeared. It has moved off the model rankings and into the infrastructure, and that infrastructure decides how widely a country can put AI to use.

Even that infrastructure lead rests on a partial picture of China. China's computing figures are opaque, the Federal Reserve Board reports, so the compute shares are estimates rather than counts. China's true share could be larger or smaller than those estimates suggest.

Raw capacity can also overstate what China is able to use. As much as 80% of the computing capacity China had recently built was standing unused in March 2025, according to figures from Chinese local outlets reported by the Federal Reserve Board. China gains little real strength from machines it has built but does not run.

Readers can accept the closed gap as real at the top of the model rankings and should treat any claim about which country holds more AI power overall as an open question.

The United States holds most of the buildings where AI runs, and no other country comes close. With 5,427 data centers, the U.S. has more than 10 times the number found in any other country, according to Stanford HAI. China ranks second by that count, so even Washington's main AI rival trails far behind. Whatever happens in the contest over models, the U.S. begins the year with a lead in physical capacity that it already has in place.

The chips inside those buildings are a different story, because most of the advanced ones come from a single company outside the U.S. TSMC $TSM is the leading maker of AI chips and produces them in Taiwan. Its AI chip customers include Nvidia $NVDA and AMD $AMD, CNBC reports, which means the best-known American AI chip brands depend on factories in Taiwan. The count of American data centers does not show that dependence.

Pressure on that one supplier is rising. CNBC reports that TSMC spent $40.9 billion on new plants and equipment in 2025 and has guided $52 billion to $56 billion for 2026. Jake Lai, a senior analyst at Counterpoint Research, says AI is behind the rising demand for chips across the server industry. How quickly new AI chips reach the market therefore depends heavily on one company's budget, and its plans and risks affect every buyer of AI computing.

The most direct way to reduce that risk is to make chips closer to American customers, and TSMC has started to do so, but the move is not yet as profitable. CNBC reports that the company recently bought more land in Arizona. "We are going to expand many fabs over there, and this gigafab cluster can help us to improve the productivity, to lower the cost and to serve our customers in the U.S. better," TSMC Chairman and CEO C.C. Wei said. Those lower costs are still a goal, because TSMC expects its plants outside Taiwan to earn diluted margins compared with its plants on the island.

America leads the world in AI buildings, but it is still paying to make the chips that go inside them.

An AI model that can solve problems at the level of the world's best young mathematicians can still fail at a task most schoolchildren master. At the International Mathematical Olympiad, Gemini Deep Think took a gold medal, Stanford HAI reports, while the strongest model at telling time on an analog clock answers correctly just 50.1% of the time. The field's strongest systems can do elite reasoning and still get a simple everyday task wrong about half the time. AI ability does not rise evenly across every kind of work, and one impressive result says little about how a model will handle the next task.

AI has improved quickly at competition math, and the Olympiad gold is the latest result of that steep climb. In competition-level math, AI went from 10% of top human performance to parity between 2021 and 2023, according to the World Bank. Matching top contestants on competition problems is a narrow achievement, however, and it is not the same as matching people's general ability. A high score describes performance on that kind of task alone. It sets no minimum for anything else, including something as ordinary as telling time.

The uneven skill matters most when people let AI carry out tasks for them instead of answering questions. Roughly 1 in 3 attempts by AI agents end in failure, even on structured benchmarks. For anyone relying on an agent, a failure is a routine outcome, not a rare glitch. The sensible approach is to treat an agent's output as a draft to check and to trust a tool only as far as its record on that specific kind of job.

AI has to be judged one task at a time, and its work has to be checked the same way.

People are documenting more cases of AI causing harm, and AI developers have not given the public much more information about safety in response. Stanford HAI reports that documented AI incidents climbed to 362 from 233 in 2024, while developers' reporting on safety stayed uneven. More problems are coming to light, and disclosure about how well models guard against them has not kept pace. That makes the gap in safety reporting a practical problem, because the missing information concerns the same area where harm is growing.

Frontier AI developers choose what to show the public, and they show ability far more readily than safety. Almost all of them publish how their models score on capability benchmarks, so measuring and announcing raw ability has become standard practice at the leading labs. They report much less consistently how their models perform on responsible-AI benchmarks, the tests of whether a model behaves safely. As a result, the public can compare models on what they can do far more easily than on how they behave. The companies publish the results that make a model look powerful much more consistently than the results that show whether people can trust it.

Readers should treat a new model's record scores as half an answer. Its makers chose to report on one question, which is what the model can do. A capability claim stays incomplete until the company reports the safety side next to it, and users, buyers and reviewers have good reason to ask for that side.

Until developers disclose safety as fully as they disclose ability, readers will learn more from a model's headline scores about what it can do than about what it might do wrong.

American investors are putting far more money into AI than investors in any other country. In 2025, private investors in the U.S. committed $285.9 billion to AI, compared with $12.4 billion in China, according to Stanford HAI. Chinese developers have still built models that compete closely with American ones despite that spending gap. In the U.S., the number of researchers and developers who turn money into working products is falling sharply. Since 2017, the flow of AI specialists into the country has fallen 89%, with an 80% drop over the last year. This decline is recent, and it is happening at the same time that American investors are committing large sums.

So far, AI investment appears in the U.S. economy mainly as purchases rather than results. Federal Reserve Board staff economists Paul E. Soto, Mason Thieu and Jeffrey S. Allen find meaningful AI-related contributions to GDP from 2025 into early 2026, with software and computer equipment the largest. Over time, productivity has followed a broadly similar course in sectors with high, medium and low exposure to AI. Companies have bought the hardware and the code, but those purchases have not yet changed how productivity moves in the industries most exposed to AI. For now, the U.S. funding total measures investors' confidence more than any return on it.

Companies can buy hardware quickly, but they cannot quickly replace the researchers who make it pay off, and fewer of those researchers are coming to the U.S. For anyone judging America's AI lead, people are now harder to secure than capital. A large funding total paired with a shrinking talent inflow is a weaker position than the dollar figure suggests.

People took up generative AI faster than they took up any earlier computing technology. Within three years, 53% of people were using it, a pace that neither the personal computer nor the internet matched, according to Stanford HAI. Michael S. Barr, Vice Chair for Supervision at the Federal Reserve Board, cites survey evidence that consumers adopted generative AI faster than the personal computer and the internet. Still, a record pace counts how many people tried the tools, and it says nothing about what they got from them.

Americans use generative AI less widely than people in a number of other countries. The United States ranks 24th in adoption, according to Stanford HAI, while 64% of people in the United Arab Emirates have taken up the technology. The country that builds the most AI does not have the widest use of it. A lead in models and data centers does not guarantee that a country's own people will use the tools most.

People who use generative AI are getting more out of it, but some companies that try it drop it. The median value each user gets from generative AI tripled between 2025 and 2026. Some firms have tried generative AI and then abandoned it, the Federal Reserve Board reports. Those abandoned trials are a reason to discount the adoption record for now, though not a reason to dismiss it. "In the short term, GenAI may be overhyped, while in the long run, it may be underappreciated," Barr said.

The speed record is an early sign of something large, not proof that it has already paid off.

Students have already made AI part of their schoolwork, and the adults responsible for the rules are the least sure what those rules are. Only 6% of teachers say their school's AI policies are clear, according to Stanford HAI, even though more than 80% of U.S. high school and college students use AI for schoolwork. Many teachers have no written rules to work from, because half of middle and high schools have adopted no AI policy. When teachers are unsure, it is often because nobody at their school has written anything down. As long as schools say nothing clearly, students set the norms themselves by using the tool.

For many college students, AI has become a regular way to work through material they find hard. Gallup reports that 64% of U.S. college students turn to AI at least once a week when they need help understanding their classes. A school that treats all AI use as suspect also limits a study habit that many of its students rely on.

Many colleges that do take a stance take one that runs against what their students already do. Some 42% of college students say their school's position is to discourage AI, according to Gallup, at a time when many of their classmates depend on it for help with difficult material. Students who keep using AI despite that position get no guidance on how to use it well. Among students who rarely use AI, ethical concerns are the most common reason they hold back. Those students want to know what is acceptable, and their schools have not told them.

Students have already decided to use AI, and their schools are late in telling them how to use it well.

Control over AI has become a stated goal of national policy rather than only a contest among companies. Stanford HAI reports that national AI strategies and supercomputer funding are growing. Canada's government, for one, named a pillar of its new AI outline "Building the Canadian sovereign AI foundation," according to CBC. Declaring that goal is far easier than building the machines behind it. China had 85 public AI supercomputers in 2025, while Canada's government has only opened a call for applications for its sovereign supercomputing infrastructure. All of South Asia had three supercomputers that year. One country can hold far more of this capacity than an entire region, and a claim of AI sovereignty means very different things depending on which government makes it.

A government can announce a national AI strategy, and have it counted as one, well before the strategy exists in full. CBC reports that Evan Solomon, Canada's AI minister, told a parliamentary committee in February that the strategy would launch that quarter. The government's April 28, 2026, economic update delivered only the strategy's pillars, with no full plan and no launch date. The government's description of its computing effort also refers to the future rather than to machines already running. In a news release, the federal government said the effort would "enable the development of large-scale, Canadian-based compute infrastructure to advance AI research and innovation, while safeguarding Canada’s national interests."

Governments that cannot outbuild the U.S. and China are joining in through partners and shared code. Canada's government made "Building trusted partnerships and global alliances" another pillar of its outline, according to CBC, and it says it will work with allied countries on common standards and joint investment, with democratic values as the base of its AI sector. Solomon has met with officials from Finland and Norway. For Canada, sovereignty means choosing which countries to depend on, not depending on no one. Developers outside the U.S. are also contributing open-source code on GitHub at a level approaching, though not yet matching, U.S. developers.

Computing power does not give a government control over the AI its people already use. In a recent Canadian case, OpenAI notified police only after the killings had taken place, according to CBC. Owning more computers in Canada would not have changed when that company decided to act. Manitoba's government has announced Canada's first ban on youth use of AI chatbots, which sets a limit before the federal government has published its full plan.

A country can have a sovereignty strategy, and even pay for the computers, while private AI companies and provincial governments still decide how its people's AI is run.

The people who know AI best and the people who will live with it longest expect very different futures, and they split most sharply over work. Stanford HAI reports that 73% of experts expect AI to have a positive effect on jobs, compared with 23% of the public. Optimism about work is the majority view among specialists and a minority view among everyone else. Much of that skeptical public works for a living, and its jobs are the ones that would change, so an expert forecast cannot simply override its doubts. This is a split between two groups with different stakes, not one between the informed and the uninformed.

The public's pessimism is aimed at work, not at AI as a whole. Americans are hopeful about AI in medicine, with 44% expecting a positive effect on medical care and far fewer expecting harm, according to Pew Research Center. The same people who welcome AI as patients are wary of it as workers. A single gap between experts and the public hides that difference, and the distrust sits where people earn their paychecks rather than with the technology itself.

Americans' unease about AI has grown over the past several years rather than easing. Pew Research Center finds that 37% of U.S. adults in 2021 felt their worry about the technology outweighed their enthusiasm, a share that had climbed to 50% by June 2025. A rise that steady over four years suggests the public's caution is more than a passing reaction to something new.

Americans also doubt the people who would write AI's rules, with only 31% trusting their own government to regulate the technology. That doubt may help explain why the gap holds. A public that is wary about its jobs and doubtful of its regulators has little reason to defer to optimistic experts.

The divide is likely to narrow only when people believe the rules are being set in their interest, and better arguments from experts won't do that on their own.
