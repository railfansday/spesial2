What a prenup can protect, from a business to an inheritance, and where it stops

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

What a prenup is and the seven things a prenuptial agreement can and can't protect, from premarital property and business stakes to alimony and child custody

Every marriage in the U.S. comes with a default set of financial rules. If two people never sign a prenup, the law of their state supplies the terms. Those default rules govern who keeps the house, who owes the credit card balance and what a surviving spouse can claim from an estate. A prenuptial agreement lets a couple replace some of those rules with terms they choose themselves.

So what is a prenup in practical terms? It is a contract signed before the wedding that sets out how money and property will be handled during the marriage and if it ends. It is also called a premarital agreement. States generally require it to be in writing and signed by both people.

The default rules it replaces are not the same across the U.S. Community property states treat most income and property acquired during the marriage as belonging to both spouses. Equitable distribution states divide marital property according to what a court considers fair. That does not always mean an even split. The same couple could see different results depending on where they divorce. A prenup lets them settle those questions in advance instead of leaving them to a judge.

Its reach has limits. A prenup binds the two people who sign it, not banks, credit card issuers or other lenders. It cannot settle questions that courts reserve for themselves, including custody and child support. Promises about personal conduct generally carry no legal weight. An agreement signed under pressure or without honest financial disclosure can be thrown out entirely.

The line between what a prenup can and cannot do matters for people bringing a home, savings or a business into a marriage. It is just as relevant to people carrying student loans and to parents with children from an earlier relationship.

The slides that follow open with a plain definition of how a prenuptial agreement works. They then cover seven specific areas. Five are protections a prenup can provide, some with conditions attached. The other two are matters that sit outside its reach.

A prenup, short for prenuptial agreement, is a written contract two people sign before they marry. It records how they will treat money and property while married, and how assets and obligations would be handled if the marriage ends through divorce or death.

The writing requirement has deep roots. A centuries-old contract doctrine, the statute of frauds, has long required promises made in consideration of marriage to be put in writing. A verbal understanding reached before the wedding generally carries no weight in court.

The term premarital agreement refers to the same document. It is also the label the uniform model laws use.

Courts reviewing a prenup commonly examine how the agreement was made, not only what it says. Two questions come up often. The first is whether each person signed voluntarily. The second is whether each person gave fair disclosure of their finances, so the other knew what rights they were giving up.

The Uniform Law Commission, a body that drafts model legislation for states to consider, has written two templates for these contracts. Its Uniform Premarital Agreement Act dates to 1983, and a number of states adopted versions of it. Under that model, the agreement takes effect once the couple marries. It also needs no consideration, the exchange of value most contracts require, to be enforceable.

The 1983 act describes a broad range of permitted subjects. A couple can address rights in property and the disposition of property at separation, divorce or death. They can also address the modification or elimination of spousal support, and choose which state's law will govern the agreement. After the wedding, the 1983 model allows the contract to be amended or revoked only through a new written agreement signed by both spouses.

In 2012, the commission issued a newer model, the Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act. It applies to agreements signed before the wedding and to those signed during the marriage, often called postnuptial agreements.

Model acts are templates rather than national law. Each state decides whether to adopt one and how to change it. A clause that holds up in one state may be treated differently in another. The rules for drafting and enforcing a prenup therefore still vary from state to state.

A prenup can keep the assets each person brings into a marriage — a house, a savings account, an investment account — classified as separate property. That status generally keeps those assets out of the pool that gets divided if the marriage ends.

The agreement usually does this by identifying what each person owns at signing. A schedule of assets attached to the contract gives a court a clear record of what existed before the wedding. The same schedule often doubles as part of the financial disclosure that courts look for when deciding whether to enforce a prenup.

Ownership and growth in value are separate questions. A house bought before the wedding may be worth more a decade later. An investment account can keep earning returns throughout the marriage. Without written terms, state law decides whether that increase stays with the original owner or becomes shared.

A prenup can settle the answer in advance. It can keep all of the growth separate, or treat some or all of it as marital. The couple makes that choice, within the limits of their state's law.

Many states distinguish between passive growth, such as market appreciation, and growth driven by a spouse's effort or by marital money. That line can be hard to draw after years of marriage. A clause spelling out the treatment means a court does not have to reconstruct it during a divorce.

Commingling, which means mixing separate assets with joint funds, can blur the line between separate and marital property. Paying the mortgage on a premarital home from joint income is a common example. Depositing proceeds from the sale of a premarital asset into a joint account is another. Adding a spouse's name to the deed of a premarital home can also lead a court to treat the house as marital property.

A prenup can address these situations directly. It can state that a premarital asset stays separate even when marital income pays down its loan. It can also set a reimbursement to the marital estate for those payments. Separate accounts and records still matter after signing. Courts rely on tracing to follow money back to its original source.

A prenuptial agreement can state that a business one spouse owns stays that spouse's separate property, along with any increase in its value during the marriage. That clause can keep the company out of the pool of assets a court divides in a divorce.

Growth in value is a separate question from ownership. A company worth little on the wedding day can be worth far more years later. Without an agreement, courts in many states may treat at least part of that increase as marital property. This is especially likely when the owner's work during the marriage drove the growth. A prenup can settle in advance that the appreciation belongs to the owner.

The clause can also keep the other spouse from claiming an ownership share in the company itself. That limits the effect of a divorce on the business. A settlement that hands an ex-spouse equity, or that forces a sale to split the value, can disrupt operations. It can also affect employees, customers and co-owners.

Co-owners have their own stake in the outcome. Many partnership agreements and shareholder agreements limit who can hold an ownership interest. Some require that shares be offered to the company or the remaining owners before anyone else can receive them. A prenup can be drafted to line up with those documents. The marriage contract and the business contracts then point in the same direction instead of conflicting.

Valuation is another common point of dispute. Business values are often contested in divorce, and each side's appraiser can reach a different number. A prenup can write in the method used to value the company, or the date on which value is measured. That can turn a broad fight over methodology into a narrower calculation.

The agreement can also say what the non-owner spouse receives instead. Some couples set a fixed payment or a share of other assets in exchange for a waiver of any claim on the business. Others leave the company out of the division and split the rest of the marital estate under the default rules of their state.

The same approach can apply whether the owner founded the company, bought into it or holds a stake in a family firm. In each case, the prenup records the starting point and the intended outcome before any dispute arises. How a court treats these terms still depends on the law of the state where the divorce is handled.

A prenup can assign responsibility for debt between spouses. It can state that each person keeps the debts they brought into the marriage, such as student loans or credit card balances. It can also set rules for debts either spouse takes on after the wedding.

The premarital side is the more straightforward part to write. A couple can list each person's existing obligations and confirm that they stay with the person who incurred them. A spouse who enters the marriage without debt can use that language to keep the other's balances from being treated as a shared burden if the marriage ends.

Debt taken on during the marriage needs its own terms. Without an agreement, state law decides how those obligations are divided in a divorce. A prenup can replace that default with the couple's own rules. One option is to assign each debt to the spouse whose name is on the account. Another is to split debts for jointly used purchases on terms the couple sets in advance.

The agreement has a firm limit. A lender or card issuer is not a party to the prenup. The lender can still collect from anyone who signed the loan or card agreement. If both spouses co-signed a mortgage or opened a joint credit card, each remains liable to the creditor regardless of what the prenup says.

Some agreements pair debt terms with an indemnification clause to deal with that gap. Under that kind of clause, a spouse who pays a debt assigned to the other can seek repayment from that spouse. The creditor's rights stay the same. The clause shifts the cost between the couple only after the payment is made.

Marriage itself does not merge credit histories. Each spouse keeps an individual credit report. A balance carried into the marriage stays on the record of the person who opened the account. Joint accounts opened after the wedding appear on both spouses' reports.

Student loans raise a separate practical issue. Some income-driven repayment plans for federal student loans count a spouse's income when the couple files taxes jointly. A prenup does not change how a loan servicer calculates those payments.

A prenup can waive or limit the claim one spouse would otherwise have on the other's estate. It can also keep an inheritance or a family gift classified as separate property. Those terms have firm boundaries. Federal rules for many workplace retirement plans can override what the agreement says. So can the beneficiary forms on individual accounts.

Death ends a marriage just as divorce does, and state law has default rules for that outcome too. In many states, a surviving spouse can claim a portion of the estate even when a will leaves them less. A prenup lets each person give up or reduce that claim in advance.

This kind of waiver is common among people who have children from earlier relationships. A parent entering a second marriage may want a house, savings or investments to pass to those children. Without a waiver, a new spouse's legal claim on the estate could reduce what the children receive.

The agreement can also cover what arrives from family during the marriage. An inheritance from a parent or a gift from a grandparent can be labeled as belonging only to the person who received it. That label carries weight if the marriage ends in divorce, not only at death.

Retirement accounts follow a separate set of rules. The Employee Retirement Income Security Act, known as ERISA, governs many workplace plans, including 401(k)s. Its rules give a spouse rights to certain plan benefits. Giving up those rights requires the spouse's written consent.

A person who signs a prenup before the wedding is not yet a spouse. As a result, a waiver inside a premarital agreement generally does not satisfy that consent requirement. Couples who want those plan assets to go elsewhere typically need a separate consent signed after the marriage.

Beneficiary designations create a second gap. Life insurance policies and many financial accounts pass to whoever is named on the account or policy form. A prenup does not rewrite that form. If an old designation still names someone else, the form generally controls who receives the money until it is changed.

The estate terms in a prenup therefore depend on the rest of a person's estate plan. A will, trust documents and current beneficiary forms are what actually direct assets at death.

Rules on estate waivers, and the formalities needed to make them hold up, differ from state to state.

A prenup can set how much alimony one spouse would pay after a divorce and for how long, or it can waive alimony entirely. These clauses are allowed, but courts in many states examine them more closely than the terms that divide property.

Alimony goes by other names depending on where a couple lives. Some states call it spousal support, and others use the term spousal maintenance. The idea is the same in each case. One former spouse makes payments to the other after the marriage ends.

A support clause can take several forms. It might fix a single payment amount. It might set a monthly figure that lasts for a defined number of years. It might tie the length of payments to how long the marriage lasted. It might also rule out support altogether. In that case, each spouse agrees to leave the marriage without a claim on the other's future income.

The extra scrutiny comes from timing. A couple signs a prenup before the wedding, but a court reviews a support term when the marriage ends. That can be decades later. In some states, a court can refuse to enforce a support term that is unfair at the time of divorce. That can happen even if the term looked reasonable on the day it was signed. A waiver signed by two people with similar careers can look different after one spouse leaves paid work to raise children or care for a relative.

The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act, the model law the Uniform Law Commission drafted in 1983, includes a specific safeguard on this point. It targets clauses that reduce or eliminate spousal support. If such a clause would leave one spouse eligible for public assistance at separation or divorce, a court may order the other spouse to pay support. That support covers only what is needed to avoid the eligibility.

State law differs on these questions. Some states have adopted versions of the uniform act, and others apply their own standards to support waivers. A support term that holds up in one state may fail in another. Many prenups include a severability provision. It is intended to keep the remaining terms in effect if a court strikes one clause.

A prenup cannot settle child custody, parenting time or child support. Those decisions stay with a court, which applies a best-interests-of-the-child standard when the question comes up.

The reasoning starts with who holds the right. Child support exists for the benefit of the child, not the parents. Two adults planning a wedding cannot sign away money that a future child would be owed. They also cannot agree in advance to pay less than the law would otherwise require.

Custody follows the same logic. A judge looks at a family's circumstances as they exist when the parents separate or divorce, not as the couple imagined them before the wedding. A child may not even be born when the agreement is signed. The parents' jobs, homes and health can change in the years between.

Each state sets its own factors for weighing a child's best interests. Every state also uses child support guidelines to calculate payment amounts. Those guidelines apply regardless of what a premarital agreement says about children.

The court's authority continues after a divorce, too. Custody and support orders can be revisited when circumstances change in a significant way, such as a parent's move or a shift in income. A clause written years earlier cannot lock those outcomes in place.

A provision that tries to fix custody arrangements or cap child support is generally unenforceable. The risk can extend beyond that single clause. A judge reviewing an agreement that overreaches on children's issues may look harder at the remaining terms. That scrutiny can reach property provisions that would otherwise hold up.

Parents still have a way to shape these outcomes, just not years ahead of time. Divorcing parents can negotiate a parenting plan and a support arrangement when they separate. A court then reviews that agreement against the best-interests standard before approving it.

The prenup itself remains a tool for the two adults' own finances. It can set out how they divide property and debts between themselves. Questions about their children are left for the time a family faces them, and for a court's review.

A prenup cannot make personal or lifestyle promises legally binding. A court can also refuse to enforce an agreement that was signed under coercion, without meaningful financial disclosure or on unconscionable terms.

Some couples write in who handles the chores, how often they visit in-laws, a limit on a spouse's weight or expectations about sex. Terms like these are often called lifestyle clauses. Courts generally decline to enforce them. They deal with private conduct inside a marriage rather than with money or property.

A non-financial term can still sit in a signed document. It simply carries no practical legal force. A couple who wants a written record of household expectations cannot turn it into an obligation that a judge will order someone to follow.

The way a prenup was signed can also decide whether it survives. An agreement can fail if one person was coerced into signing it. A contract built on pressure does not reflect a voluntary choice. Courts commonly look for voluntary signing when deciding whether a premarital agreement holds up.

Timing is part of that analysis in some states. Courts there weigh whether one person received the agreement at the last minute, such as shortly before the wedding, with little room to read it or push back. Some states also consider whether each person had independent legal advice. In those states, a spouse who signed without a lawyer of their own may have a stronger basis to challenge the terms.

Disclosure is a separate ground. A prenup asks each person to give up rights they would otherwise have under state law. If one person hid assets, income or debts, the other could not know what they were giving up. A lack of meaningful financial disclosure can sink the agreement.

Unconscionability covers terms so one-sided that a court will not enforce them. How courts apply that standard differs from state to state.

A prenup also cannot authorize illegal conduct. No private contract between spouses can make an unlawful act permissible.
