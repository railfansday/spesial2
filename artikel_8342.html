Dreaded Message Received — John Muir Trail Day 4

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

After summiting Mount Whitney I went to sleep early, and for the first stretch I slept soundly and deeply.  So soundly, in fact, that when I woke my bladder was already near bursting and I had to run out to find a tree to pee against.  Sometimes when it is cold, or raining, you are tempted to wait a while longer to venture out.  Not this time!

Later during the night I left the tent three more times with a full bladder.  Maybe it was a response to the day at altitude?  Unfortunately, the good deep sleep came only during the first stretch.  After that it was interrupted with incessant severe coughing and blowing my nose.  I kept hoping to get over this thing and it kept hanging on.

I got up fairly late in the morning; it was already after seven o’clock.  This did not worry me as we had a relatively short day planned.  I went to the stream to rinse out my shorts and to wash my sock liners.  I pack two pairs of liners to use on alternate days, and I try to wash the “dirty” pair each day while I wear the other one.  Of course, washing in cold water with no soap does not work perfectly, but it helps.

On my way back from the creek, Sam accosted me.  “George, we gotta play some frisbee.”

I agreed and we started tossing it back-and-forth.  Once again, I was not delivering very good passes, but what started to worry me more was my breath.   I was not running downfield routes for the disc; we were just standing there calmly throwing back-and-forth.  Yet after maybe 15-20 throws I realized I was getting out of breath.  Really sucking air, but why?  Sure, we were camped at over 10,000 feet elevation, but that should not have been affecting me so much by Day Four.  I quit and went to get my Garmin.

My Garmin inReach looks huge, but it is barely longer than my middle finger

The Garmin InReach is a handy communications device in the wilderness.  For an emergency, it has an SOS function designed to bring the search-and-rescue folks running.  For everyday use, it allows text communication by satellite where there is no phone signal.  That is the case on much of the John Muir Trail.  Just be prepared to spend some real time painstakingly typing in one letter at a time!  What is more, under heavy overcast skies the satellite connection is limited, and that had prevented me from notifying my wife the night before that we had summited Mt. Whitney.

Now in the morning I sent out the word, and I got a dreaded message in reply.

Let me go back a year.  In 2025, during my hike of the Long Trail in Vermont, my son Ben developed an appendicitis that turned out to be colon cancer.  I wrote about this in last year’s blog on The Trek.  After surgery, he went through a dozen rounds of chemotherapy, after which a scan showed him to be cancer free.  In celebration he and his wife went to Greece and my wife Lee and I went to Vermont to take care of our granddaughters while they were gone.  Just a few weeks after our return, and before I headed to the John Muir Trail, they found some nodules on his follow-up scan.  They said these might just be scar tissue from his surgery and we ardently hoped they were only that.

But I was skeptical.  Why would this scar tissue, from a year-old surgery, not have shown up on the earlier scan, but only showed up now?  The plan was to have another scan in 4-6 weeks to see what it looked like.  That would have come close to the end of my John Muir Trail hike (if I were successful).  However, Ben’s blood markers were highly concerning and, unbeknownst to me, they moved up his scan date.  Now my wife sent me the bad news through the Garmin.  The scan showed that the cancer had returned with a vengeance.  Ben now had Stage Four colon cancer.  It felt like a gut punch to me; no doubt worse for him.

Moments later, Lee texted me again.  She was going to the Emergency Room with our friend and neighbor Susan.  She didn’t say why or even whether she was taking Susan for treatment or Susan was taking her.  Shortly before leaving on the trail Lee had her appendix out.  When it was getting time for me to leave, she assured me that she was much, much better and she urged me to go.  Apparently, that was not entirely true, and she was in a lot of pain in her incision.  It was made worse by her coughing, as it seems I had generously shared my own illness with her before I left.

I texted her asking whether I should leave the trail and she replied, “No, not yet.”  I was not sure whether she realized that my offer to leave the trail – given where I was located – was intended for several days in the future.  A swift departure from where I stood then would have required a helicopter.  I decided not to clarify that, although – with my breathing difficulties – I wondered whether I might actually need the chopper myself before long.

Yes, it was an awful morning between my son’s cancer, my wife in the Emergency Room, and my own health issues.  I was tempted to return to my tent and launch headfirst back into my sleeping bag and not come out.  Instead, I made breakfast, used the campsite’s well-designed three-sided privy, and packed up.

As we set off, we followed the same route as we had in the dark the night before.  First, over the stream on a pair of logs and then up a couple of small slopes to the John Muir Trail.  This time, when we got there, we would turn left, northbound, instead of right toward Mount Whitney.

The two small slopes were nothing really.  But they left me gasping and wheezing.  The same person who had ascended over 4,000 feet just the day before was now struggling with less than 100 feet.  I truly worried how I would make it through the day.

I was thinking I needed to get off trail with Sam at Onion Valley, but that felt impossibly distant.  Could I make it over Forrester Pass?  Could I even make it to our intended campsite tonight at Tyndall Creek?

Luckily the first part of the trail was gently sloped.  What I gradually discovered was that if it was level or downhill I could handle it pretty well.  The uphills by contrast were killing me, having to catch my breath gasping for air, and dealing with this perpetual cough and blowing my nose.  This is disgusting, I know, but I realized that my handkerchief could not hold all the mucus I was producing.  So, after blowing my nose, I found a stone or stick to wipe it out of my filthy rag and to fling it away from the trail.

As we continued with the mild gradient, I started feeling a bit stronger.  That was certainly a relief.  Some of the trees by the trail had amazing textures as well, and the scenery around me distracted me.

Amazing close-up showing the texture of some exposed tree-trunk.

The improvement continued until I felt almost normal  when we stopped  for a midday snack at a river crossing and filled our water containers.  At that point the weather was ominous with a lot of big thunderheads rumbling and rain threatening.  I regretted that our late start might have pushed us into the bad weather window.   We were both anticipating having to wear our rain gear.  At that river crossing I took my rain poncho out of my backpack and put it in my front left pocket.  It was bulky and a little annoying there, but it was the best I could do to have it accessible if or when the skies opened up.

Stark bare tree reaching into the sky (and note the thunderclouds behind it!)

The bulky pocket turned out to be a good rain prevention device, because the rest of the day we only got brief sprinkles despite the ominous clouds all around.  Beyond the creek the path turned uphill in a way that would have crushed me that morning.  Now my stronger legs propelled me upward, slowly but without breaking my spirit.  The beautiful countryside inspired me as well.  Eventually we emerged from the forest into an area of open views called the Bighorn Plateau.  There we could see the mountains encircling us and in the grasses were ravens foraging for I-know-not-what.

From the plateau the trail went downhill to our planned campsite at Tyndall Creek.  Our intention was to camp on the far north side of the cluster of tent sites scattered along the creek to be closer to Forrester Pass the next morning.  But folks already camped told us the far side was crowded and possibly already filled.

Therefore, we took  the first pleasant spot with two tent sites available and made our camp.  Nearby we met another camper named Daniel.  He was a friendly fellow and we chatted.  He told us he was a 35-year-old pharmacist living in Portland.  His father Norm, and his father’s friend Brian, were hiking SOBO and would be meeting up with him if they could get over Forrester Pass the next day.  He told us that Brian was Chinese and nursing a knee injury, so that we might identify the two older hikers if we encountered them the next day.  We could tell them that Daniel was waiting for them.

After a nice dinner with Brian, I recited Edgar Allen Poe’s poem “The Raven” which confirmed just how much my breath had improved over the course of the day.  Sam actually filmed me doing it and, other than one coughing fit and one memory lapse, I gave a respectable performance.

Then it was time for the nightly ablutions.  I even dug my cathole to be ready after breakfast in the morning.  At 8:30 p.m. I set my alarm for 4:30 a.m., giving me exactly eight hours of sleep if I could manage it.

In my tent, nestled in my sleeping bag, I thought of Ben and his battle with cancer, and Lee dealing with that and with her own health issues.   While on the trail there is too much focus on the right here and right now, one step in front of the other, to think much of those things.  But once settled down they seep back into consciousness.  The stormy overcast of the day had matched my mood, and I felt sorrowful as I dozed off.
