Australia’s Hannah Nielsen on her comeback, leading by example and her love for the game at 38 years young

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Growing up in Adelaide, Australia, Hannah Nielsen played "every sport under the sun" besides lacrosse until she was 13 years old. At a "try day" that her tennis doubles partner at the time told her about, Nielsen got her first opportunity to fall in love with lacrosse.

Most Australian athletes would have to be born into a lacrosse family or get introduced to the sport by someone to even know it existed. "Try day" became Nielsen's in.

It was essentially a casual event that the local city organizations hosted to allow young athletes to pick up a lacrosse stick for the first time with no pressure or strings attached. Curious to try something new, Nielsen ended up quickly admiring how unique, free-flowing and physical the game was compared to other sports she played.

From then on, she was hooked – and one of the most decorated careers in international lacrosse history was born.

Twenty-five years later, as Nielsen prepares to take the field for Australia at the Lexus Global Lacrosse Games in December, she remembers the teenage version of herself who went from discovering the sport at 13 to playing for her national team at 17. That young girl played with passion, grit, and strove to put her best effort on the field every chance she got.

Playing for Brighton Lacrosse Club throughout her developmental years, Nielsen, now 38, describes her suburb's club team as a "fun but competitive family atmosphere" where kids start as young as 8U and teams exist for players well into their 50s.

Surrounded by that fun-hearted environment, it was hard for Nielsen not to fall in love with lacrosse. And someone who helped turn that initial interest into true development was her coach, Trish Adams.

"Trish was the one who was most instrumental in my career," Nielsen said. "She taught me how to catch and throw. She would sit us down and teach us the rules. She would give us quizzes on IQ situations. She coached me at the state, club and Australian levels. She did so much for me and really looked out for me."

Adams is the older sister of women's lacrosse legend Jen Adams. Nielsen belonged to the same club as the Adams sisters and other lacrosse stars like Sonia LaMonica and Courtney Hobbs, so she was graced with proximate inspiration for dreaming about her future in lacrosse. The combination of idolizing these local legends who went on to play at the University of Maryland and watching a few Terps highlight videos as a teenager set Nielsen's sights on making it to the United States and playing collegiately for a top-tier program.

But how did she put herself on recruiters' radars? By making the senior national team at a mere 17 years old in 2005.

"When you're 17 playing with your heroes, your idols, like Jen Adams, Sonia LaMonica, Sarah Forbes, Courtney Hobbs – they don't treat you like you're 17, nor do the coaches," Nielsen said. "That 2005 World Cup was a pivotal moment for me and my journey. That was a platform for me to have a successful college experience. That experience allowed me a platform to go into college without being timid and with confidence as a freshman."

Nielsen often thinks back to that World Championship, but not only because she was living a dream playing alongside her idols. Australia defeated a dominant United States team on U.S. soil to win the gold medal for just the second time in its history ('86, '05). Since 1982, Australia has earned four silver medals and three bronze medals, but the No. 1 spot has evaded them ever since '05.

After her successful senior team international debut, Nielsen's college interest came flooding in. She ultimately decided to pave her own pathway and attend Northwestern University.

Needless to say, she chose the right path, and she had the confidence to make an immediate impact. She went on to win four national championships with the Wildcats and took home two Tewaaraton Awards ('08, '09) while setting multiple NCAA and program records. Nielsen remains one of the greatest players ever to pick up a stick, and she's only gotten better.

"Who I am to the core is who I was as a kid back in Australia," she said. "I'm not the flashiest. I'm gritty. I'm tough. But also my IQ has grown and grown. The evolution of becoming more confident with my stick, the knowledge of the sport and being able to lead offenses has helped me grow as a player."

After graduating from Northwestern, Nielsen coached at a handful of highly touted programs – Penn State, Towson, Colorado and Northwestern – before eventually landing the head coach position at Michigan in 2017. This upcoming spring will mark her 10th season leading the Big Ten standouts. All the while, she's continued to star for Australia's national team.

Nielsen attributes her growth as a player to her daily immersion in the coaching side of the game.

"I like to think now that what I lack in speed and youth, I make up for in experience and knowledge," she said. "I watch and learn from others constantly. I don't think I'd still be doing this if I wasn't in the coaching profession. It's a big reason why I've still been able to play."

Nielsen hasn't just continued to play. She has played in five World Championships with her national team – a feat that no other modern-day women's lacrosse player has accomplished. So when she went down with an injury at a national team training camp in December 2025, she was especially bummed to learn it would cause her to miss out on her sixth World Championship in Tokyo this past summer.

"I don't scream, I don't cry, I don't yell. But I did all three," she admitted. "I completely ripped all three tendons of my hamstring off the bone."

Nielsen had surgery the first week of January and was back coaching her Michigan team at practice on crutches that same week. Due to the nature of her job, she was able to experience first-class rehab and recovery, for which she is very grateful. Nielsen has always prided herself on being go-go-go, with a "work harder than anyone" mindset.

This injury was a true test of her patience, forcing her to tweak her training as she fought daily to get healthy over the course of the past year. The 2028 Olympics was a realistic goal that kept her motivated as she battled through the tougher moments of her recovery.

"I wouldn't change it," Nielsen said. "It's made my body stronger than it would've been. I've learned a lot about injuries and having more empathy towards them. My body feels great now, and I'm getting ready to play with the team in December."

When asked about how she finds the motivation to keep going after so many successful years on the field, she answered in typical Hannah Nielsen fashion:

"I just love it. People tell you that you'll know when you're done, but I just haven't felt that. Every little piece of it, I love. Putting on cleats, waking up feeling sore, going to Australia events and having that camaraderie in the locker room. Being an athlete is such a special thing, and being on a team is one of the best things you can do. Those experiences are what keep me going."

Nielsen's passion for the game and the pride of playing for her country will be on display next month. She was one of 13 players selected to the Australian roster for the Global Lacrosse Games, which will take place Dec. 8-13 outside Washington, D.C.

Other notable names include Georgia Latch and Miriam Suares-Jury, two athletes Nielsen loves to play alongside, saying they "keep her young." Marlee Wood brings a helpful veteran presence to Nielsen's side, making an inspiring comeback after having her two children.

It's a group worth coming back for. In addition to her individual motivation to get back on the field after injury, Nielsen is ultimately excited to showcase Australian lacrosse at its best.

"The Aussie spirit is tough, resilient, no frills," she said. "Toughness always comes to mind. That's just our DNA. We're not afraid to take hits and give hits; we're a hard-nose group. We're prideful, too. Everyone is super eager to get Australia back on the podium."

Australia has not won a gold medal at an international event since Nielsen's first World Championship in 2005. It will battle the U.S., Canada and the Haudenosaunee for a spot on the inaugural Global Lacrosse Games podium.

"When I go to these events, it's full circle," Nielsen said. "It's interesting being the oldest person now, having started my international career as the youngest. I try to instill what I learned from my older teammates back then into my younger teammates now. Truthfully, I'm just happy to be healthy and putting a jersey back on."

And truthfully, so are lacrosse fans everywhere.
