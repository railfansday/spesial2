Australia’s ‘problematic’ surrogacy laws could be transformed under proposed changes

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Law reform body recommends ongoing ban on commercial surrogacy, but greater compensation for surrogates, and safeguards around who the child’s legal parents are

Surrogates would be entitled to full compensation, with a nationally consistent framework ensuring domestic surrogacy is a viable option to parenthood, under proposed changes by Australia’s law reform body.

The Australian Law Reform Commission has been investigating how to better regulate surrogacy and harmonise laws so they are consistent with international legal obligations, while protecting human rights – particularly the rights and best interests of the child.

On Tuesday evening, it tabled its 47 recommendations, with the aim of making the process easier to access while ensuring the safety of all parties. These include licensed surrogacy support organisations to help intended parents connect with surrogates, greater compensation for surrogates and greater safeguards around who the child’s legal parents are.

“Good surrogacy laws should do two things: enable people to form families through surrogacy where it can be undertaken responsibly; and provide effective safeguards against exploitation and harm. Our current laws do not achieve either aim,” the ALRC’s president, Justice Mordy Bromberg, said.

“Instead, they drive Australians to pursue overseas surrogacy arrangements, which can expose children, surrogates and intended parents to great risk of harm and exploitation.”

The review found that inconsistent surrogacy laws and criminal sanctions are driving parents overseas and arrangements underground, making them “less visible, increasing rather than reducing the risk of harm”.

While the exact number of surrogacy births is hard to determine, estimates suggest that domestic gestational births more than tripled over the recent decade, from 27 births in 2014 to 84 births in 2023, the report found.

Overseas surrogacy also increased substantially over a similar period, from 246 in 2015 to 375 births in 2024, an increase of more than 40% – suggesting surrogacy is becoming a more significant pathway to family formation because it has occurred while the overall birthrate has fallen, the report said.

The report recommended that while a ban on commercial surrogacy should stay in place, surrogates should receive greater compensation to cover medical costs, loss of earnings including superannuation, birth support and other costs incurred by the pregnancy.

The report found the “problematic nature of Australian surrogacy laws” is a major driver of intended parents going overseas, where the risk of harm and exploitation is at its highest.

ALRC’s principal legal officer, Jacinth Pathmanathan, said the report calls for lawful, well-regulated overseas surrogacy.

“Under the recommended framework, intended parents would need to take several steps before entering an overseas arrangement,” Pathmanathan said.

“They would have to register, receive official guidance and obtain legal advice in both Australia and the overseas country. They would also need to take reasonable steps to avoid arrangements that are unlawful in the overseas country, don’t meet minimum Australian requirements or that pose significant risk. Failing to meet these obligations could result in a civil penalty.”

The Australian Law Reform Commission’s report and its recommendations are now with the commonwealth attorney general for consideration.
