‘Percy Jackson’ Officially Enters a New Era After That Brutal Death

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The ‘Percy Jackson’ showrunners preview what’s to come in the final four episodes.

Editor's Note: This interview contains spoilers for Percy Jackson and the Olympians Season 3, Episode 4.

Percy Jackson has entered a new era, and the stakes couldn't be higher. Not only has Percy Jackson and the Olympians become a smash hit for Disney+, Season 3 marks the first time the third Percy Jackson book, The Titan's Curse, has been adapted. Book fans and even new fans, thanks to the fantasy show streaming on Netflix, have been eagerly awaiting this. The pressure was on for the showrunners — Dan Shotz, Craig Silverstein, and Jon Steinberg — to bring this pivotal chapter in Percy's story to life. Would they achieve greatness or suffer the wrath of the gods (or, more pressing, the fanbase)? It’s a tall order to step up the series in a major way, and so far, the showrunners have succeeded.

Season 3 puts Percy Jackson (Walker Scobell) and his allies on shaky ground after Annabeth Chase (Leah Sava Jeffries) is taken captive by Kronos' forces. As Percy, Thalia Grace (Tamara Smart), and Grover Underwood (Aryan Simhadri) work with newcomers, Zoë Nightshade (Saara Chaudry) and Bianca di Angelo (Olive Abercrombie), to bring her back, their quest will bring these demigods face-to-face with an undead army, the Nemean Lion, and a 115-foot mechanical giant. Regarding that last obstacle, "We Visit the Junkyard of the Gods" ends with Percy and fellow quest mates victorious over Talos, but it comes with a heavy price — Bianca dies after defeating it. This is a key scene in the book; her death is not only traumatic for the quest mates, but it will ripple out and affect the cosmos forever.

Ahead of this pivotal episode’s release, the showrunners broke down the ramifications of Bianca's death. Shotz and Silverstein also share the key character dynamics at play this season and what facets they get to explore in this adaptation, including Luke Castellan (Charlie Bushnell). The showrunners peel back the curtain on the junkyard sequence, as well as preview the epic fight choreography coming in the latter half of the season. Plus, Steinberg addresses his interest in exploring Percy's parents, Sally Jackson (Virginia Kull) and Poseidon (Toby Stephens), before the series concludes.

COLLIDER: You've hit this new milestone with Percy Jackson officially being the first to adapt The Titan's Curse. What pressure, if any, did you feel in adapting this pivotal chapter in the story?

CRAIG SILVERSTEIN: The pressure to get as much of it in as possible, knowing that we can't get every single moment in, and to hit what we think from our reading of the books are the big moments that people have been waiting a couple of decades to see.

How did you determine which scenes stayed versus which ones were open to be fleshed out and altered for this season?

SILVERSTEIN: Some of it's about really looking beneath the hood at the moments and seeing how these change the story, and do they change the characters? Or is it just something that gets you from here to there? Because if it's something that just gets you from here to there, then that one is first up to go to the back of the line or turn to something else. Like, the boar. I have seen zero comments of, "They didn't do the boar!" Zero. Now I'm going to see a million.

You say that, and I'm sure there will be a handful of people like, "But where is the boar?"

I think there are so many fascinating character dynamics at play this season, with Percy and Talia being foils to each other, and then you've got Percy and Nico (Levi Chrisopulos). For each of you, which character relationship has been the juiciest to explore this season?

SHOTZ: I think the Annabeth-Luke one just because it is not in the book; we don't get to see that point of view. Annabeth and Luke are such key members of this ensemble that, in the series, we want to honor these great characters. Getting to look outside the book and think about what is going on with them at the same time that our quest mates are heading out to go find her, it was awesome to see what was happening with her up in Mount Orthys and to see where she is now with Luke and what she has to face to get back to her friends. I think getting to explore that was just an open canvas of how to tell that story, and so those are the opportunities and adaptations that just make it really rewarding.

SILVERSTEIN: For me, it was Percy and Thalia because of just that alpha energy of both of them. Both are used to being the de facto leaders. It comes to them a little bit differently; Thalia maybe more than Percy, right? He's sometimes happy to rely on someone else, but now he's finally grown into a place where he is starting to accept the mantle of responsibility that's been dumped on his shoulders with the Great Prophecy. And then she's somebody who's used to leading, but doesn't want to have anything to do with this Great Prophecy. It's frustrating for him also, because that's exactly how he was last season. All those things that spark off each other were fresh and new, and it's exciting to dive into.

To get back to Luke and Annabeth, I do think, despite seeing how far he's willing to go for Kronos, he undeniably cares about Annabeth. What can you tease about this tension with him, and how it will manifest for the rest of the season?

SILVERSTEIN: Luke's carrying a lot. Everyone's carrying a lot of weight on their shoulders this season. In a way, this is Luke's last gasp of that side of him that cares, as he's struggling to be loyal to his friends, the only family he had, and Kronos, who he's convinced himself is sort of his new family. That reaches a breaking point. It has to, and it comes in the season.

It's also going to create some tension between Percy and Annabeth when they finally reunite, because Annabeth is seeing Luke from this side right now, and the last time Percy saw him, Luke was trying to murder him.

SILVERSTEIN: Yeah. Percy always has trouble seeing that good side that he keeps hearing about.

How would you describe the theme of the first four episodes and the themes of the last half of this season?

SILVERSTEIN: I don't know if we made a definitive continental divide between the first half and the last half. Many of the themes that are brought up early on continue, like immortality versus invulnerability, and what makes a hero. All those things grow over the course of the whole quest, over the whole season, and end up getting put to the test and literally getting put on trial at the end.

The action had already taken a step up in Season 2, but I feel like Season 3 has just elevated it to a new level. Of the sequences we've seen so far, which did you find the most challenging, and how did you overcome those obstacles?

SHOTZ: It's pretty tricky to have a 115-foot giant and how to portray that on screen. But we have such an incredible team in Vancouver. Eli Zagadakis, who's our stunt coordinator, is a bit of a visionary, where we put, obviously, this down on the page, but then we have such an incredible team around us, led by Eli, that they just come up with such incredible ideas that just add to it and elevate the words. I think even Walker [Scobell] asks it to be his PE, because I think for schooling, he's like, "I did that course today! I got to run from a 115-foot giant." There's just so many people who, at the highest end, have delivered on this.

That junkyard sequence that you saw in Episode 4, it was a 90,000-foot stage that we filled with all that junk and then had to perform stunts in there. We even had a rig that, when Talos was coming out, got to rock them. There was the giant foot that we had to build that was coming down to crash on them. There were so many different elements, and then our VFX team, led by Eric Henry, just delivered such an incredible job. They did such an incredible job of delivering Talos to make him such an opponent for them. So I would say that was probably one of the most complicated sequences to build.

SILVERSTEIN: Yeah, and in terms of fight choreography, I would just say get ready for Episode 7. Walker takes it to a new level. He gets a run for his money from Saara [Chaudry], an actor who does their own stunts and fighting. Luke and Thalia: there's a 60-to-70-move fight. It really kicks it up a notch, and then we have gods fighting each other, so there's a lot to look forward to.

Before we leave the junkyard, it is filled with so much stuff there. Do you have any Easter eggs that you can reveal that pop up there?

SILVERSTEIN: There's so much stuff that set deck jammed into those things. Yeah, I think we actually had some little deleted bits that we had in there.

SILVERSTEIN: I don't know, definitely freeze-frame.

SHOTZ: There's so much stuff that they've pulled from. I'm trying to remember a specific one. There's a giant Medusa shield that's behind Percy when he's up on the top level. One of the things that we wanted to do with that sequence was all the stuff that you do see; there was a moment of, "OK, what is a junkyard? Should it have cars? Should it have planes?" I think we wanted to make it feel very ancient and that there are things that have been around there for thousands of years. So we really pushed them to find very weird stuff, and they had to build a lot of stuff to make it look that way. Helen Jarvis, our production designer, really did an incredible job of building those towers. All the stuff that they interact with, it's all practical. And then, obviously, our VFX team extended the world, but they really had a lot of it there.

At the end of Episode 4, we have Bianca's death, which was a shocking moment in the book, but really heartbreaking to watch it visualized here in the series. Can you talk about filming that scene in particular? How will we see her death impact the rest of the quest mates moving forward?

SILVERSTEIN: The biggest surprise is going to be in the immediate aftermath at the top of Episode 5 of how we address that because she is there. She's still there, and they have to deal with her body, right? And then from there, obviously, it's going to affect them every step of the way.

But filming it was actually a great experience, and it was great to see Olive so proud of her performance. It gave a lot of joy because she was right to be proud of it, you know? She was just excited by the fact that the opportunity to play a death scene. I didn't think about that as an actor, to play a great death scene is one of those things that you hope to check off on your list of things to do. She did an amazing job, and I was excited by that. Which you wouldn't necessarily think about for such a heavy scene. You'd think that would make you really sad to play, but she was thrilled.

SHOTZ: It just carries so much weight going forward, and it really changes the series going forward. We wanted to honor that moment. Kids deal with death, and this is traumatic, and you feel the repercussions of it through the back half of the season, what it's doing to each of the characters as they grapple with that grief. I think you've seen up to probably Episode 4, but you really feel it specifically in the next episode, where they have to come to terms with having to move forward while dealing with this trauma.

Switching gears, I want to talk about Sally and Poseidon. In The Titan's Curse, Sally is seeing somebody new. Is that something we could get this season, or is there still time for a Sally and Poseidon flashback moment of when they met?

JON STEINBERG: You're not alone. That story holds a place in our minds as well, in terms of wanting to own in the way that I think Season 1 feels as though... It's willing and eager to go into places the book doesn't, in terms of what must have been, both in their origin and just what it is now. To be parents separated in that way. I will say this: I think it would be disappointing to get to the end of this show and not address it again.

It's been so cool to see Walker just progress, as an actor in front of the screen and behind the screen. How have you seen him evolve as a leader, both on and off set, for Percy Jackson?

STEINBERG: When you start building a show, there's an awareness that whoever is Number 1 on the call sheet every day, it goes a long way towards setting the tone of what your production and your show is. I remember early on feeling like this couldn't be that way. There's no way you can ask a 13-year-old boy to have that kind of responsibility for something this complicated. It's hard to answer the question of how he changed, because he was Number 1 on the call sheet pretty much from the beginning. In terms of the way he shows up for work, in terms of the way he is prepared, in terms of the way that he is determined to make sure other people get credit for things and to make sure that everybody feels safe. You'd be really lucky to find a far more experienced actor who can do that. He [Walker] did and does.

As the show has gotten bigger, and as he has been experiencing the things you experience when you're Number 1 on the call sheet on a show that's gotten into Season 3, it hasn't changed. If anything, it's gotten stronger. I don't know if the show works without it. I think his performance makes the show work on screen. But his humility, his confidence, and his maturity, and just the kind of person he is, are really going a long way towards holding it together as a production.
