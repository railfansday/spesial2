Eukaryotes’ closest relatives are internally simple syntrophic peptidotrophic archaea

Eukaryotes are theorized to have an origin associated with the archaeal phylum Promethearchaeota (formerly Asgard archaea)1,2,3. The first cultured representatives revealed valuable insights4,5 but are still distantly related to eukaryotes, leaving many unknowns regarding eukaryogenesis. Here we report the isolation of two strains, HC1 and SC1, that belong to the archaeal lineage that branches closest to eukaryotes (Heimdallarchaeia);6,7,8 these isolates are members of a pure co-culture and a tri-culture with methanogenic partners. Both are obligately anaerobic, syntrophic, peptide-degrading and mesophilic archaea that produce protrusions and vesicles but lack persistent internal membrane structures, like Promethearchaeales4,5. All Promethearchaeota isolates demonstrate behaviour focused on cell construction and membrane structures rather than division, in contrast to typical prokaryotes9. Strain HC1 possesses genes associated with aerobic lifestyles (such as cytochrome c oxidase (Cox)) but co-cultures cannot grow under (micro)oxic conditions. Meanwhile, the strain demonstrates growth under microoxic conditions when in the presence of aerobes, suggesting that Cox supports oxygen detoxification rather than respiration. Comparative genomics provides evidence that, like HC1, Cox-possessing Heimdallarchaeia studied to date probably do not respire oxygen. The wide conservation of anaerobiosis, cell structure without persistent intracellular membranes and membrane protrusions across Promethearchaeota suggest that the archaeal ancestor of eukaryotes may have had such physiological features. In this cultivation-informed scenario for eukaryogenesis, the archaea–eukaryote transition would involve a steep transition in cell structure and physiological shift from anaerobiosis to (facultative) aerobiosis10, with early interactions between the archaeal host and mitochondrial ancestor probably occurring under low-oxygen conditions and at least partially mediated by oxygen detoxification.

Understanding how complex eukaryotic cells emerged from their prokaryotic ancestors is a fundamental challenge in biology. The widely discussed endosymbiotic theory posits that the last eukaryotic common ancestor was derived from a host cell that had taken up an alphaproteobacteria-related symbiont that later became the mitochondrion11,12,13,14. Although the identity of the putative host cell in this theory has long been enigmatic, recent metagenomic and phylogenetic analyses indicate that eukaryotes probably evolved from a lineage closely related to members of the archaeal group Asgard archaea2,6,7,15,16,17, which has now been formally classified as the phylum Promethearchaeota within the kingdom Promethearchaeati3. This relationship is further reflected in the presence of abundant eukaryotic signature proteins (ESPs) in their genomes15,16,18. Moreover, several of these ESPs exhibit structural and functional similarities to those found in eukaryotes19,20,21,22.

What has remained unclear was the cell structure and physiology of the archaeal group; both have important implications for how archaea may have transitioned towards eukaryotes. On the basis of the presence of abundant and diverse ESPs in these archaea, studies hypothesized that the archaeal ancestor to eukaryotes may have already evolved a complex internal cell structure (that is, an endomembrane system) before endosymbiosis of the alphaproteobacterial ancestor of mitochondria (that is, mito-intermediate or mito-late)1,23,24,25. Cultivation efforts yielded one isolate, Promethearchaeum syntrophicum strain MK-D13,4, as well as enrichment cultures predominated by ‘Candidatus Lokiarchaeum ossiferum’5 and ‘Candidatus Nerearchaeum marumarumayae’26, all revealing a typical prokaryotic internal structure without a persistent eukaryote-like endomembrane system4,5,26 and potential evidence for an actin-based cytoskeleton5,27. All of the Promethearchaeaceae strains gain energy from anaerobic degradation of peptides/amino acids (and/or sugars) through symbiotic metabolic (that is, syntrophic) interactions with H2-consuming methanogenic archaea or sulfate-reducing bacteria3,4,5,26. A metagenomic study also observed organotrophic H2 generation as a shared metabolic feature across four classes of Promethearchaeota23. Collectively, these data suggest that anaerobiosis, H2 generation and prokaryotic cell structure may have been relevant to the evolutionary transition between archaea and eukaryotes.

However, whether features found in these Promethearchaeia (formerly Lokiarchaeia) cultures are relevant to the origin of eukaryotes remains a matter of debate1,7,27. First, metagenomic predictions suggest a wide diversity of physiologies across Promethearchaeota (such as autotrophy, organotrophy and rhodopsin-based phototrophy)23,28,29,30,31. Second, according to recent phylogenetic analyses, the class ‘Candidatus Heimdallarchaeia’ or its order ‘Candidatus Hodarchaeales’ may branch as a sister group to eukaryotes6,7,8. On the basis of these observations, a variety of features, including complex internal cell structure4,7,8,23,24,25, have been proposed for the last archaea (specifically ‘Ca. Heimdallarchaeia’) eukaryote common ancestor (LAECA). Another potential feature of this ancestor that has recently attracted attention is aerobic respiration7,23,30,32. If LAECA were capable of oxygen respiration, this would substantially alter some models of eukaryogenesis that posit an anaerobic archaeal host interacting syntrophically with an alphaproteobacterial endosymbiont capable of aerobic respiration. Studies find that some ‘Ca. Heimdallarchaeia’ possess terminal oxidases (Cox)7,23 and further propose LAECA may have been aerobic30,32. These observations suggest that LAECA already had the beginnings of structural and respiratory features associated with eukaryotes. However, the cell structure of ‘Ca. Heimdallarchaeia’ has yet to be elucidated and the simple presence of such enzymes cannot distinguish between aerotolerant anaerobes and (facultative) aerobes given that many obligately anaerobic organisms possess terminal oxidases for oxygen tolerance33,34. In light of this, cultivation of class ‘Ca. Heimdallarchaeia’ from habitats exposed to oxygen is essential to identify how these archaea may interact with oxygen and determine the cell structures and physiologies that are potentially relevant to LAECA1.

Here we successfully cultured two strains of Heimdallarchaeia belonging to the order Hodarchaeales from a biofilm submerged in anoxic subsurface-derived brine water but exposed to air hourly. One was isolated in a pure syntrophic co-culture and another in a pure tri-culture with methanogenic partners. The strain in co-culture had terminal oxidases and yet exhibited strict dependence on anaerobic metabolism. We further report the cultivation procedures, cellular structures, physiological characteristics and genomic features of these strains, and their implications for the origin of eukaryotes.

To culture members of ‘Heimdallarchaeia’ and examine their potential relationship with oxygen, we capitalized on previous experience in cultivation of P. syntrophicum MK-D14,35. We attributed successful isolation of this strain to a long-term bioreactor-based increase in metabolic activity, acclimatization to artificial conditions and selection against other populations (that is, enrichment)3,4,35. Previous studies also hinted that the presence and activity of anaerobic methane-oxidizing microbial consortia were probably important in supporting the growth of Promethearchaeota members, presumably through the excretion of organic compounds35,36. Here we looked to a gas production well in the Boso Peninsula, Chiba Prefecture, Japan, which is operationally similar to our previous cultivation methane-fed bioreactor (Extended Data Fig. 1). Methane-bearing saline groundwater (that is, brine) upwells into the well and supports a biofilm on the walls containing populations identical or similar to those found in marine sediments, according to 16S rRNA gene tag-sequencing (iTag) analysis37. In contrast to our previous anoxic reactor, the biofilm is intermittently exposed to air (and therefore oxygen) due to periodic eruptions of brine, akin to geysers, that cause the water level to drop temporarily37. Enriched in these biofilms were small populations of Heimdallarchaeia, which we targeted for isolation (Supplementary Table 1).

We aimed to culture the biofilm microbial community in the laboratory while mimicking natural conditions. Filtered brine from the well was used as the basal medium and supplemented this with methane and magnetite to attempt co-enrichment of Promethearchaeota members with anaerobic methane-oxidizing archaea (Supplementary Note 1). While typical anaerobe cultivation procedures call for addition of reducing agents (such as sodium sulfide and/or cysteine) to the culture medium to scavenge oxygen, we omitted these to retain exposure to low concentrations of oxygen. After approximately 3 months, we detected several 16S rRNA gene sequences of Hodarchaeales, Heimdallarchaeales and Promethearchaeales (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). We implemented proteinaceous substrates, casamino acids and powdered milk (each 0.005%, w/v), as energy sources for subcultures to enhance the growth of these archaea, based on our previous observation that syntrophic degradation of amino acids/peptides is a widespread feature across the phylum4 (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Table 1). To also select against other organisms, we supplemented the medium with five antibiotics: ampicillin, vancomycin, kanamycin, streptomycin and erythromycin.

For cultures that sustained Promethearchaeota members according to iTag analysis, we subcultured twice in fresh medium of the same composition at roughly 100-day intervals; cultures that enriched one population of Hodarchaeales over other Promethearchaeota were further subcultured using a synthetic medium mimicking natural brine supplemented with casamino acids, powdered milk and five antibiotics (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). By omitting reducing agents, we were able to retain microoxic conditions (281 ± 123 nM dissolved oxygen, n = 5) that would possibly select for Hodarchaeales over Promethearchaeales members, each presumably associated with more oxidized and reduced lifestyles3,23. After four successive transfers under the same cultivation conditions, the culture retained aerobic bacteria (that is, Mariniphaga and Alcanivorax), contained a higher abundance of the Hodarchaeales population (1.8% in relative abundance) and lost the Promethearchaeales members (Supplementary Table 1). This suggested that the Hodarchaeales population can at least tolerate low-oxygen concentrations and is probably more adapted to oxic conditions compared with the Promethearchaeales members.

In subsequent subcultures, reducing agents were introduced and peptone was added as an additional energy source. The Hodarchaeales population reached up to 30% in relative abundance and maximum cell densities up to 106 16S rRNA gene copy numbers per ml. As for the other populations, only Mariniphaga, Alcanivorax and Methanocalculus remained (Supplementary Table 1). We isolated the Mariniphaga and Alcanivorax species, identified antibiotics that they are sensitive to (Supplementary Table 2) and applied these antibiotics (gentamycin, ciprofloxacin, cephalothin or ofloxacin) to the Hodarchaeales-dominated cultures (Supplementary Table 1). In parallel we also optimized the cultivation temperature and energy source types and concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Three years after the original sampling, we isolated Hodarchaeales in a purified co-culture with a single partnering methanogen population (Methanocalculus), each designated as strains HC1 and MC2, in an anaerobic medium supplemented with casamino acids, peptone and yeast extract (each 0.005%, w/v) at 30 °C (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Under these cultivation conditions, HC1 required 25–35 days to reach full growth with a doubling time of approximately 7–12 days, reaching a maximum cell density of 106 16S rRNA gene copies per ml (Fig. 1c). HC1 cells were morphologically similar to Promethearchaeales members3,4,5, with coccoid cells, albeit with larger diameters of 0.8–2.3 µm (1.3 µm on average; Supplementary Table 3) and multiple protrusions extending from the cell surface (Fig. 1b).

Using a cultivation strategy similar to that for HC1, we successfully isolated another Hodarchaeales strain and also enriched other Hodarchaeales and Heimdallarchaeaceae members (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Note 2). One Hodarchaeales strain, designated SC1, was isolated in a purified tri-culture with Methanocalculus sp. strain MC3 and Methanolobus sp. strain MLB, with relative abundances of approximately 40%, 60% and <0.3%, respectively, based on iTag analysis (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 1). The tri-culture is stably maintained in anaerobic medium supplemented with casamino acids, powdered milk, polypeptone peptone (0.01%, w/v) and yeast extract (0.05%, w/v). SC1 required approximately 60 days to reach maximum growth with a doubling time of approximately 14–20 days, reaching a maximum cell density of 106 16S rRNA gene copies per ml (Fig. 1e). The 16S rRNA gene sequence identity between SC1 and HC1 was 77.55% based on BLAST analysis. SC1 cells were morphologically similar to HC1 but with a smaller diameter, 0.6–1.2 µm (0.9 µm on average) (Fig. 1d).

We sequenced the genomes of both strains. HC1 and SC1 possess circular genomes 7.91 Mb and 6.75 Mb, respectively (Supplementary Tables 4–6). HC1 has a larger genome than all reported Heimdallarchaeia metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs)32. Phylogenetic analyses showed that both strains belonged to the family-level clade LC-3 in Hodarchaeales (Fig. 1a). We tentatively propose the names ‘Candidatus Margulisarchaeum peptidiphilum’ and ‘Candidatus Flexarchaeum multiprotrusionum’ for strains HC1 and SC1, respectively, under the family ‘Candidatus Margulisarchaeaceae’ (Supplementary Note 3).

According to our analyses, HC1 and SC1 each possess 27 and 29 types of ESPs (Supplementary Table 7), putting them in the top quartile of Heimdallarchaeia. Both strains have numbers of ESP types for each ESP category greater than or equal to the average among Heimdallarchaeia. As such, we propose that HC1 is a suitable representative of extant Heimdallarchaeia or Hodarchaeales for examining whether these lineages harbour any intracellular cell structures related to or resembling eukaryotic complexity.

Observation of HC1 and SC1 cells in late exponential and early stationary phases using electron microscopy (EM) revealed that both strains have signatures of typical prokaryotic cells (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 3). Cells are coccoid and generally found as individuals, occasionally in aggregates (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Figs. 2c and 3c,d,s). Cells divide through binary fission and, during this, exhibit a ring-like structure (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 3e). Both strains produced membrane vesicles (Extended Data Figs. 2o–s and 3w). On the basis of cryo-EM, the cytoplasm and protrusions contained actin-filament-like structures and ferritin-like membrane-less granules (Fig. 2j–n, Extended Data Figs. 2h–j,m, 3t–w, 4 and 5, Supplementary Tables 5 and 6, Supplementary Figs. 2–4, Supplementary Notes 4–6 and Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). On the cell surface, we observed regular S-layer-like structures under cryo-EM and quick-freeze deep-etch replica EM (QFDE-EM) (Fig. 2f and Extended Data Figs. 2g,l and 3r,s,v,w). A combination of protein multimer structure prediction using AlphaFold338 and glycoproteomics revealed S-layer proteins conserved across many Promethearchaeota lineages that form symmetric homotetramers with a central vertical pore and are N-glycosylated like S-layer proteins of other archaea39 (Extended Data Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 8). In addition to the S-layer, we observed that the cell body and protrusion surfaces of HC1 are covered with very thin filaments (around 55 nm in length and approximately 1 nm in diameter), although their function remains unclear (Extended Data Fig. 2i–k,m,n).

After mid-exponential phase, most cells possess multiple protrusions of uniform diameter (approximately 370 nm), varying lengths, branching and bulbous expansions (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 3), morphologically similar to P. syntrophicum MK-D13,4 and ‘Ca. L. ossiferum’5. Based on observations using scanning EM (SEM), protrusions were absent in all cells that appear to be undergoing cell division (cells that are in the middle of separating into two cocci with characteristic rings in both cocci marking where cell division probably began; Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 3e), suggesting that protrusion generation and cell division are mutually exclusive processes. The protrusions often comprised more than half the volume of each cell (roughly 1.7 µm3 or 142% of the main cell body, on average; Supplementary Table 3), suggesting they have an important role in the physiology of these archaea. Notably, both protrusions and cells were flexible and were observed to sway by Brownian motion (Supplementary Videos 3 and 4). We found that fixation with formaldehyde tended to disconnect their protrusions (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating that caution must be taken in defining morphology using fixed cells. The protrusions of all isolated and highly enriched Promethearchaeota3,4,5 were observed to physically attach to syntrophic partners. For HC1 and SC1, helical filaments on the protrusion surface tended to be found at the protrusion–partner interface (Fig. 2d,e, Extended Data Figs. 2e,f and 3g–j and Supplementary Note 7). Given the consistent presence of protrusions in Promethearchaeota cultures, we suspect that LAECA also probably possessed such structures.

We further microscopically investigated whether these Hodarchaeales strains have a complex eukaryote-like internal system. The eukaryote endomembrane system is characterized with universally conserved, physically persistent organelles (the nuclear envelope and endoplasmic reticulum) and structurally transient compartments (such as Golgi apparatus and lysosomes), all integrated by regulated membrane trafficking. Based on transmission EM (TEM) and cryo-EM, we did not observe any persistent internal membrane structures shared among all HC1 or SC1 cells (Fig. 2g,h,k,l, Extended Data Figs. 2h–j and 3k,l,t,u,w and Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). Meanwhile, although infrequent, low-electron-density intracellular vesicle-like structures were observed in some cells (Fig. 2m,n, Extended Data Fig. 3m,n,v and Supplementary Video 1). This indicates the absence of persistent eukaryote-like organelles and presence of transient internal structures. Consistent with this, a Heimdallarchaeales archaeon (‘Candidatus Yibarchaeum umbracryptum’) enriched by a recent study lacked persistent organelles and possessed transient intracellular vesicles of unknown function that appeared during early growth stages40. With current data, it is unclear whether the transient structures are related to ESPs, a primitive endomembrane system, or neither.

Despite the absence of conserved internal membrane structures, nearly all HC1 and SC1 cells showed spatial separation between DNA and rRNA based on fluorescence microscopy using DAPI and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) (Fig. 2i, Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Video 5), as also previously observed in other Promethearchaeia and Heimdallarchaeia41,42. The reason for this spatial localization is not yet clear, but histones may mediate DNA compaction43. All microscopically studied Hodarchaeales and Heimdallarchaeales—albeit three strains only—consistently lack conserved, persistent eukaryote-like organelles. Despite this cell architecture, HC1 and SC1 have a comparable number of ESPs associated with endosomal sorting and trafficking (11 and 10 out of 34 types) to other Hodarchaeales and Heimdallarchaeia (10 ± 2 and 10 ± 2 types on average, respectively) (Supplementary Table 7). These suggest that other extant Heimdallarchaeia may also lack persistent internal structures. Given that only 9 out of 34 of the endosomal sorting and trafficking-related ESP types are moderately conserved across Heimdallarchaeia (found in at least 50% of the genomes associated with each order), LAECA may have also possessed few such ESPs. Although this suggests the possibility that LAECA also lacked persistent internal cell structure, the genomic composition of LAECA remains to be resolved.

The HC1 co-culture and SC1 tri-culture both contain H2-using methanogenic archaea, and addition of the methanogenesis inhibitor 2-bromoethanesulfonate (10 mM) to the cultures completely suppressed growth. This shows that both HC1 and SC1 depend on H2-mediated symbiosis with methanogens. Such a metabolic symbiosis is known as syntrophy, in which an organotroph performs a hydrogen and/or formate-yielding metabolism that is thermodynamically inhibited by these products and a partner organism scavenges these products to low non-inhibitory concentrations44. Accordingly, amending high concentrations of hydrogen gas or formate also suppressed the growth of both strains (Supplementary Table 9). In the SC1 tri-culture, we detected a methanogen that uses only methylated compounds, Methanolobus sp. MLB, and suspect that SC1 may generate methanol through formate reduction through the expressed aldehyde reductase and alcohol dehydrogenase (Supplementary Table 6), a recently discovered pathway and phenomenon45. Given that syntrophy and symbiosis have been hypothesized to be relevant to the origin of eukaryotes4,12,23, the identification of these features in Heimadallarchaeia has major evolutionary implications.

Both strains are clearly dependent on peptides, as supplementation with non-proteinaceous substrates did not stimulate growth of HC1 and SC1, and neither strain could grow when peptide substrates were replaced with a mixture of 20 proteinogenic amino acids (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 9). Ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC)-based analysis of macromolecule distributions in the cultures indicated clear consumption of higher-molecular-mass molecules (Fig. 3b,c, Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 8). Genome analyses revealed that both strains encode extracellular peptidases, peptide transporters, amino acid degradation pathways of glutamate, aspartate, threonine, methionine, glycine, serine, alanine and cysteine in both strains, and NiFe hydrogenases for syntrophic peptide degradation (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Energy is conserved through ATP synthesis through substrate-level phosphorylation and proton motive force generation coupled with ferredoxin-driven H2 generation (Fig. 4). Transcriptomic and proteomic data further confirmed their expression (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Glycoproteomics revealed that peptide/nickel transport system substrate-binding proteins of both strains were highly N-glycosylated like P. syntrophicum MK-D146 (Extended Data Fig. 8, Supplementary Table 8 and Supplementary Note 10), further evidencing the importance of peptides for the growth of these strains. Collectively, we demonstrate that both strains are obligately syntrophy-dependent peptidotrophic archaea.

In cultures of HC1, we observed that an increasing amount of peptides added to the medium increased the cell size and protrusion number but not the growth rate—a trend that is atypical of prokaryotes9. When the yeast extract concentration in the HC1 culture was increased from 0.005% to 0.05%, the growth rate (doubling times from 7 days to 6 days) did not significantly change (Fig. 1c). By contrast, observation of cells showed significant increases in the volume of the main cell body (from 1.2 ± 0.01 to 2.6 ± 0.01 µm3 estimated based on cell diameter increase from 1.3 ± 0.3 to 1.7 ± 0.3 µm; P = 1.3 × 10−24) (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Table 3). We further used wide-area SEM to analyse roughly 15,000 cells for changes in protrusion and vesicle production (Supplementary Fig. 7). We observed consistent increases, on a per-cell basis, in the volume and maximum number of protrusions (1.7 to 4.1 µm3 and 8 to 11, respectively), the volume and number of vesicles (0.02 to 0.11 µm3 and 0.4 to 3.7, respectively) and protein content (66 to 226 fg; Supplementary Table 10). Similar trends were observed for P. syntrophicum MK-D1 and SC1 (Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Note 11).

Collectively, these findings suggest that, when more carbon and energy are available, these strains allocate them primarily to the cell (that is, main cell body and protrusions) rather than to cell division. This is in direct contrast to typical prokaryotes that allocate available carbon and energy to the cell body and cell division and analogous to the biology of eukaryotes, for which size and reproduction rates are inversely related9,47,48. Although much more research is necessary to connect the cell biology and ESPs in Promethearchaeota with eukaryogenesis, we suspect from these results that Promethearchaeota’s unique aprokaryotic focus on cell maintenance/construction of membrane-based structures over growth may have served as a foundation for the evolution of elaborate membrane or cytoskeletal structures in its descendants that evolved towards eukaryotes. Potentially related to this, for all three Promethearchaeota strains, only non-dividing cells produce complex actin-containing protrusions, which are a major contributor to cell size (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Figs. 2 and 3). As the number and volumes of protrusions increase when more carbon and energy are available and the protrusions are never observed in dividing cells, we suspect that, besides syntrophy, the protrusions and their contents (including actin filaments) may also serve as nutrient, energy and building block storage for later cell division. For example, in eukaryotes, it has long been known that muscle cells—more specifically, proteins abundant in these cells (such as actin and myosin)—function as reservoirs of amino acids49. Although tempting to speculate, it remains unclear whether these physiological functions are evolutionarily related.

The purified HC1 co-cultures showed no growth in culture medium containing trace levels of oxygen either (1) left by omission of reducing agents or (2) further added by supplementing the head space with gas containing 1% (v/v) oxygen. Both resulted in major decreases (roughly two orders of magnitude) in HC1 cell densities (Fig. 3a). However, for reducing-agent-free cultures, we found that HC1 could grow when aerobic bacteria were present—that is, Alcanivorax sp. AC and Mariniphaga sp. PC isolated from HC1 enrichment cultures (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 12). Nevertheless, HC1 cell yields did not increase. This indicates that HC1 is not stimulated by oxygen but can tolerate continuous exposure to oxygen when aerobic organisms are present and scavenging oxygen.

HC1 encodes and expresses pathways coupling anaerobic amino acid degradation with H2 generation (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 5). These pathways rely on NAD+, NADP+, ferredoxin and quinone as electron carriers and are fundamentally centred on ferredoxin-dependent electron transfer. We identified oxidoreductases that may facilitate electron transfer between these carriers, including NAD+:quinone and ferredoxin + NAD+:2NADP+ (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 5). These can ultimately funnel reducing equivalents to H2 or formate generation through membrane-bound and cytosolic NiFe hydrogenases and cytosolic formate dehydrogenase.

In contrast to hydrogenases found in MK-D1 and ‘Ca. L. ossiferum’, those encoded by HC1 are potentially aerotolerant, based on homology to archaeal hydrogenases that can tolerate short exposure to oxygen: MBH (group 4g) and SHI (group 3b)50,51 (catalytic subunit sequence identities of 45% and 49%). These observations suggest that HC1’s syntrophic anaerobic metabolism can tolerate low concentrations of oxygen while remaining fundamentally ferredoxin centred, a hallmark of anaerobic energy metabolism.

HC1 possesses genes encoding terminal oxidases—cytochrome bd oxidase (Cbd)-like protein and Cox—and expresses them despite the reduced anoxic culture conditions. While aerobic organisms possess a full set of enzymes for funnelling electrons from electron donors to quinones or cytochrome c, such pathways are absent in HC1 (Fig. 4). Even if we assumed the presence of an uncharacterized redox protein with quinone:cytochrome c oxidoreductase activity like cytochrome bc1, only part of the electrons derived from amino acid degradation would be transferrable to oxygen. As such, HC1 lacks the capacity to completely couple peptide degradation and oxygen respiration.

Previous studies, together with our genomic survey (Supplementary Table 13), show that strict anaerobes can use Cbd and/or Cox for scavenging and tolerating nanomolar to micromolar concentrations of oxygen rather than for respiration or growth52,53,54,55. Gene expressivity (translation efficiency) predicted from codon use56 also supported our interpretation that oxygen reduction is a supplementary function in HC1 physiology (Supplementary Table 5).

As a metabolic link between peptidotrophy and aerotolerance, we identified expression of oxygen-dependent enzymes for degradation of aromatic amino acids—phenylalanine, tryptophan and tyrosine57,58 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 14). While these enzymes can participate in pathways that convert these amino acids to central metabolites (for example, fumarate, acetyl-CoA and alanine), HC1 possesses truncated pathways that neither conserve energy nor generate intermediates feeding into other metabolic processes. Such pathways can also be found in genera containing obligate anaerobes, such as Pyrobaculum, Thermoproteus, Thermovibrio and Desulfurobacterium. We therefore suspect that HC1 uses aromatic amino acids and these pathways to scavenge oxygen.

Moreover, the HC1 genome also encodes oxygen-tolerant central metabolism (pyruvate dehydrogenase) and oxidative stress enzymes (that is, catalase, superoxide dismutase, thioredoxin-dependent peroxiredoxin, peroxiredoxin 2/4, glutaredoxin-dependent peroxiredoxin, and globin; Supplementary Table 5). In HC1 cultures, we also verified the presence of haem B (Supplementary Fig. 8), a cofactor for catalase and superoxide dismutase. HC1 also possesses and expresses genes encoding proteins (such as xanthine dehydrogenase; Xdh) for the production of the antioxidant uric acid from purines—an oxidative-stress-mitigation strategy59. Ferritin, a key protein for reducing reactive oxygen species generation from the interaction of iron and oxygen60, was also among the three most expressed proteins in HC1’s transcriptome.

As with oxygen reduction, HC1 possesses a nitrate reductase homologous to respiratory nitrate reductase NarGHI but lacks pathways for funnelling all electrons from peptidotrophy to nitrate reduction (Supplementary Table 5). In HC1 and methanogen co-cultures supplemented with nitrate, we observed nitrate reduction activity and associated nitrite accumulation, but no stimulation of growth (Extended Data Fig. 10a). As predicted from the genome, only a small proportion of the electrons derived from peptide degradation (around 15%) were funnelled into nitrate reduction and the rest were transferred to the partner methanogen. Moreover, nitrate-amended cultures showed no growth when a methanogen inhibitor was present, indicating that HC1 strictly depends on syntrophy even when performing nitrate respiration. Given that nitrate (1 mM) is inhibitory (Extended Data Fig. 10b), we suspect the nitrate reductase may serve to scavenge nitrate rather than support nitrate-respiration-driven growth. This is analogous to the terminal oxidase-supported aerotolerance discussed above (Extended Data Fig. 10c).

We further evaluate whether the Promethearchaeota phylum broadly conserves anaerobic lifestyles regardless of the presence or absence of Cox, as observed in cultured representatives. One distinguishing metabolic feature between aerobes and anaerobes is dependency on oxygen-sensitive 4Fe–4S ferredoxin-dependent enzymes and alternative oxygen-tolerant NAD-dependent enzymes in central metabolism: 2-oxoacid:ferredoxin oxidoreductases (OFORs) and 2-oxoacid dehydrogenases (ODHs). Oxidation or reductive generation of 2-oxoacids occurs repeatedly across multiple central metabolic pathways, including pyruvate oxidation/synthesis, the citric acid cycle and amino acid metabolism, with distinct enzymes acting on specific 2-oxoacids or sets of 2-oxoacids. Thus, the relative genomic representation of OFORs and ODHs should reflect the extent to which an organism relies on anaerobic ferredoxin-dependent metabolism versus oxygen-tolerant NAD-dependent metabolism. For example, the obligate aerobe Bacillus subtilis lacks OFOR and possesses four core E1 dehydrogenase subunits of ODH to support organics degradation and assimilation, whereas the obligate anaerobe Methanocaldococcus jannaschii lacks ODH but encodes two OFORs to support pyruvate synthesis and a partial reductive TCA cycle for autotrophic growth. We therefore propose abundances of these proteins as a metric for examining whether an organism may be capable or incapable of aerobic growth. The abundances of OFOR and ODH homologues in each isolate normalized to genome size clearly separate obligate anaerobes (with and without Cox) from obligate and facultative aerobes (Fig. 5a–c and Supplementary Tables 15 and 16). A binary classifier trained on these features achieved high accuracy with consistent results across models (balanced accuracies of 0.96 and 0.95 using logistic regression (LR) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA); Supplementary Tables 16) and accurately classified obligate anaerobes that encode Cox (accuracy of 0.97 using LR). This indicates that comparison of OFOR and ODH abundance provides a robust discriminator of metabolic lifestyle.

While recently proposed approaches for aerobe–anaerobe classification show comparable overall accuracy32,61,62, they exhibited lower predictive capacities for obligate anaerobes that encode Cox compared with the OFOR–ODH-based classifier, falsely classifying some as aerobes (accuracies of 0.9 and 0.76; Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 16). For the classifier based on aerobe–anaerobe-associated genes62, the inclusion and predominance of terminal-oxidase-related genes (that is, terminal oxidase subunits and biosynthesis proteins for terminal oxidase haem groups) in the top contributing genes for aerobe prediction may have contributed to the false predictions. Thus, although the presence of Cox is often implemented as an indicator for potential for aerobiosis, cultivation and comparative genomics here indicate that caution must be taken in interpreting the physiology of Cox-containing organisms, including those of Heimdallarchaeia.

Promethearchaeota, including HC1 and other recently reported Cox-encoding Heimdallarchaeia31,32, exhibit OFOR–ODH abundances similar to anaerobic isolates (Fig. 5a–d). Applying the OFOR–ODH-based classifiers, all the analysed genomes of Heimdallarchaeia were consistently predicted to be anaerobic with high confidence regardless of the presence or absence of Cox (mean maximum posterior probabilities of 0.98 ± 0.04 and 0.97 ± 0.06 for LR and LDA; Supplementary Tables 16 and 17). Nearly all other Promethearchaeota genomes were also confidently classified as anaerobic. Classification based on aerobe–anaerobe-associated genes62 also predicted most Cox-encoding Heimdallarchaeia, including HC1, as anaerobic (maximum posterior probabilities of 0.97 ± 0.12 and 0.96 ± 0.09 for LR and LDA, for those predicted as anaerobes). For lineages encoding Cox, this pattern suggests that terminal oxidases are more likely to function in aerotolerance rather than supporting canonical aerobic respiration. We illustrate the ferredoxin-centric metabolisms of HC1 in Fig. 4.

A specific comparison of Promethearchaeota with other archaea yielded a similar pattern. Although archaea capable of aerobic growth often retain ferredoxin-dependent metabolism despite exposure to O2, their OFOR repertoires were genomically distinct from those of obligate anaerobes. Nearly all aerobic isolates (334 out of 340 isolates, or 97%) encoded OFOR containing the POR, POR_N and PFOR_II domains that accounted for more than one-third of their OFORs, whereas, for obligately anaerobic isolates, this OFOR represented no more than one-third of their OFORs (Supplementary Table 18). All analysed Heimdallarchaeia also showed low relative abundances of this aerobe-associated OFOR. Accordingly, their abundances of other OFORs and ODHs were distinct from those of aerobic archaea and instead similar to those of obligately anaerobic archaea and bacteria (Fig. 5d).

Although a recent metagenomic survey proposed aerobic lifestyles for Hodarchaeales based on occurrence of Cox-harbouring MAGs in oxic environments including pelagic habitats (for example, epi-, bathy- and hadal-pelagic)32, all such MAGs were derived from sediments (that is, benthic), where oxygen exposure is highly ambiguous given that concentrations typically decline sharply below the surface63. Moreover, Hodarchaeales were detected in public metagenomic datasets annotated as derived from oxygen-associated environments32, but the presence of oxygen is unclear given that these are sediment samples or oxygen concentrations are either below the detection limit or not measured.

Consistent with this, most Promethearchaeota species—including HC1 and Cox-containing Heimdallarchaeia—have oxygen-sensitive NiFe hydrogenases, reflecting dependence on anaerobic metabolism. Promethearchaeota nearly universally possess group 3c and/or 4g hydrogenases (Supplementary Table 19). In Heimdallarchaeia, the orders Heimdallarchaeales, Gerdarchaeales and Hodarchaeales each generally conserve group 4g (92%), 3b/3c/4g (92%/80%/80%) and 3c (90%) hydrogenases, respectively (Supplementary Table 20). The widespread conservation of ferredoxin-oxidizing hydrogenases (groups 3c and 4g) further supports reliance on a ferredoxin-centred, anaerobic metabolic strategy across Promethearchaeota, including Heimdallarchaeia. Analysis of computed gene expressivity across all archaeal species representatives in GTDB release 220 suggests that anaerobic peptidotrophy is also widely conserved across Promethearchaeota (Fig. 3f, Methods, Supplementary Table 21 and Supplementary Note 12). The coupling of the two would implicate anaerobic H2-evolving peptidotrophy as a widely conserved feature of Promethearchaeota, which agrees with our previous hypothesis4 and phylogenetic analyses tracing organotrophic H2 production and acetogenesis through the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway to the Promethearchaeota ancestor7,64.

Given this broadly anaerobic metabolic framework, we next considered when these archaea may have acquired Cox-mediated aerotolerance. Phylogenetic analyses of the Cox complex based on a concatenated alignment (Cox1–3), Cox1 and Cox3 all show Heimdallarchaeales sequences nested within those of Hodarchaeales (Fig. 5e–g and Supplementary Fig. 10c–e). These topologies do not show evidence for the presence of Cox at the common ancestor of Hodarchaeales and Heimdallarchaeales and suggest that Heimdallarchaeales acquired Cox horizontally from Hodarchaeales.

Here we isolated two strains from the archaeal lineage that descended from the last common ancestor of archaea and eukaryotes—Heimdallarchaeia—and reveal insights into their biology. Anaerobiosis, the absence of persistent intracellular structures and membrane protrusions are conserved among all cultured members, P. syntrophicum MK-D13,4, ‘Ca. L. ossiferum’5, ‘Ca. N. marumarumayae’26, strains HC1 and SC1, and a recent Heimdallarchaeaceae enrichment culture40. Dependence on peptides and syntrophy for energy metabolism is also consistent across those with detailed physiological characterization3,5. These organisms further exhibit symbiotic, protrusion-mediated intercellular attachment, linking cell morphology with syntrophic interactions. The conservation of these physiological features across strains spanning two Promethearchaeota classes points towards an anaerobic, peptide-degrading and H2-evolving LAECA that lacked persistent internal structures but possessed membrane-based protrusions.

Consistent with this, members of the phylum, including all cultured strains, lack many pathways for biosynthesis of amino acids, vitamins and nucleotides (Supplementary Table 22), indicating metabolic dependence on partner organisms. Moreover, slow growth is also conserved across all cultured strains and, if LAECA also shared this feature, it may have contributed to key processes in eukaryogenesis (Supplementary Note 13). Although it is unclear whether LAECA possessed Cox, cultivation and comparative genomics indicate that Cox in Heimdallarchaeia are unlikely to function in oxygen respiration. Together, these observations suggest that the prokaryote–eukaryote transition may have involved a physiological shift from an anaerobic to (facultatively) aerobic lifestyle10, alongside major structural innovations. If LAECA was anaerobic but possessed aerotolerance like HC1 (and potentially other Heimdallarchaeia), this feature could have supported interaction with a mitochondrial ancestor under low-oxygen or fluctuating redox conditions, where oxygen reduction by the bacterial partner may have alleviated oxygen toxicity. This relationship is also consistent with the ‘Ox–Tox’ model positing that the mitochondrial progenitor had a role in oxygen detoxification for an anaerobic host65.

Although the extremely small cell size and slow growth rate of P. syntrophicum MK-D1, the only publicly available Promethearchaeota strain3,4, had posed a major obstacle, efforts in cultivation here led to the isolation of two strains that have larger cell sizes, higher growth rates, shorter lag phases and a closer relationship to eukaryotes. These make the strains promising model organisms for exploring the biology of Promethearchaeota that can catalyse investigations of cellular and physiological transitions underlying the emergence of eukaryotic complexity.

Coding sequences were predicted using Prokka (v.1.14.6)94 with the default parameters and annotated with eggnog-mapper (v.2.1.12)95 and the eggnog (v.5.0) database using the default parameters96. For constructing a genome-based phylogeny of Promethearchaeota, the following single-copy marker genes defined by GTDB were used for phylogenetic tree construction (Supplementary Table 25). Maximum-likelihood estimation of the phylogeny was performed using IQTREE v.2.3.5 with ultrafast bootstrapping97, a concatenated alignment of these marker genes trimmed using BMGE v.1.12 (-m BLOSUM30 -g 0.67 -b 3)98 and a universal distribution model (UDM0064 LCLR) as previously described99. Maximum-likelihood estimation of the phylogeny for the 16S rRNA gene was performed using a sequence alignment generated with MAFFT (--maxiterate 1000 --localpair)87, the optimum model selected by ModelFinder (SYM + R6)100 and regular bootstrapping. Bootstrap values were recalculated using BOOSTER (v.0.1.2)101.

For maximum-likelihood estimation of the phylogeny of target genes, we first collected relevant sequences by using amino acid sequences obtained from Promethearchaeota as the query and searching against genes belonging to the same protein family collected from GTDB r220 species representatives92. For this, the MMseqs2 v.16.747c6 search command102 was used with three iterative searches with different sensitivities from sensitivity level 4 to 8.5 (--min-seq-id 0.3 -c 0.7). To select representative sequences for tree construction, we (1) clustered the collected sequences using the MMseqs2 cluster command102 (--min-seq-id 0.6 -c 0.7); (2) constructed a rough tree using FastTreeMP (v.2.1.11)91 with an alignment generated with MAFFT (v.7.525)87 and trimmed with trimAl v.1.5.0 (-gt 0.1)90; and (3) subsampled phylogenetically representative sequences from the tree using Parnas (v.0.1.6)103. An alignment for the representatives was generated using MAFFT-DASH (--localpair --maxiterate 100 --dash)104 and trimmed using trimAl (-gt 0.3). Finally, we used this as an input for maximum-likelihood estimation of the phylogeny using IQTREE v.3.1.1 with either 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates or 100 bootstrap replicates97, and used ModelFinder100 to identify the best-fitting model among C10, C20, C30, C40, C50 and C60 (LG + CXX + G + F). For bootstrap replicates, transfer bootstrap expectation values were also calculated using BOOSTER (v.0.1.2)101.

For estimation of the phylogeny of the Cox complex, we used a concatenated alignment of subunits 1, 2 and 3. For all archaeal and bacterial species representatives in GTDB release 220, each subunit was identified with hmmsearch85 against the corresponding protein families (PF00115, PF00116 and PF00510) using a domain e-value cut-off of 0.001 and coverage of 50%. Subunits were concatenated if the target organism encoded (1) one copy of each subunit across its genome; or (2) multiple copies but organized one copy of each within 10 loci of each other. For organisms outside of Promethearchaeota, Cox1 was clustered with 50% sequence identity and 80% coverage using MMseqs2 easy-cluster to choose representative sets of Cox1, 2 and 3. Alignment for each subunit and tree construction of the concatenated alignment was performed as described above but with ultrafast bootstrap support (-bb 1000).

Gene expressivity was calculated using cordon56 using ribosomal proteins as a reference and the default settings. Peptidotrophy was annotated based on the presence of extracellular peptidases, glutamate dehydrogenases and glycine dehydrogenases with high expressivity (within the top 25% of each genome), all consistently found in isolates specialized in peptide degradation (strains MK-D1, HC1, SC1, Coprothermobacter proteolyticus, Necrotrophus endoclepta, Porphyromonas gingivalis and Salinivirga cyanobacteriivorans). Anaerobic peptidotrophy was annotated if high-expressivity OFORs and FeFe or NiFe hydrogenases were also present. Those with NiFe hydrogenase-containing gene sets homologous to those highly expressed by syntrophic Promethearchaeota isolates or archaeal electron-bifurcating hydrogenase MvhADG–HdrABC were further annotated as H2-evolving. Aerobic peptidotrophy was annotated based on the presence of a high-expressivity Cox (within top 50% of each genome) along with a complete complex III (cytochrome b/b6 with a cytochrome c, Rieske FeS protein and/or a copper-binding electron carrier like plastocyanin or halocyanin). Aerotolerant peptidotrophy was annotated based on the presence of a high-expressivity Cbd with type 1 or 2 NADH:quinone oxidoreductase but lacking the ability to transfer all electrons from peptide degradation to oxygen reduction. Different expressivity thresholds were used for peptide metabolism and terminal oxidases based on the observation that expressivity tends to be in the 25–50% range for archaea that are known to be aerobic (that is, Halobacteriales).

For prediction of aerobic capacity in Promethearchaeota genomes, classifiers were trained using a dataset of strains with annotated aerobic capacity derived from BacDive105. Strains were categorized as obligate aerobes, diazotrophic aerobes, facultative (an)aerobes and obligate anaerobes (with and without Cox). For classification, obligate aerobes, diazotrophic aerobes and facultative (an)aerobes were grouped as organisms with aerobic capacity, whereas obligate anaerobes were grouped as lacking aerobic capacity. Host-associated and phototrophic organisms (including oxygenic and anoxygenic phototrophs such as purple and green bacteria) were excluded, as host-associated oxygen gradients and light-driven metabolism can decouple oxygen exposure from intrinsic respiratory strategies, thereby confounding the relationship between genomic features and metabolic lifestyle. BacDive annotations were manually curated based on literature information and genomic annotation.

For genomic annotation, strains annotated as obligate aerobes in BacDive but encoding pathways for anaerobic respiration (that is, dissimilatory nitrate reduction or sulfate reduction) were reclassified as facultative anaerobes. Dissimilatory nitrate reduction and sulfate reduction were annotated based on the presence of genes with the following PFAM domains: Molybdopterin, Molydop_binding, Nitr_red_alph_N or NapB for dissimilatory nitrate reductases, and APS_reductase_C for adenylyl-sulfate reductase. Diazotrophy was annotated for isolates harbouring genes containing the PFAM domain Oxidored_nitro.

Strains classified as obligate anaerobes without experimental evidence of growth under aerobic conditions were excluded. Likewise, strains classified as facultative anaerobes without experimental evidence of enhanced growth under aerobic conditions were removed as they may rather be oxygen-tolerant anaerobes.

For each genome, OFOR and ODH abundance was calculated as log((n + 1)/genome size (Mb)), where n corresponds to the number of OFOR or ODH homologues. Genes encoding OFOR and ODH were annotated based on PFAM annotations generated by eggNOG-mapper (v.2.1.2)95. OFORs were identified based on the presence of PFAM domain combinations ‘POR,POR_N’, ‘PFOR_II,POR_N’, ‘POR_N,TPP_enzyme_C’ or ‘PFOR_II,TPP_enzyme_C’. OFORs containing three domains were annotated using hmmsearch and a domain e-value cutoff of 0.00001 and domain coverage cut-off of 0.8. ODH abundance was estimated based on the number of annotated E1 (decarboxylase) subunits (PFAM E1_dh), which determines the substrate specificity and serves as a proxy for the presence of 2-oxoacid dehydrogenase complexes. Genomic frequencies were calculated for the five most discriminative amino acids reported previously61, as these capture the majority of the discriminatory signal while reducing redundancy among correlated amino acid frequencies.

Classification of aerobic capacity based on OFOR and ODH abundance or amino acid frequency was performed using LR and LDA implemented in scikit-learn (v.1.8.0). Features were standardized using z-score normalization before model training. LR was performed with balanced class weights to account for class imbalance. Models were evaluated using stratified k-fold cross-validation (StratifiedKFold; k = 5), in which the dataset was partitioned into training and test sets while preserving the proportion of organisms with and without aerobic capacity in each split. Predictions were generated using cross_val_predict, ensuring that each sample was evaluated in a held-out fold. Classification performance was assessed using accuracy and balanced accuracy based on these out-of-fold predictions. For each genome, posterior probabilities were obtained from the fitted models, and the maximum posterior probability was used as a measure of classification confidence. For Promethearchaeota, only genomes with ≥85% completeness and ≤5% contamination were analysed, with two Hodarchaeales MAGs encoding Cox and Complex I (JBSWRZ01) or Cox, Complex I and Complex III (JBSWRG01) as exceptions.

To quantify the similarity between metabolic categories, the centroid of each category was calculated as the mean of the normalized OFOR abundance and normalized ODH abundance across all genomes in that category. Pairwise distances between category centroids were first computed using Euclidean distance in the two-dimensional feature space: dij = ((xi − xj)2 + (yi − yj)2)0.5, where x and y represent the mean values of normalized OFOR abundance and normalized ODH abundance for categories i and j. These distances quantify the separation between metabolic regimes in the feature space and were visualized as a centroid distance matrix.

Moreover, Mahalanobis distances were calculated to account for the covariance structure of the data. For each pair of category centroids i and j, the Mahalanobis distance was computed as: Dij = (mui – muj)T × Σ–1 × (mui – muj), where mui and muj are the centroid vectors of the two categories and Σ is the pooled covariance matrix estimated from all genomes in the feature space.

To examine archaea specifically, ODH and OFOR abundances were also comparatively analysed for archaeal isolates. Although archaea capable of aerobic growth often use ferredoxin as an electron carrier, such organisms nearly universally conserve a unique OFOR annotatable with three PFAM domains (PFOR_II, POR_N and POR; domain e-value ≤ 0.0001 and domain coverage ≥ 80% based on hmmsearch) (334 out of 340 analysed isolates; Supplementary Table 18). The abundances of ODH and other OFORs were calculated for each archaeal isolate as described above. Promethearchaeota were compared with mesophilic archaea as, for thermophiles, increased temperature decreases oxygen solubility and may therefore influence the interaction between metabolism and oxygen.
