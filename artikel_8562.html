This used to be my day job. The IRS agents I worked with didn’t think this was a valid argument. Now it has been a few years since I have done this so maybe a new directive has come out.
The flip side of that question would be on what did those agents base their opinion that two ETFs following different indexes might be substantially identical?

The way I've been approaching it, is that if the two funds are following different indexes they are likely not substantially identical for any number of reasons. Number of holdings was mentioned as one difference.

I think an argument could be made for how the fund actually tracks the index. For a large index Fund A could use a full replication of the index, where Fund B could use a representative sampling. The representative sampling could be rules based and therefore be considered passive, but how the funds replicate the index could make them enough different that the argument could be made they are not substantially identical. However, even if I think that argument could be made, I don't want to be the one trying to make it. So I stick to just using ETFs that track different indexes.

The law states “substantially identical stock or securities”. It doesn't say "a securities or stock that which has substantially identical performance. I've felt that as a taxpayer, the burden would be to show that the two ETFs are different, not that their performance is different.

An interesting case could be SCHK and VONE. They follow similar indexes, but not the same index. The rules for the underlying indexes are similar, but not identical. The two ETFs have about a 93% overlap by cap-weighting. Is a 93% overlap "substantially identical"? I wouldn't think so, but I also wouldn't want the IRS to ask.
