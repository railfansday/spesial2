In July 2023, a high-altitude porter named Muhammad Hassan fell at the Bottleneck, one of the most dangerous sections of K2. Drone footage later showed a long line of climbers moving past him toward the summit for three hours before he died (Benavides, 2023).

Each of those climbers brought a different history, perspective, and set of values. One climber might have walked past Mr. Hassan with a clear understanding of the complications of a rescue, and a sense that, while sad, death was a risk they all signed up for. Another might experience profound guilt, shame, and anger.

Say this second climber begins to escape or avoid this suffering. They start using substances, isolate, and ruminate on what they should have done differently. They begin to struggle at work. The spiral of moral pain and survival behaviors keeps building, like a second avalanche tumbling down K2. This avalanche is moral injury.

The term "moral injury" was born on the battlefield. Psychiatrist Jonathan Shay (1994) coined it in his work with Vietnam veterans, and Litz and colleagues (2009) later formalized it as the lasting psychological, biological, spiritual, behavioral, and social impact of "perpetrating, failing to prevent, bearing witness to, or learning about acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations" (p. 697). I know this pain firsthand. I served as a USMC officer in combat, and for sixteen years I have treated Veterans suffering from moral injury.

During my years as an elite mountain biker, sport brought moral dilemmas of its own. Should I have stopped to help a fellow competitor when it cost me the top of the podium? Did I violate my personal values when I hurled myself off the "In-N-Out Burger" rock slab in Squamish and busted up my bike and body? Those questions led me to work with elite and professional athletes whose drive to win led them to violate a deeply held value.

Every sport carries a moral code (implicit or explicit). Jonathan Haidt (2012) describes moral foundations shared across cultures, including care, fairness, loyalty, and respect for legitimate authority. Sport activates every one of these: caring for a partner on the rope, expecting a fair race, staying loyal to the team, trusting the coach.

Moral injury begins with a potentially morally injurious event (PMIE): a high-stakes situation that violates a deeply held value. Some PMIEs are transgressions. Others are betrayals. A PMIE could be:

Injuring a competitor on purpose. In "Bountygate," the NFL found the New Orleans Saints paid players to injure opponents, including a bounty on Brett Favre in the January 2010 NFC Championship Game (Yasinskas, 2013).

When moral pain after a PMIE is escaped or avoided, it can lead to significant psychological impairment. Moral injury, the cycle of moral pain and avoidance, is even linked to suicidality (Griffin et al., 2019). Moral pain can avalanche, and we need to interrupt that slide.

Moral pain has to be faced. Often we need support to do this. Moral pain carries information about what you value, and you can use it as a guide. As Steve Hayes (2019) puts it, "We hurt where we care."

When guilt is the heaviest weight, Trauma-Informed Guilt Reduction (TrIGR) can help examine guilt honestly (Norman et al., 2014).

With the adaptive guilt that remains, you get to act on it to repair when possible, make amends, or honor the violated value, moving forward with new resolve and commitment.

The arena of sport can be high-stakes and will continually present value conflicts. We can reflect on our moral pain, resurrect our values, and move forward wiser.

What would it look like to let your moral pain guide your next step on the mountain, on the field, or at the starting line?
