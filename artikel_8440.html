Control Your Risk
1) Read the Wiki article for Assessing Risk Tolerance, take the Vanguard Investor Questionnaire, then tailor the asset allocation (AA) that was recommended by the quiz based on your knowledge of your personal risk tolerance having read the Wiki article.

2) Alternatively (or in addition to), ask "How much of a drop in portfolio value as a % of total value can I handle?" cut that % in half to get standard deviation, then lookup that std. dev. on the X-Axis of the chart below, and finally scan up to see what AA that corresponds to. As an example, if you can only stomach a -24% drop in portfolio value, that's a ±12% std. dev, which corresponds to an AA of 60/40. The return you get is an average and you'll get what you get with your unique sequence of returns (there's a lot of variance in outcomes due to the associated volatility of stocks so it probably will NOT be the average, but something more or less).

a. For a long time-frame (>10 years) AAs below 20% stock are dominated (red dots) by another AA with similar risk but higher reward (blue dots).
b. The dotted line represents a hypothetical linear risk-reward from 100% stocks down to 100% bonds; the historical risk-reward curve has an improvement for risk-adjusted return due to the lack of correlation between stocks & bonds.

3) Vanguard's Target Date Retirement Glide-Path Design is a also a good starting point for choosing an AA. The choice of a specific TDF year should not solely be based on age, but could be earlier or later than your expected retirement age based on your personal risk-tolerance. You're not necessarily picking a specific TDF fund, you're just getting an idea of how to change your AA over time to apply to your specific fund choices.

For a defined benefit over N years, you can calculate the Present Value of the annuity payments and use that $ amount as a bond in your portfolio (see the Journal of Accountancy legacy article on Valuing an Annuity). I don't like that approach and don't recommend it because you can't sell this bond (to spend it) nor can you use it to rebalance your portfolio (can't buy as well as can't sell). It's a separate income stream that you don't control, and it certainly counts towards your total retirement income, but it's not a phantom bond in one's portfolio in my opinion. So I use it as an offset to the portfolio draw (which in the case of CALSTRS is maybe not a permanent offset?).

I could run my Withdrawal Monte Carlo (MC) model, but I'm hesitant to do so without the following:
a) a more verified estimate of retirement expenses;
b) a $ value for the two pensions, as well as her SocSec and when you're both claiming that, and whether or not they're inflation-adjusted each year in retirement; and
c) Desired life-expectancy for planning purposes (typically 95 but if either has a family history of shorter/longer lifespans you might want to use something other than age 95).

All that info will improve the accuracy of the MC inputs, but MCs always produce a large range of outcomes, so then there's the risk-acceptance level you want to choose for planning (you'll probably get something near the median/50th percentile, but it's likely better to plan on a bad sequence of returns and use 10th or 20th and rejoice when you actually get something better, rather than plan on 50th and have a 50% chance of having to cut retirement spending to remain solvent for the remainder of the period).

On getting a verification check against your budgeted expenses in retirement, start with @KlangFool's formula to estimate your current expenses: Annual Expenses = Gross Income (1040, Line 9) - Taxes (1040, Line 24 + State + Local + SocSec + Medicare) - Annual Savings (Roth 401k, Roth IRA & Taxable). Note that suggested accounting for annual savings does not include deductible Trad 401k/IRAs because those are already accounted for in 1040, Line 1 (from W-2, Box 1) which feeds Line 9. Then adjust that by what will change in retirement (retirement savings and SocSec/Medicare taxes go away, but savings for home repair/upgrade, new cars, gifts to children remain; principal & interest eventually go away when mortgage is paid off, but property tax & hazard insurance remain; commuting costs might go down but vacation spending might go up; term life insurance payments might go away but costs for long-term care in late retirement might go up).

The beauty of Klang's formula is that if you take out taxes and known savings you are spending all the rest of it. We've seen posters that had a detailed budget in a spreadsheet or phone app and were surprised to see Klang's formula show them as much as $50K of unaccounted spending!
You can tailor where taxes get accounted for (e.g., you could just consider it all as "retirement savings" or "lumpy expenses"), just make sure all your expenses are accounted for somewhere among taxes, savings, and monthly expenses. Don't forget stuff that gets deducted from your paycheck besides income taxes like health insurance, life insurance, pension contributions, etc.

Again, I'm happy to help run a MC sim or a TPAW run, but you need to prove more details on retirement age for each, and expected "other income" streams ($/yr and when that age they kick in).

That 3.7% initial draw with adjustment for inflation thereafter is a Const-$ withdrawal strategy per the Trinity Study. While it can feel good to have a 90% likelihood of being able to draw an ever-increasing amount from the portfolio for at least 35 years, there are alternative withdrawal methods that allow you to spend more during your lifetime (leaving a smaller residual to heirs/charity) such as Variable Percentage Withdrawal (VPW) or the TPAW Planner's Amortization Based Withdrawal (ABW is is recalculated each year by TPAW, so also a variable strategy). If discretionary is say at least 1/3 of total expenses, then VPW or ABW might be a better strategy for you.

That 10th percentile result represents a bad SOR (e.g., multiple years with bad returns, perhaps as low as a -60% crash). The uncertainty in projected balance tends to grow exponentially with the duration of the accumulation/withdrawal period (the longer you're saving or withdrawing the bigger the variance between the 10th and 90th percentiles). However, if some/most of your spending in retirement is discretionary then VPW or ABW withdrawal strategies largely mitigate the SOR risk (in exchange for a cut in spending which reduces inflation-adjusted purchasing power, but you'll never run out of money early with VPW and it's much less likely to run out with ABW vs about 10% risk of running out early with Const-$).

Standard caveats about simulations apply. Those using 30y rolling periods suffer from small sample size for estimating a binomial proportion as the success rate and can potentially indicate counter-intuitive results (e.g., implying higher stock exposure will reduce risk of running out early; FICalc was showing this and it's probably just an anomaly of the specific 30y rolling periods, skewed by the latest 15 years having phenomenal stock returns). Those using a large sample size as random draws from a distribution assume the distribution is constant over the withdrawal period. All sims suffer from assuming the future looks like the past in one way or another.
