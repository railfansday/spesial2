Kona Mechanics’ View of the Ironman World Championship

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

On the Thursday before the 2026 Ironman World Championship, I was cruising down Aliʻi Drive around Kaliua-Kona, Hawaii, after a delicious lunch at Clover and Mug Coffee when it struck me that there weren’t many triathletes out biking and running. Over the next six miles, I saw a total of only 15 athletes (two of whom may have just been locals on an afternoon run).

Then I turned onto Palani Rd. and was greeted by the sight of around 30 athletes with pink, turquoise, and a few neon yellow wristbands wheeling their bikes up and down the hill. As I pulled into the parking lot, I quickly learned where all the Ironman athletes were – at Bike Works Kona, our town’s one and only dedicated triathlon shop.

After finding a parking spot, I tried to fight my way through the stream of jittery athletes and into the shop. I settled in to chat with Taylor Bushong, a traveling race mechanic who had a pop-up work stand on the shop’s lanai, about the crazy things he’d seen during the week of the 2026 Ironman World Championship.

It begins with the absurdly catastrophic: “Someone brought in a bike for us to build and it had two front wheels. How do you leave home with two front wheels? I blame it on taper brain,” said Bushong, speaking from personal experience as an Ironman Wisconsin 2025 finisher.

“Someone else brought in a bike with two rear wheels,” added Ivey Langley, a globe-trotting race mechanic. Could the two athletes combine their taper half brains to form one complete brain and one complete bike? Not quite, but fortunately, there happened to be a few wheels available for rent from the shop.

“Then there was the guy who needed us to fix his front derailleur. He insisted it would only take five minutes,” Bushong said. Like many of the repairs that the traveling mechanics and employees of Bike Works Kona tackle during race week, fixing the derailleur was not as simple as turning a screw or two. “I had to drill his frame and rivet something back into it. That’s really the hardest part of Ironman week – managing athletes’ timeline expectations. Managing the unrealistic ‘can you just’ requests. The understanding of the level of the request seems to be outside the scope of recognition.”

When time is of the essence, changing tires is a reasonable request but new chains are tricky, especially at 3 p.m. on Friday, three hours before the bike check-in cut-off. That’s when one athlete chose to seek help with a chain that was skipping after they replaced the old one.

“They failed to realize that if the old cassette is heavily worn, you’ll need a new cassette and a new chain because the new chain won’t sit properly in the worn grooves of the old cassette. When you pedal hard, the chain will skip over the teeth,” Langley said.

Replacing a chain can be tricky when there’s a tight deadline, but changing out wheels is a nightmare. And it often starts with a seemingly bright idea: Give your older, 11-speed bike an upgrade by borrowing Zipp wheels from a friend. If the friend rides a brand new, 12-speed Cervélo, however, you could run into trouble.

“One person came in on Friday [the day before race start] because their gears weren’t working after they changed out their race wheels,” Langley said. “ I had to explain that they had a 12-speed cassette on an 11-speed drivetrain and their gears were jumping because the spacing between cogs, the chain width, and the shifter indexing on an 11-speed drivetrain don’t match a 12-speed setup.”

The consensus among everyone I spoke to at Bike Works Kona was that Friday was the most chaotic day of Ironman week, demanding that the staff act as both professional mechanics and temporary therapists.

“There was one athlete who was on their way to check in his bike when the back tire just went flat. The tube he was putting on was not working so he came back in here. He was so worried that he wouldn’t make it to bike check-in that he started crying. I had to calm him down,” Bike Works’ Marc Grande told me once I finally made it through the shop’s door to the sales counter. “Sometimes they need new tubes or valve extenders. Other times, they just need someone to look at their bike and tell them that their bike is OK and there’s nothing to worry about.”

“But sometimes they need new bike computers,” added my husband, Patrick Stover, who helped at least three triathletes whose computers had popped off while they were on training rides during race week. “Often, it isn’t enough to click it into the mount. If you don’t want to buy a new Garmin the day before Ironman, use a tether or find another way to secure your bike computer.”

The most common issue that arose during race week was with tubeless tires. Triathlon has largely been moving toward tubeless tires and the appeal feels like a no-brainer: Tubeless tires with sealant are far less prone to flats than tubular tires — and you can run them at lower pressures for lower rolling resistance and increased comfort. But you have to know how to properly put sealant in those tires.

Or you can head to a bike shop in a panic before your race, like hundreds (literally, hundreds) of athletes did this year during Ironman World Championship week. There were tubeless tires that were bone dry. Somehow there were both tires lazily hanging off their rims, while others obstinately clung to their rims. It wasn’t pretty. And it wouldn’t happen if more of us learned how to add sealant to our tires and test out our tubeless repair kits earlier in the season.

Another common yet preventable problem? Gear that was lost or damaged because of poor packing practices. If you’re packing your bike yourself, don’t forget to take the CO2 cartridges out. If you do forget, the TSA is going to go through your bike case and “help” you repack. Spoiler alert: It rarely goes well.

“We’ve heard firsthand from athletes whose cases were opened by TSA. One lost a shoe. The other lost a wheel. Save yourself the headache and buy your CO2 canisters once you get to Kona,” said high-performance technical director at Breakthrough Sports and lead mechanic for neutral support for pro athletes at the T100 Series Anthony Foy, who was on hand to help the Bike Works staff and traveling mechanics. “We’ve also seen shifters broken on the front end because people forget to remove the handlebar extensions when they pack their bikes.”

A handful of athletes visited the shop for help replacing their gear after their luggage was lost, including their race kit. You can’t control these things, but it helps to pack your goggles, race kit, bike and run shoes, and helmet in your carry-on. You really don’t want to be rushing around trying to replace those familiar touchpoints once you get to Kona for ostensibly the biggest race of your year (or life).

“One stressful situation involved a bike that came in just a few days before race day with several issues to sort out. We had to do quite a bit of troubleshooting to figure out what was going on. We ended up replacing several pinched wires in the aero bars and seat tube, and found that the Di2 battery had failed. … The damage looked consistent with an assembly issue, and we believe the customer assembled the bicycle themselves,” said Alex Flores, Bike Works’ service manager.

Other telltale signs of an athlete-assembled bike are loose aero bars and incorrectly mounted derailleurs and chains. “It’s a good reminder to have your bike assembled or checked by a mechanic well before [Ironman World Championship]. When your bike is essential to your race, a little preparation can help prevent stressful and costly surprises close to race day.”

But despite some forehead-slapping moments and unseen eye rolls, there’s something special about the triathlon world descending upon one place in time at a bike shop in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. There’s a universality to the experience, even if triathletes are speaking different languages. Fortunately, the Bike Works team during race week included mechanics who speak Spanish, Filipino, Japanese, and Swiss German, making the shop feel like a U.N. camp for those seeking mechanical refuge.

“It’s fun to see bikes and racing bring together people from so many different cultures,” added Stover.

And there can be perks: Last year, a triathlete left five carbon fiber wheels at the thrift shop by the Kona Transfer Station (ostensibly the town’s dump), which Stover bought for a grand total of $50. “The Zipp 808 front wheel alone was worth $500. It’s interesting to learn about where the athletes come from. It’s equally as interesting to see what they leave behind.”
