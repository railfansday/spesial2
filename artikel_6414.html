Want to lose weight? The type of protein you eat may matter

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

A new study links eating less animal protein and more plant protein to greater weight loss, but other dietary changes may also explain the results.

When you’re trying to lose weight, protein advice can turn meals into a daily tally of grams. A new study suggests there’s more to consider than the total.

Among adults with overweight who tried two diets, those who reduced animal protein the most and ate more plant protein tended to lose the most weight.

They also ate less fat and more carbohydrates, though, so the researchers couldn’t pin the weight loss on protein alone.

Hana Kahleova, director of clinical research at the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine, was the study’s senior author. The nonprofit, based in Washington, DC, ran the trial.

“Patients are constantly told to worry about getting enough protein,” Kahleova said.

The trial ran from February to October 2019 and involved 62 overweight adults in Washington. At the start, they weighed about 216 pounds (98 kilograms) on average.

About half began with a low-fat vegan diet and half with a Mediterranean diet. They followed their assigned plan for 16 weeks, took a four-week break, then spent another 16 weeks on the other diet.

On the vegan diet, volunteers ate vegetables, fruit, grains and legumes such as beans.

Meat, fish, dairy and eggs were off the menu, as were added fats and cooking oils.

The Mediterranean plan included plenty of vegetables, fruit, legumes, fish and nuts, with nearly four tablespoons (50 grams) of extra-virgin olive oil each day. The plan favored lean white meat over red meat.

Neither plan set a calorie limit. At four points in the trial, volunteers wrote down everything they ate for three days, and a registered dietitian analyzed the records.

In all, 52 people finished the full 36 weeks. An earlier report on this trial found that volunteers lost about 13 pounds (six kilograms) while eating the vegan diet, but their weight didn’t change reliably on the Mediterranean plan.

The new study revisited those food records, this time focusing on protein.

The question was whether changes in the amount and type of protein went along with changes on the scale.

On the vegan plan, animal protein fell from about 51 grams a day to fewer than three. Plant protein went up, from roughly 29 grams to 43 grams daily.

That extra plant protein didn’t replace all the animal protein people had given up. Total protein intake dropped by about 33 grams a day, from roughly 80 grams to 46.

So this wasn’t a straightforward exchange of one protein source for another. Volunteers ate less protein overall, which makes it difficult to separate the effects of the amount from the effects of its source.

The Mediterranean diet brought little change in either animal or plant protein. Total intake stayed near 73 grams a day.

For this comparison, the team used the first 16 weeks, when about half the volunteers were on each diet. They compared each person’s change in protein intake with that person’s change in weight.

Those who cut animal protein the most generally lost the most weight. The same pattern appeared among people who increased plant protein the most, and among those who made the largest cuts to total protein.

All three links were moderate. Each passed a stricter statistical check the researchers used to guard against chance findings.

“This doesn’t mean protein is the enemy,” Kahleova said. “It means the source matters.”

Protein is made of smaller molecules called amino acids, and foods contain different amounts of each one. Using the food records, the team estimated how much of each amino acid volunteers ate.

Volunteers ate less of every amino acid the researchers tracked on the vegan diet, with methionine dropping by more than half, from about 1.8 grams a day to 0.7 grams.

Lower intake of almost every amino acid went along with more weight loss.

Only four of those links passed the strictest statistical check: isoleucine, lysine, methionine and tyrosine.

Earlier research cited in the paper found less isoleucine, lysine and methionine in plant protein than in animal protein.

Methionine contains sulfur. In a 2024 trial cited by the authors, people on a vegan diet low in sulfur-containing amino acids lost about 20% more weight than a comparison group.

None of this tells us what would happen if people changed their protein source and left everything else alone. The volunteers’ meals changed in several ways at once.

On the vegan diet, fat supplied about 17% of daily calories, down from 38%. Carbohydrates went the other way, rising from 42% to 69% of calories.

Daily cholesterol intake fell by more than 270 milligrams, and each gram of food contained fewer calories. The authors also pointed to fiber and other nutrients as possible parts of the explanation.

Earlier analyses of the same volunteers also linked weight loss to other features of the vegan diet, including a score measuring the proportion of plant-based foods in their diets.

These are all correlations drawn from the same dietary changes, rather than separate proof of what caused the weight loss.

Food records depend on people remembering and accurately reporting what they ate, so errors are possible. About 78% of the volunteers were women, and the average age was 57.

They also all lived in one city. The findings may not hold for men, younger people or more diverse groups.

The Physicians Committee promotes plant-based diets and paid for the study. Three of the five authors work there, although the authors stated that the funder did not influence the results.

People lost weight while eating the vegan diet, but how much of that came from plant protein itself remains unknown.

The authors described the findings as a starting point for ideas to test, not proof.

They called for trials that change only the protein source while keeping calories, fat and fiber the same. That would give researchers a clearer way to tell whether protein deserves the credit.

The study is published in the journal BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health.
