‘Not your Silicon, not your keys’ — Ethereum’s Buterin calls for open-source chip design

Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin has come up with an amendment to crypto’s oldest security motto, arguing that key safety increasingly depends on open-source chip designs.

In a blog post titled A shallow dive into open-source chip design, published on 26th November, Buterin shed light on the importance of making hardware designs publicly accessible and verifiable. He also argued that while the crypto industry has focused on securing software, cryptographic protocols, and private keys, underlying hardware remains an overlooked security risk.

Even if wallet software is secure, compromised hardware could expose sensitive information, including private keys and seed phrases.

Given the case in point, Buterin believes that verifying how a computer operates ultimately requires examining its underlying hardware, including transistors, silicon, and electrical circuits.

Even when software is open-source and independently audited, users have limited visibility into what the underlying chip actually does.

Probably the most frustrating thing about chip design is how opaque and proprietary the whole thing is.

To explain this risk better, he took reference from an attack involving a malicious implant with an eSIM inserted into a hardware wallet before it reaches consumers. Once activated, the implant can exfiltrate the wallet’s seed phrase.

Using this example, he also warned that attackers could replace legitimate chips with nearly identical versions containing additional malicious features. These, in turn, could bypass software-level security checks.

Not only this, but Buterin also believes that publicly accessible hardware designs would allow independent researchers to verify whether chips perform their intended functions.

However, publishing a chip’s Verilog code alone is insufficient. He argued that meaningful verification requires openness down to the GDS level, which describes the actual physical geometry of a chip’s components.

He also highlighted Process Design Kits (PDKs), which contain manufacturing-specific information and design rules needed to produce and validate chips.

Buterin highlighted IRIS, a chip-inspection technique being developed by hardware researcher Bunnie, which uses optical imaging to examine chip structures.

More sophisticated attacks involving changes to doping levels may require destructive methods such as Focused Ion Beam-Scanning Electron Microscopy (FIB-SEM).

He also proposed designing chips for easier inspection by enabling independent testing of smaller components, reducing dependence on fragile configurations, and making physical layouts more inspection-friendly.

Rather than expecting consumers to inspect chips themselves, he envisions professional auditors, journalists, and civil society groups using specialized equipment to verify manufacturers’ claims.

Finally, Buterin acknowledged that performance-intensive sectors such as AI are unlikely to adopt open-source manufacturing entirely. Instead, he is expecting a divide between performance-focused and security-focused hardware, with the latter prioritizing transparency and verifiability over efficiency.

He concluded it best by extending crypto’s familiar motto, “not your keys, not your coins”, with a hardware-focused warning.
