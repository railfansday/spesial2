10 things to know about Record, Song and Album of the Year at the Grammys

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Record, Song and Album of the Year honor different work at the Grammys, and these 10 explainers cover who each award credits, who votes and how eligibility works

Three of the Grammys' biggest trophies have names that sound almost interchangeable. Record of the Year, Song of the Year and Album of the Year can read like three ways of saying the same thing. They are not. Each one honors a different kind of work, and each one sends a trophy to a different group of people.

The Recording Academy, which presents the Grammys, groups these awards with Best New Artist in what it calls the general field. Those four categories are the ones not tied to a single genre. A pop single, a country album and a hip-hop track can all compete in them.

The distinction comes down to what is being judged. Record of the Year looks at a finished recording — the performance and the way it was produced and engineered. Song of the Year looks at the composition underneath it, meaning the lyrics and melody. Album of the Year covers a full body of work rather than a single track.

That difference decides who walks away with a Grammy. A Record of the Year win is shared by the performing artist with the producers, recording engineers and mixers behind the track. Under current rules, the mastering engineer is credited too. A Song of the Year win goes to the songwriters, and a performer is included only if they also helped write it. An album award reaches the widest group, from the artist to the people who produced, engineered, mixed and mastered the record.

The split explains how a song can be honored for its writing while a different track takes the recording prize. At the 2024 ceremony, Billie Eilish's "What Was I Made For?" won Song of the Year. Record of the Year went to Miley Cyrus for "Flowers."

The difference between Record, Song and Album of the Year at the Grammys also carries into the rules on eligibility, voting and nominee counts that govern the general field.

The 10 explainers that follow cover each general-field category and how the eligibility window and voting work. They also track how the nominee lists have grown and look at two artists whose wins show the rules in practice.

Record of the Year honors a single recording — the finished track as listeners hear it. The Grammy goes to the performing artist, the producer, the recording engineer and the mixer. Current rules also credit the mastering engineer.

Of the general-field awards, this one focuses most directly on sound and performance. It judges how a track was sung, played, captured and shaped in the studio. Authorship of the words and melody is judged separately, by Song of the Year.

The list of recipients mirrors how a modern record gets made. The performing artist is the voice or act listeners associate with the track. The producer oversees the recording as a whole. That role guides arrangements, performances and creative choices from the first session to the final version.

The recording engineer handles the technical work of capturing the performance. That covers microphone choice and placement, signal flow and the quality of the raw tracks. Everything that follows in the process builds on those tracks.

The mixer takes the recorded parts and balances them into a finished whole. Mixing sets the relative volume of each voice and instrument. It also places sounds across the stereo field and adds effects such as reverb and compression.

The mastering engineer handles the last step before release. Mastering prepares the final mix for playback across formats and devices. It adjusts overall loudness, tone and consistency. The goal is a track that holds together on any playback system.

Crediting all of these roles means a single Record of the Year win is shared by everyone in that production chain. The category puts the names of producers and engineers next to the artist's in one of the four general-field honors.

The award is tied to a specific recording, so the track is the unit being judged, not the tune behind it. A cover, live version or remix of the same song is a separate recording. Each version has its own performers and its own production credits.

Album of the Year honors a complete album rather than a single track. The trophy goes to the artist and to the producers, engineers, mixers and mastering engineers who worked on the record.

One win can send trophies to many people at once. It is one of four general-field Grammys. Voters weigh the album as a single release, from its opening track to its closing one.

The credit list mirrors the way an album gets made. A producer guides the recording sessions and shapes the overall sound. That can mean choosing arrangements, coaching performances and deciding which takes make the final cut.

A recording engineer captures the performances. The job covers microphone choice and placement, signal levels and the technical quality of each take.

A mixer blends the separate parts — vocals, instruments and effects — into finished tracks. Mixing sets how loud each element sits, where it is placed in the stereo field and how it is shaped with equalization and effects.

A mastering engineer handles the last step before release. On an album, mastering matches loudness and tone across the tracks. It also sets the spacing between songs so the record plays as one sequence.

A full album can involve several people in each of those roles. One producer might handle a few songs while another oversees the rest. Different engineers and mixers can work on different tracks. The Recording Academy's rules for each awards cycle decide which of those contributors are named as recipients.

Featured artists may also be credited, depending on their role on the album. A guest appearance on one track does not by itself make someone an Album of the Year recipient. The rules look at the part a featured artist played on the record.

The name on the cover belongs to the performer. The list of recipients reflects the studio work behind that name, from the first session to the final master.

Song of the Year at the Grammys honors the composition itself — the lyrics and melody — rather than any particular performance of it. The trophy goes to the songwriters. A performer is credited only when that performer also helped write the song.

That rule separates Song of the Year from Record of the Year, the award with the most similar name. A track can stand out for its vocal, its arrangement or its production. Those qualities belong to the recording. Song of the Year voters judge something narrower: the words and the tune as written.

The category has been part of the Grammys since the first ceremony in 1959. Its basic purpose has stayed the same. It is the general-field award aimed at the people who write music, whether or not they ever step in front of a microphone.

The 2024 ceremony offers a clear case. "What Was I Made For?" won Song of the Year that February. Billie Eilish and her brother Finneas O'Connell wrote it for the 2023 film Barbie. Both are credited songwriters. That is why both received the award. Eilish's role as the singer was not the reason she was honored in this category. Her co-writing credit was.

The same song also won the Academy Award for Best Original Song in March 2024. That Oscar category likewise goes to songwriters. The two wins recognized the same piece of writing.

The 2003 ceremony shows the opposite situation. Norah Jones sang "Don't Know Why," but Jesse Harris wrote it. Harris won Song of the Year for the track. Jones did not share that award because she was not a writer on the song. Her recording of it won Record of the Year that night instead.

That split means a singer can perform the winning song and still leave without this particular trophy. It also means a songwriter who never appears on stage can win one of the Grammys' four general-field awards.

The category also reflects how the music business divides ownership. A composition and a recording of it are separate pieces of property. Songwriters and their publishers hold rights in the composition. Song of the Year honors that side of the work.

Billie Eilish's "Bad Guy" won both Record of the Year and Song of the Year at the 2020 Grammys. One track earned one trophy for the way it was recorded and a second for the way it was written.

The double win came at the 62nd Grammy Awards, held in Los Angeles on January 26, 2020. Each prize went to a different set of credits. The recording award covered the finished track. The songwriting award covered only the words and melody underneath it.

Eilish wrote "Bad Guy" with her brother, Finneas O'Connell, who performs as Finneas. The two siblings shared the Song of the Year trophy as its songwriters.

The Record of the Year credits reached further. Eilish received the award as the performing artist. O'Connell was credited as producer and also as an engineer and mixer, alongside mixer Rob Kinelski. John Greenham was credited as the mastering engineer. O'Connell therefore collected trophies in both categories for the same track. One was for writing it, and the other was for making the recording.

The song was made outside a commercial studio. The siblings wrote and recorded its parent album, When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go?, in O'Connell's bedroom at their family home in Los Angeles. The track's sparse bass line, whispered vocal and abrupt tempo change near the end are production choices. Those elements belong to the recording. The lyrics and melody belong to the song.

"Bad Guy" had a long commercial run before the ceremony. It reached No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 in August 2019. That ended the 19-week run of Lil Nas X $TWTR's "Old Town Road" at the top of the chart.

The two wins were part of a larger result that night. Eilish also won Album of the Year and Best New Artist, taking all four general-field awards at a single ceremony. Christopher Cross was the previous artist to do so, at the 1981 Grammys. Eilish was 18 years old when she matched him.

Best New Artist honors a performer or group rather than a single recording, song or album. It recognizes an artist whose breakthrough into public awareness came during the Grammys' eligibility period. It sits alongside Record, Album and Song of the Year in the general field.

That focus on the artist sets it apart from the other three top awards. Record of the Year, Album of the Year and Song of the Year each reward a specific piece of work. Their credits can stretch to producers, engineers or songwriters. Best New Artist goes to the act itself, whether a solo performer or a band. A producer or songwriter who worked on a nominee's music receives no trophy in this category.

Bobby Darin won Best New Artist at the 1960 ceremony, the first time the category was presented. The Beatles took it home in 1965, the year after their first visit to the U.S.

New does not mean a debut release. The Recording Academy writes its criteria for the category into the rulebook it issues each cycle. Those criteria have been revised over the years. Artists are judged on when they broke through, so an act with earlier releases can still qualify. The specific thresholds can change from one cycle to the next. Anyone tracking a contender should check the rules for that year.

Milli Vanilli won Best New Artist at the 1990 ceremony. The Recording Academy withdrew the award later that year. The withdrawal followed the revelation that the duo had not sung the lead vocals on their recordings.

A newcomer can also collect the other general-field trophies on the same night. Christopher Cross became the first artist to win all four general-field awards in a single night, at the 1981 ceremony.

Recent winners show the range of genres the category covers. Jazz singer Samara Joy won at the 2023 ceremony. R&B singer Victoria Monét won in 2024, and pop singer Chappell Roan won in 2025.

The general field is the group of four Grammy categories open to music from every genre. Those categories are Record of the Year, Album of the Year, Song of the Year and Best New Artist.

Genre categories, by contrast, consider only work within one style of music, such as rap, country or jazz.

That split shapes how the full awards list is organized. The Recording Academy sorts its categories into fields. Most fields are built around a genre or a family of related genres. Pop, rock, R&B, rap, country, jazz, Latin, gospel and classical music each have their own set of awards.

Inside a genre field, categories compare like with like. A rap album competes against other rap albums for Best Rap Album. A country album competes against other country albums for Best Country Album. The question in those races is narrower: how a recording stands within its own tradition.

The general field removes that boundary. Nominees there are weighed against the full range of music released during the eligibility period, not against a single style. A jazz record and a rock album can end up competing for the same trophy.

The two paths are not exclusive. One recording can be nominated in its genre categories and in the general field in the same year. An album might earn a nomination for Best Pop Vocal Album alongside a nomination for Album of the Year. A win in one category does not guarantee a win in the other. Each category is decided on its own.

Genre fields also tend to break a style into smaller pieces. Many of them separate performance, song and album awards. Some go further and split solo work from duo or group work, as pop does with Best Pop Duo/Group Performance. That structure gives musicians within each style several separate categories to compete in, compared with the four slots in the general field.

The difference is visible on Grammy night itself. Most genre awards are handed out at the Premiere Ceremony, which takes place before the main broadcast. The general-field awards are usually held for the televised show. That placement puts Record, Song and Album of the Year, along with Best New Artist, on the main telecast.

A recording can compete for a Grammy only if it was released during the eligibility period. The Recording Academy sets this window for each awards cycle. The window, not the calendar year, decides which ceremony a song or album enters.

The window applies across the competition, including Record, Album and Song of the Year. The Recording Academy publishes each cycle's dates alongside its rules for that year. Best New Artist uses the same period differently. That award looks at whether an artist's breakthrough came during the window, rather than at a single release.

A release that falls outside one window is not lost. It is considered in the cycle whose window it does fall into. An album that misses one cutoff can still compete the following year.

Every entry in a cycle is measured against the same window. A record released near the window's opening and one released just before it closes can compete in the same category. By the time of the ceremony, one may have been in circulation far longer than the other.

The window closes before the ceremony takes place. Once it closes, the pool of eligible music for that cycle is fixed. Nominations, voting and the awards show all come after that close. The ceremony therefore honors music released earlier. The year attached to a ceremony is not necessarily the year the winning music came out.

That timing explains a common source of confusion for listeners. A widely heard release can seem absent from a year's list of nominees. If it came out after that cycle's window closed, it has not been passed over. It moves to the next cycle instead.

The exact dates can differ from one year to the next. Anyone checking whether a specific release qualifies needs the rules the Recording Academy published for that cycle, not the rules from an earlier year.

Grammy winners are chosen by voting members of the Recording Academy, the organization that presents the awards. Those members can vote in the general field and in a limited number of genre fields.

That setup makes the Grammys a peer award. The people deciding are music professionals rather than the public, critics or sales charts. Streaming totals and radio play are not part of the count. A single can top the charts for months and still lose to a smaller release if voting members prefer the other recording.

Voting membership is tied to professional work in recorded music. To qualify, a person needs credits on commercially released recordings in a creative or technical role. Members include performers, songwriters, producers and engineers. Those are the same roles credited across the four general-field categories. The Academy also has members who belong without voting rights. Membership alone does not guarantee a ballot.

The process runs in two rounds. The first round starts with entries submitted for consideration. Members vote on those entries to choose the nominees in each category. In the final round, they vote again on the nominees to pick the winners. Those results are kept sealed until the awards are announced.

The cap on genre fields is meant to keep each genre decision with people who work in that style. A jazz engineer and a country songwriter can both vote for Album of the Year. Each is expected to vote in the genre categories closest to their own work. The general field stays open to every voting member. That makes it the category group with the widest pool of judges. It also means a producer can help pick Song of the Year, even though the award itself goes only to songwriters.

Individual ballots stay private. The Academy publishes nominees and winners. It does not release vote totals or margins. The public never learns how close a race was.

The Grammys' general-field categories, including Record, Album and Song of the Year, have changed the length of their nominee lists over time. Each category once had five nominee slots. The Recording Academy later raised that number to eight, then to 10.

The change was not limited to the three headline awards. Best New Artist belongs to the same general field, and its shortlist went through the same expansion. That kept all four general-field shortlists the same length.

A nominee slot is one place on a category's final ballot. Voting members choose the winner from that list. Its length sets how many recordings, albums, songs or new acts are still in contention on Grammy night.

The first expansion, from five to eight, added three places in each category. The second, from eight to 10, added two more. At 10 slots, each shortlist was twice the size of the original five.

A longer Song of the Year list is still a list of compositions. A longer Record of the Year list is still a list of recordings. The expansion changed how many entries reach the final ballot. It did not change what each category judges or who can receive the trophy.

The number of slots is a rule, not a fixed feature of the awards. The Recording Academy writes the rules for each awards cycle. The nominee count is one of those rules, and it has been revised more than once.

That history affects comparisons between ceremonies. Two Album of the Year races from different eras may have shortlists of different lengths. A nomination in a five-slot year meant making a list half as long as one with 10 slots. An artist's general-field nominations across a long career can come from lists of different lengths. The official nominee list for a given ceremony shows how many slots applied that year.

Taylor Swift has won Album of the Year four times, more than any other artist in Grammy history. Her winning albums are Fearless, 1989, Folklore and Midnights, and the fourth win came at the February 2024 ceremony.

Fearless was the first. It won at the ceremony held in January 2010, when Swift was 20 years old. That made her the youngest artist to win the category up to that point. Billie Eilish later took that distinction at the 2020 ceremony. She was 18 when When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go? won the award.

Fearless was released on Big Machine Records, a Nashville label, and was promoted as a country album. Swift co-produced it with Nathan Chapman, who had worked with her since her debut album.

1989 followed at the February 2016 ceremony. The album marked her move from country to mainstream pop. Max Martin and Shellback were among its producers. The win made Swift the first woman to take Album of the Year twice as a lead artist.

Folklore won at the ceremony held in March 2021. The Recording Academy had pushed that year's show back from its planned January date because of the COVID-19 pandemic. Swift made the album largely remotely with Aaron Dessner of the National and with Jack Antonoff. It was released with little advance notice in July 2020. The award made her the first woman to win the category three times as a lead artist.

Midnights brought the fourth trophy. Swift co-produced it with Antonoff, and it was released in October 2022. That win moved her past Frank Sinatra, Stevie Wonder and Paul Simon. Each of them had won Album of the Year three times.

The gap between her first and fourth wins is 14 years. Her four winners also span several styles. Fearless is rooted in country, and 1989 is built on mainstream pop. Folklore leans on spare indie-folk arrangements, while Midnights returns to synth-driven pop.
