10 marathon medical emergencies, from heat stroke to cardiac arrest

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

These 10 marathon medical emergencies, from heat stroke and hyponatremia to cardiac arrest and blisters, cover what race medical teams treat and the warning signs runners should know

A marathon asks thousands of people to hold a hard effort for about 42 kilometers (26.2 miles), and most of them stay on the course for several hours. Organizers treat a big-city race as a temporary public health operation as well as a sporting event. They staff aid stations along the route, send medical responders out onto the course and set up a medical tent near the finish line.

Most of what race medics see is minor. Blisters, chafing and cramps are routine complaints at aid stations and in the marathon medical tent. A smaller group of marathon medical emergencies can be life-threatening, and the outcome often depends on how quickly someone recognizes the problem.

The difficulty is that several dangerous conditions look alike at first. A runner who becomes confused or collapses near the finish could have heat stroke. The same runner could instead have dangerously low blood sodium from drinking too much fluid. Or the runner may have fainted because blood pressure dropped once the legs stopped moving. Each possibility calls for a different response. Cooling treats the first, and extra fluid can make the second worse. The third often needs little more than lying down with the legs raised.

Weather changes the picture too. Warm, humid race days raise the risk of heat illness, although heat stroke can also strike at high effort in cool conditions. Cold, wet or windy days bring the risk of hypothermia. Cold, dry air can also narrow the airways of runners with asthma. Underlying heart disease, training load and pace also shape who runs into trouble on race day.

The 10 conditions below are a selection presented in no particular order. They are not ranked by how often they occur or by how dangerous they are. They run from the routine to the rare, and from skin-deep problems to cardiac arrest.

Each slide explains what the condition is, the warning signs runners and spectators can watch for and how race medical teams typically manage it. The information is general. It is not a substitute for advice from a doctor, particularly for runners with a known heart, lung or kidney condition.

Exertional heat stroke is a core body temperature above about 40°C (104°F) combined with signs of brain dysfunction, such as confusion or collapse. It is life-threatening. Race medical teams treat it on the spot by cooling the runner first and arranging transport second.

The condition develops when working muscles generate heat faster than the body can shed it. A runner holding a hard pace for hours produces a large, steady heat load. Sweating is the body's main way to release that heat. Sweat cools the skin only when it evaporates, and humid air slows evaporation.

Brain-related symptoms separate heat stroke from heat exhaustion. A runner with heat exhaustion may feel weak, dizzy or nauseated and may be drenched in sweat. That runner still thinks clearly. A runner with heat stroke may become confused, agitated, combative or unresponsive. Some stagger or weave across the course. Others act irrationally or simply collapse. Medical teams treat any change in mental state in a hot, exhausted runner as possible heat stroke until they can rule it out.

Accurate temperature measurement matters. Oral, ear and forehead readings can be misleading after intense exercise. For that reason, well-equipped marathon medical tents use rectal thermometers to confirm core temperature in a collapsed or confused runner.

Cold-water immersion is the preferred treatment at events that can set up ice baths. Staff place the runner in a tub of ice water up to the neck. They support the head and monitor temperature continuously. Water draws heat away from the body much faster than air does. Where a tub is not available, medics use alternatives such as rotating ice-soaked towels over the body. The guiding principle is that cooling cannot wait for an ambulance ride. The harm from heat stroke depends on how long the body stays dangerously hot.

Warm, humid weather raises the risk most. Many race organizers track the wet bulb globe temperature, a reading that combines heat, humidity, sunlight and wind. They use it to display color-coded flags that warn runners as conditions worsen. Cool weather does not rule the condition out. A runner pushing at a very high effort can overheat on a mild day, especially in a hard finishing sprint. A lack of heat acclimatization can also raise a runner's vulnerability. So can a recent illness, such as a fever or a stomach bug.

Exercise-associated hyponatremia is a blood sodium level below 135 mmol/L. It develops mainly when a runner drinks more fluid than the body loses. It is a separate problem from dehydration, and extra fluid can make it worse.

Sodium helps control how water moves between the blood and the body's cells. When a runner takes in more water than sweat, breath and urine remove, the surplus dilutes the sodium already in the bloodstream. Sweat does carry some sodium out of the body. The main driver, however, is the excess water rather than the salt that is lost.

Healthy kidneys normally clear extra water quickly. During long, strenuous exercise, the body can release antidiuretic hormone, which signals the kidneys to hold on to water. That limits how fast a runner can shed a fluid surplus while still on the course.

The risk is higher in slower runners. Someone finishing in five or six hours spends far longer on the course than an elite athlete. That leaves more time to drink. A gentler pace also generally produces less sweat. Runners who stop to drink at every aid station out of habit can build up a large surplus over those hours.

The early signs are easy to mistake for ordinary race fatigue. They include nausea, headache and a bloated feeling. Some runners notice swollen hands or a ring that suddenly feels tight. As sodium falls further, water shifts into brain cells. That can cause confusion, seizures and dangerous brain swelling.

Body weight offers a useful clue. A runner who weighs more at the finish than at the start has taken in more fluid than they lost. That gain is a recognized warning sign of hyponatremia.

Treatment runs opposite to the usual instinct. Water or a standard intravenous drip can push sodium even lower in an affected runner. Medical teams at larger races may check blood sodium with a rapid test before deciding what to do. Mild cases are usually managed by stopping fluids and letting the kidneys clear the excess. Severe cases with confusion or seizures need hospital-level care. There, doctors may give a concentrated salt solution known as hypertonic saline.

Prevention centers on how much a runner drinks, not what. Drinking according to thirst, rather than on a fixed schedule, is common guidance for distance runners. Sports drinks contain sodium, but at a much lower concentration than blood. Drinking too much of them can still lead to hyponatremia.

Sudden cardiac arrest is rare compared with the number of people who run marathons, but it is the most serious emergency a race medical team can face. Immediate CPR and early use of a defibrillator are the factors that matter most for survival.

Cardiac arrest is not the same as a heart attack. A heart attack happens when blood flow to part of the heart muscle is blocked. Cardiac arrest is an electrical failure in which the heart stops pumping blood effectively. A heart attack can trigger cardiac arrest, but the two terms describe different events.

A 2012 study in the New England Journal of Medicine by Kim and colleagues measured how often cardiac arrest occurs at U.S. road races. The researchers examined marathons and half marathons held in the U.S. from 2000 to 2010. They counted 59 cardiac arrests among about 10.9 million runners. The study put the rate at roughly one arrest per 184,000 participants. Of those 59 runners, 42 died.

Underlying heart disease is usually the cause. In older runners, it is most often coronary artery disease, in which fatty plaque narrows the arteries that supply the heart muscle. In younger runners, inherited conditions are a more common cause. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, an abnormal thickening of the heart muscle, is one example. Some people with these conditions have no warning symptoms beforehand.

A runner in cardiac arrest typically falls suddenly and becomes unresponsive. Breathing stops or turns into irregular gasps, which bystanders can mistake for normal breathing. The first steps are to call for help, start chest compressions and get an automated external defibrillator, or AED, as fast as possible.

Hands-only CPR means pushing hard and fast in the center of the chest at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions a minute. Compressions keep some blood moving to the brain until a defibrillator arrives. An AED gives spoken instructions, reads the heart's rhythm and delivers a shock only if the rhythm calls for one.

Every minute without circulation counts. For that reason, large marathons position AEDs and trained responders along the course, at aid stations and at the finish line. Some events also station medics on bicycles or carts. They can reach a fallen runner faster than an ambulance moving through crowded streets.

Many runners faint or collapse at or just after the marathon finish line because their blood pressure drops once they stop moving. The standard response is to lay the runner flat with the legs raised.

The cause lies in how the legs move blood during a run. Every stride squeezes the calf and thigh muscles. Those muscles press on the veins and push blood back up toward the heart. Physiologists call this the skeletal muscle pump.

During a marathon, blood vessels in the working leg muscles stay widened to deliver oxygen. While the runner keeps moving, the muscle pump offsets any pooling in those vessels. When the runner crosses the line and stops abruptly, the pump shuts off but the vessels remain open.

Blood then collects in the lower body. Less of it returns to the heart, less is pumped out and blood pressure falls. The brain briefly gets too little blood flow. The runner becomes dizzy, sags at the knees or drops to the ground. Sports medicine refers to the episode as exercise-associated collapse, or exercise-associated postural hypotension.

This timing explains why the problem clusters past the finish rather than out on the course. The muscle pump keeps working as long as the legs keep moving. It is also why race organizers routinely ask finishers to keep walking through the finish area instead of stopping or sitting down right away.

Management is straightforward in typical cases. Medical staff place the runner on their back and raise the legs and pelvis above heart level. Gravity sends the pooled blood back toward the chest and head. A runner with this form of collapse is usually alert and able to answer questions. Most improve after a short rest lying down.

A collapsed runner can look much like one with a life-threatening condition. For that reason, the medical team does not assume the cause. Staff check body temperature to rule out exertional heat stroke and may test blood sodium to rule out hyponatremia. A runner who goes down before reaching the finish, or who stays confused after lying flat, is treated as a possible serious case rather than a simple faint.

Friction blisters and chafing are among the most frequent minor problems treated in marathon medical tents and at aid stations. Neither is dangerous on its own, but either can force a runner to slow down, change stride or stop before the finish.

A friction blister forms when a sock, shoe or other surface rubs the skin back and forth over and over. That repeated shearing separates the upper layers of skin. Fluid then collects in the gap and creates the raised, tender pocket most runners recognize. Moisture from sweat, rain or water poured over the head softens the skin and makes it easier to damage. Shoes that are too tight, too loose or not yet broken in add to the friction.

On a marathon course, the feet take most of this punishment. Blisters tend to appear on the toes, the heels and the balls of the feet. Across a full marathon, each foot strikes the ground thousands of times. Small points of rubbing that go unnoticed on a short run can become painful by the second half of the race.

Chafing works in a similar way but across a wider patch of skin. It shows up as raw, red, stinging areas where skin rubs against skin or clothing. The inner thighs, underarms, waistband line and nipples are common sites. Nipple chafing, often called runner's nipple, can bleed through a shirt during a long race. Salt left behind by dried sweat can make the irritation worse.

A warm, sore patch known as a hot spot usually comes before a blister. Runners who notice one early can sometimes keep it from getting worse with tape or lubricant.

Aid stations and medical tents typically treat these problems with dressings, blister pads, tape and petroleum jelly. Many races also hand out petroleum jelly along the course for runners to apply themselves. Medical staff may drain a large, painful blister with a sterile needle. They generally leave the roof of skin in place to protect the raw layer underneath. A blister filled with blood needs closer attention. So does one that later shows spreading redness, warmth or pus.

Prevention centers on gear and preparation. Shoes worn in over several training runs are less likely to rub than a new pair. Socks made from synthetic fibers or wool blends move moisture away from the skin better than cotton. Anti-chafe balms, tape on known trouble spots and clothing with flat seams reduce friction elsewhere on the body. A common rule among distance runners is to try nothing new on race day.

Sunburn is a related skin problem on long, sunny races. Slower runners can spend five or six hours outdoors, sometimes with little shade along the route. Sunscreen, a cap and sunglasses limit that exposure.

Exercise-associated muscle cramps are painful, involuntary contractions that most often strike the calves and hamstrings in the late, fatigued stages of a marathon. Gentle stretching of the affected muscle is the standard first response.

A cramp seizes a muscle without warning and holds it tight. The muscle can feel hard to the touch, and a visible knot sometimes forms under the skin. Most episodes ease within seconds to a few minutes. The muscle may stay sore and prone to twitching afterward.

The timing offers a clue to the cause. Cramps tend to appear in the later miles of a race, in the muscles that have done the most repetitive work. The leading explanation among sports medicine researchers centers on altered neuromuscular control. Under this view, fatigue upsets the balance between nerve signals that tell a muscle to contract and those that tell it to relax. The muscle keeps firing when it should let go.

An older explanation blames the loss of salt and water through sweat. That idea remains widespread among runners, and it is still debated. One difficulty for it is that cramps usually hit only the working muscles rather than the whole body. No single cause has been settled, and more than one factor may play a part.

Pacing and preparation figure heavily in the fatigue-based account. Runners who start faster than their training supports push their legs into deeper fatigue. So do runners who race well beyond the distance of their longest training runs.

At an aid station or in a marathon medical tent, the usual treatment is a sustained stretch. For a calf cramp, the runner or a helper pulls the toes toward the shin to lengthen the muscle. For a hamstring cramp, the runner lies on their back while a helper raises the straightened leg. The stretch is held until the spasm releases. Many runners then walk for a while before trying to run again.

Some cramps signal something more serious. A cramp that does not ease with stretching warrants medical attention. So does cramping that spreads across many muscle groups at once. Confusion, vomiting or collapse alongside cramps can point to heat illness or a blood sodium problem rather than an ordinary cramp.

Exertional rhabdomyolysis is the breakdown of muscle fibers during or after hard exercise, which releases myoglobin and other cell contents into the bloodstream. Severe muscle pain, weakness, swelling and dark, tea-colored urine are warning signs that call for hospital evaluation.

Myoglobin is the protein that stores oxygen inside muscle cells. Once damaged fibers release it, the kidneys have to filter it out of the blood. In large amounts it can injure the kidneys and lead to acute kidney injury. That risk is the main reason the condition is treated as a medical problem rather than ordinary soreness.

Damaged muscle also releases potassium. High blood potassium can disturb the heart's rhythm. That adds a second reason for prompt care in severe cases.

Doctors confirm the diagnosis with blood tests for creatine kinase, an enzyme that leaks out of injured muscle. Some rise in creatine kinase is normal after a marathon. Long-distance running causes minor muscle damage in many finishers. Clinicians weigh how high the level climbs, along with kidney function and symptoms, to judge severity. Hospital treatment usually centers on intravenous fluids to help the kidneys clear myoglobin. Staff also monitor blood chemistry closely.

Urine color is often the clue that separates rhabdomyolysis from routine post-race aches. Myoglobin can turn urine brown or cola-colored rather than the deeper yellow of concentrated urine. Pain far out of proportion to the effort points in the same direction. So does a limb that becomes swollen and weak.

Ordinary delayed-onset muscle soreness tends to build over the day or two after a race and then fade. It does not change the color of urine or cause marked weakness. That difference helps runners and medical staff decide who needs blood work.

Severe cases are less common than muscle cramps at marathons. Hot conditions raise the risk. So does unaccustomed hard effort — running faster or farther than training prepared the body for. Downhill running forces muscles to lengthen under load. This eccentric contraction causes more fiber damage than running on flat ground. Sickle cell trait is also a recognized risk factor for exertional rhabdomyolysis.

Kidney damage can develop over hours. Dark urine after a race is a reason to get a medical evaluation promptly rather than wait to see whether it clears on its own.

Hypothermia is a drop in core body temperature below 35°C (95°F). Cold, wet or windy race days can push marathon runners into it. Slower runners who stop moving in the final miles or at the finish face the highest risk.

The condition does not require freezing weather. A runner soaked by rain or sweat can lose heat quickly at air temperatures well above 0°C (32°F). Water draws heat away from the skin far faster than dry air does. Wind adds to the loss by stripping away the thin layer of warmed air next to the body.

Running produces a large amount of heat. That heat keeps many runners warm through a cold race. The danger grows when heat production falls. A runner who slows to a walk, stops with a cramp or crosses the finish line makes far less heat almost at once. Wet clothing keeps pulling warmth away regardless.

That pattern puts slower runners at particular risk. They spend more hours exposed to the weather. They also run at a lower intensity and generate less heat along the way. Lean runners carry less body fat and therefore have less natural insulation.

Early warning signs include shivering, slurred speech and clumsiness. A runner may fumble with a zipper or a cup, or stumble on flat ground. As body temperature falls further, confusion and drowsiness can set in. In severe cases shivering may stop. That is a sign the condition is worsening, not easing.

Treatment starts by moving the person out of the wind and rain, ideally indoors or into a heated medical tent. Wet clothing comes off and is replaced with dry layers or blankets. The runner is then insulated and warmed gradually. A runner who is alert and able to swallow may be given a warm drink. Severe hypothermia requires emergency medical care. Responders handle these patients gently, because rough movement can trigger dangerous heart rhythms in a severely chilled body.

Race organizers commonly hand finishers heat sheets — thin foil blankets — to limit heat loss once running stops. The reflective material cuts the heat lost to the surrounding air. It also acts as a windbreak during the walk from the finish line to the baggage area.

Hypothermia can also follow a race that began in mild conditions. A dry start can give way to cold rain hours later. Runners dressed for the starting temperature may then have no extra layer to put on.

Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction is a temporary narrowing of the airways triggered by hard breathing, especially of cold or dry air. It causes wheezing, coughing and chest tightness. Runners with asthma may need a rescue inhaler, typically a short-acting bronchodilator such as albuterol.

The trigger is the sheer volume of air a runner moves. At marathon effort, most people breathe heavily through the mouth. That bypasses the nose, which normally warms and moistens incoming air. The lining of the airways cools and loses water. In response, the muscles around the bronchial tubes tighten and the passages narrow.

The condition is not limited to people with a formal asthma diagnosis. Some runners have symptoms only during or after hard exercise and breathe normally the rest of the time. Others have underlying asthma that exercise makes worse.

A dry cough is often the most persistent symptom. Shortness of breath can seem out of proportion to the pace. The symptoms often peak in the minutes after a runner stops rather than mid-effort. In many mild cases they ease on their own within an hour.

In the U.S., the most common rescue inhaler is albuterol. The same drug is called salbutamol in the U.K. and many other countries. It relaxes the muscles around the airways and usually starts working within minutes. People with known exercise-induced symptoms are often prescribed it to use about 15 minutes before exercise as a preventive step.

Runners with asthma are typically told to carry their inhaler during a race rather than leave it in a gear bag. A rescue inhaler only helps if it is within reach when symptoms start.

Cold-weather marathons raise the risk. Winter air is both cold and dry. For some people, high pollen counts and air pollution act as additional triggers.

A severe attack is a medical emergency. Warning signs include struggling to speak in full sentences and lips or fingertips turning blue. Visible strain in the neck and chest muscles with each breath is another signal. So is a rescue inhaler that brings no relief. A runner showing those signs needs emergency care right away. Race medical teams can give oxygen and repeated bronchodilator doses before transport to a hospital.

Marathon $MPC medical staff treat a steady stream of musculoskeletal injuries, from ankle sprains and muscle strains to knee pain and stress fractures. Some happen in a single misstep on the course. Others develop over months of training and surface as pain that forces a runner to stop on race day.

Acute injuries are the sudden kind. A runner can roll an ankle on a pothole, a curb, a manhole cover or a discarded cup near an aid station. Crowded starts and narrow stretches of course add the risk of tripping over another runner. Falls can also cause scrapes, wrist injuries and, less often, fractures.

An ankle sprain usually involves the ligaments on the outside of the ankle. They stretch or tear when the foot rolls inward. Swelling, bruising and pain when bearing weight are typical signs. Medical staff check whether the runner can stand on the foot and press for tenderness over the bone. Those findings help decide whether an X $TWTR-ray is needed to rule out a break.

Muscle strains are a separate problem. A strain is a tear in muscle fibers, often in the hamstring or calf. It tends to produce a sharp, sudden pain at a specific point rather than the gradual tightening of a cramp.

Overuse injuries build more slowly. A stress fracture is a small crack in a bone caused by repeated loading without enough time for repair. In runners, common sites include the shin bone, or tibia, and the metatarsal bones of the foot. Pain that sharpens with each step and is tender at one spot on a bone is a warning sign. Continuing to run on a stress fracture can turn a small crack into a complete break.

Tendon and joint problems fall into the same category. Achilles tendinopathy causes pain at the back of the heel. Patellofemoral pain, often called runner's knee, produces an ache around or behind the kneecap. Iliotibial band syndrome causes pain on the outer side of the knee. These conditions usually reflect training load in the months before the race. Race day can push them past the point where a runner can continue.

On the course, medics typically treat acute injuries with ice, compression, elevation and support such as taping or a splint. Runners who cannot bear weight or who have a suspected fracture are moved to the medical tent or taken to a hospital. Runners who drop out at an aid station can usually get transport back to the finish area.

Pain that changes a runner's stride or centers on one point of bone is a reason to stop rather than push through. A doctor or physical therapist can diagnose a specific injury and guide a safe return to running.
