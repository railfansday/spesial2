The Stock Market Just Had Its Worst Week of 2026. Here's Why Long-Term Investors Shouldn't Flinch.

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Investors should be prepared for this ahead of time.

By David Dierking – Nov 21, 2026 at 9:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

The U.S. midterm election has ushered in some of the most stock market volatility we've seen since the start of the Iran war. In the two days following Election Day, the Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -1.93%) experienced both its best and worst trading sessions since April 2025. The 2.7% decline for the week as a whole is also the worst in 19 months.

Since we haven't seen a week like this in a while, you might think it's rare. It isn't. If you're a long-term investor and reacted to virtually any of the trading days we've seen this month, it might be worth reconsidering just how small the move was in the bigger picture.

The election-week losses have helped push the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) to nearly 7% below its all-time high. But those kinds of pullbacks have actually been fairly normal over even just the past year.

That's three separate incidents over just the past year before what happened in November. Their relatively quick turnarounds may have made them largely forgettable. But pullbacks like this are far more common than you might think.

But it's the speed of the recoveries that has made these pullbacks especially dangerous. If you saw the S&P 500 falling and decided to sell in order to protect yourself, you had almost no time to buy back in before it was too late.

And that's the biggest problem with trying to time the market. The typical outcome is you lock in the losses after selling, but you miss out on the subsequent rebound. All you've essentially done is damage your portfolio's returns.

That's why long-term investors shouldn't flinch. These kinds of 5%-10% declines in the S&P 500 are normal and should be expected when investing in equities. It's simply the price of admission to the stock market.

Trying to react to short-term volatility is usually a bad idea. If you're a long-term investor, riding out the ups and downs is the key to success.
