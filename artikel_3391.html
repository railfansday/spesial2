10-Episode HBO Max Fantasy Series Delivers A Masterclass In Bending Genres

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Even though the show takes its name from an iconic horror author, one HBO miniseries is notable for its deft ability to blend a wide array of genres in its complex, compelling story. The miniseries format can be both a blessing and a curse for TV creators. On the one hand, its inherently self-contained style can allow creators to tell a perfect standalone story without worrying about renewals, as evidenced by HBO’s post-apocalyptic drama Station Eleven.

A near-flawless adaptation of author Emily St. John Mandel’s 2014 novel of the same name, Station Eleven would likely have struggled if the show needed to consider a second season storyline that expanded beyond the book’s plot. Instead, like Patrick Stewart’s breakout hit I, Claudius, the miniseries was a success precisely because Station Eleven was a miniseries, so the show didn’t have to expand its plot beyond the novel’s already-cohesive story.

Of course, there are also challenges when it comes to adapting books into miniseries, especially when it is not clear what genre category the story slots into. Although Hulu’s dystopian series The Handmaid’s Tale] is often classified as a straightforward sci-fi story, the show also leans into the psychological horror of its premise. Similarly, the 2020 critical hit Lovecraft Country saw HBO turn author Matt Ruff’s 2016 novel of the same name into a series that defied any simplistic genre categorization.

The story of Lovecraft Country follows Jonathan Majors’ Tic Freeman, who returns to the US in the aftermath of the Korean War to search for his missing father in the Jim Crow South. Joined by Jurnee Smollett’s Leti, Tic encounters both the terrifying cosmic horrors of HP Lovecraft’s writings and the more banal human horrors of racial segregation, discrimination, and lynching on his perilous journey.

A Deep South travelog, a grim period drama, a character study about grief and loss, a Lovecraftian horror complete with monstrous ageless beings, a dark fantasy about magic, and a sci-fi series all at once, Lovecraft Country can’t be considered any one genre at any given time. Whenever HBO’s adaptation leans into one side of the source story, the next scene or episode will change up its plot again, switching from terrifying paranormal monsters to human antagonists, or from sweet moments of character building to horrific violence.

Compared to hit horror shows of the same era, like Netflix’s critically acclaimed Marianne, which also flirted with Lovecraftian themes, Lovecraft Country was a hard show to pin down. The character study of the show’s slower moments is deeply felt and believable, but the scale of its cosmic horror is just as memorable as the show’s quieter scenes.

Meanwhile, Lovecraft Country’s meta-engagement with its titular co-creator makes the show’s story even more complex. Although the writer is arguably one of the most influential US horror authors of all time, alongside Shirley Jackson, Edgar Allan Poe, and Stephen King, Lovecraft was also a notoriously unapologetic racist whose views were considered retrograde and offensive even during his lifetime.

A lot of Lovecraft’s incredibly influential work features the racism and bigotry found in his personal letters, which has inevitably shaded how readers engage with his writings. Thus, HBO’s Lovecraft Country is also attempting the impressive feat of turning a racist author’s creations into a way to express the anxieties and experiences of a group of Black characters, alongside the rest of the show’s genre-blending experimentation.

It is a big ask, but one that Lovecraft Country pulls off with panache. A lesser show might not have known how to turn a single book into a character drama, a dark fantasy, a cosmic sci-fi, a gruesome horror, and a road trip story, but Lovecraft Country managed to make this blend look easy.
