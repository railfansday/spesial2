Monolithically and in situ integrated PbS infrared imagers

Nature (2026) Cite this article

Image sensors are critical components in artificial intelligence1,2,3, yet conventional approaches bond detector arrays to readout integrated circuits (ROICs), a process that increases complexity and cost4. The ideal alternative—growing the detector directly on the ROIC from solution using raw materials—has remained elusive owing to the difficulty in achieving electronic-grade films through a ROIC-compatible process5,6. Here, inspired by biomineralization principles7, we demonstrate monolithic in situ integration (MISI) of high-performance lead sulfide (PbS) photodetectors on ROICs by means of a room-temperature aqueous process. By regulating Pb–OH complexation and suppressing environmental perturbations, we achieve quasi-single-crystalline PbS films with excellent electronic properties (carrier density approximately 2 × 1015 cm−3, mobility 124 cm2 V−1 s−1) and wafer-scale, pixel-level uniformity. Through antimony doping, the resulting photodiodes exhibit a record specific detectivity of 4.4 × 1011 Jones at 2.5 µm, surpassing commercial InGaAs detectors8. We further fabricate a 640 × 512-pixel focal-plane array that captures clear infrared images and also integrate devices directly onto flexible ROICs. This solution-based MISI approach offers a scalable and transformative route for advanced optoelectronics and sensors.

The equilibrium calculations were performed using Visual MINTEQ. Together, these results identify an alkaline chemical window in which HPbO2− is abundant and comparatively buffered against variations in pH and precursor concentration, supporting stable control of supersaturation during aqueous PbS growth.

a, Texture factor of the PbS (200) reflection for films grown at pH values ranging from 11.0 to 11.4. Each point represents an independently prepared film and the distributions were obtained from seven independent measurements at each pH. The highest (200) texture factor was obtained at pH 11.2. b, Dynamic-light-scattering-derived particle counts as a function of reaction time at the indicated pH values. Solid lines represent the mean values and shaded regions indicate the variation across three repeated scans. The negligible particle signal at pH 11.2, together with the enhanced (200) texture factor, is consistent with the suppression of homogeneous nucleation and preferential PbS growth at the substrate interface.

a, Measured velocity spectra for applied vertical vibrations spanning 0.1–100 Hz. b, Enlarged velocity spectra near 40 Hz at the different driving amplitudes used in d; the peak velocity was taken as the vibration velocity amplitude. c, PbS (200) diffraction peak intensity (black, left axis) and FWHM (red, right axis) as functions of vibration frequency. The dashed lines indicate the corresponding values measured without externally applied vibration (0 Hz). d, PbS (200) peak intensity and FWHM as functions of vibration velocity amplitude at a fixed frequency of 40 Hz. Data in c and d are presented as mean ± standard deviation from n = 3 independently prepared films for each condition. The reduction in diffraction intensity and accompanying peak broadening at higher vibration frequencies and amplitudes are consistent with decreased crystallographic ordering under stronger mechanical perturbation.

a, Schematic illustration of the customized in situ synchrotron XRD configuration used to monitor aqueous PbS crystallization directly on a CMOS ROIC. b, Photograph of the experimental set-up, including the beamline configuration, sample holder and vibration-isolation platform; the growth cell is indicated by the red arrow. c, Representative one-dimensional radial intensity profiles obtained by integrating the two-dimensional diffraction patterns at selected reaction times. The PbS (002) peak near q ≈ 2.3 Å−1 increases progressively, whereas the reflections originating from the CMOS substrate remain detectable throughout the measurement. The PbS diffraction intensity increases continuously over the 200-min growth period, whereas the substrate signal gradually decreases. These time-resolved measurements directly reveal sustained PbS crystallization and film accumulation on the CMOS ROIC during aqueous growth.

a, Representative photographs of the substrate before and after aqueous PbS deposition, showing uniform film coverage over the full substrate area. b, Spatial distribution of the PbS film thickness measured by spectroscopic ellipsometry at the indicated sampling positions. The thickness variation across the wafer is limited to approximately 50 nm. c, Spatial distribution of the integrated intensity of the PbS (200) diffraction peak. d, Spatial distribution of the FWHM of the PbS (200) diffraction peak. Black points in b–d indicate the measurement positions and the continuous maps were generated by interpolation between these points. The colour scales represent film thickness in nm, diffraction intensity in arbitrary units and FWHM in degrees, respectively. Both the PbS (200) peak intensity and FWHM remain broadly uniform across the mapped region. Although minor localized variations are present, no systematic spatial degradation or large-scale gradients are observed, supporting well-controlled film growth and crystallographic uniformity over the inch-scale substrate.

a–c, Pole-figure maps of the PbS (220) (a), PbS (200) (b) and PbS (111) (c) reflections. The colour scales indicate diffraction intensity, whereas grey points denote positions with no detectable diffraction above the background. The (200) pole figure exhibits a strongly localized maximum at the centre, consistent with preferential alignment of the PbS (200) planes parallel to the substrate surface. The discrete symmetry-related maxima observed in the (220) and (111) pole figures, together with the absence of continuous diffraction rings, indicate a highly textured film with a limited population of randomly oriented grains. These measurements support the dominant out-of-plane orientation and low orientational dispersion of the MISI-grown PbS films.

a,e,i, Chemical-potential stability regions for In-doped, Sb-doped and Bi-doped PbS, respectively. Red points 1–3 denote S-rich, intermediate and Pb-rich conditions, respectively. b–d,f–h,j–l, Calculated defect-formation energies as functions of Fermi level under the corresponding chemical-potential conditions. Vertical dashed lines mark the equilibrium Fermi levels. Among the three dopants, substitutional SbPb exhibits the lowest formation energy and weakest competition from compensating acceptor defects, allowing the equilibrium Fermi level to approach or enter the conduction band. In-doped and Bi-doped PbS show comparatively higher donor-formation energies and stronger compensation, indicating narrower processing windows for stable n-type doping.

a–c, SEM image (a), high-resolution TEM image (b) and XRD pattern (c) of an undoped PbS film grown under optimized MISI conditions. d–f, Corresponding SEM image (d), high-resolution TEM image (e) and XRD (f) of an Sb-doped PbS film. The Sb-doped film retains a dense and continuous morphology without obvious pinholes or phase segregation. The measured lattice spacing decreases locally from 0.292 nm in the undoped film to 0.285 nm after Sb doping, consistent with local lattice contraction associated with Sb incorporation. The nearly unchanged position and intensity of the PbS (200) diffraction peak indicate that the average crystal structure and preferred orientation are preserved after doping.

a, Photograph of a PbSe film deposited directly on a CMOS ROIC substrate by the MISI process at room temperature. b, Surface SEM image of the on-chip PbSe film, showing dense and continuous crystalline coverage. c, XRD pattern of the PbSe film. The dominant PbSe reflections are indexed to the rock-salt phase, whereas the extra peaks originate from the Au and SiO2 layers of the CMOS ROIC; the reference PbSe diffraction pattern is shown at the bottom. d, Pole figure of the PbSe (200) reflection, indicating a pronounced preferred crystallographic orientation. e, Time-dependent current response of a PbSe photoconductive device under repeated on–off illumination from a 573-K blackbody source at a bias of 10 V and an incident power density of 0.1 mW cm−2. f, Normalized spectral photoresponse of the PbSe device, extending into the mid-wave infrared range. Together, the preserved film morphology, preferred crystallographic orientation and functional infrared photoresponse provide a proof-of-concept demonstration that the MISI strategy can be extended from PbS to PbSe.
