8 Takeaways from Design Miami and Art Basel in Paris

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Inside the week’s standout moments, from Miu Miu to Madonna.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

There was plenty of ground to cover in Paris last week where the French editions of Design Miami and Art Basel took place alongside a full slate of noteworthy openings and events. (“I have 55 invites this week,” said one harried interiors maven in between stops.) Though the protests in Paris had temporarily subsided by the time the fairs began, they remained top of mind. But still, there was a sense of optimism in the air, which could be chalked up to the unifying power of art and design.

This was the fourth consecutive year that Design Miami, Paris was shown at L’Hôtel de Maisons (the one-time home of Karl Lagerfeld), and the second year that it has been led by Jesse Lee, the proprietor of the luxury resale platform Basic. Space. Below, a few of the best moments from the week.

Jeanne Greenberg Rohatyn’s Salon 94 Design received the award for Best Gallery Presentation at Design Miami Paris. The New York gallerist took over the far end of the first floor and filled it with an eclectic mix of pieces: Next to chairs by Alberto Giacometti, she showed furniture—sleek “Stag Stools” replete with protruding antlers—by Rick Owens and Michèle Lamy for the first time. Celebrated artist (and occasional Acne model) Peter Schlesinger quietly stood by as guests admired his large ceramic swan and geese urns, which were fired in a kiln in the garage of his Long Island home. Overlooking it all was a sculptural chandelier by Rome-based designer F. Taylor Colantonio that looked as if it was sliced from the surface of the moon and embedded with gemstones.

For Apple’s second annual Designers of Tomorrow showcase, a quartet of creators were issued a singular prompt: presence, or rather how to hold people’s attention amid the hurried pace of the modern world. The Brooklyn-based artist Isabel Rower created “Padded Chair, 1860,” which was based on a deep dive into 18th- and 19th-century paper-mâché furniture. Fifteen hundred handmade beads lined the bottom of the piece causing more than one showgoer to comment on the work’s ASMR potential. Other participants included Salem S. Charabi, Yoonjeong Lee, and Genki Matsumura and Sebastián Zorrilla of Parafernalia. “It was easy to identify these four designers and give them this platform,” said Ago Interiors founder Rodman Primack who served as the curator of the project.

At Galerie Anne-Sophie Duval in nearby Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the renowned French architect and designer Sylvain Dubuisson presented a series of ogle-worthy pieces: a sleek black desk shellacked with Japanese lacquer, a gold table with metal legs that was designed to oscillate “just for fun,” and another wooden table that was held together vertically by a peg at its center. When the peg was released, the table went horizontal and became a functional dining piece. Another long marble table could be folded into thirds. Even though she no longer runs the gallery (that honor has been bestowed on her daughter, Julie Blum), Duval was on hand on Thursday night to show off Dubuisson’s playful and imaginative inventions.

The new showroom of ELLE Decor Rising Star Hugo Besnier is an enchanting reminder of all that Paris has to offer. Located in a historic loft off a courtyard in the heart of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the pedigree of previous inhabitants includes Balthus, Sheila Hicks, and even Vanessa Paradis—David Hockney was once a neighbor. In redesigning the property, Besnier took care to include works of these celebrated artists on the walls. The real function of the space is to not only show off his skills as an interior designer, but also his new collection of furniture, Tour de Mains, which works perfectly in the stylized surroundings.

Shattered prismatic forms by Alteronce Gumby at Jeffrey Deitch and new woven wall hangings by Sheila Hicks at Sikkema Malloy Jenkins were among our favorite pieces that bridged both art and objets. On the Palais’s second level, we encountered Marina Faust’s light sculptures, essentially stripped-down chandeliers hanging from a metal frame. Faust, a former photographer in her seventies, partnered with J. & L. Lobmeyr on these works. They were shown at the booth of Vienna-based gallery Gianni Manhattan.

At Hauser & Wirth Paris, a very specific collection pinpointed a certain passage in Alexander Calder’s life. Curated by Sandy Rower, the artist’s grandson and the founder and president of the Calder Foundation, Calder: François Premier is an examination of 1953–54 when the artist returned to live in the French countryside and had a very productive year both in terms of creating mobiles—Les Granettes (1953), and The Putney Mobile (1954)—trippy ink and gouache paintings, and household bric-a-brac like a homemade metal tea strainer.

French filmmaker Alice Diop, in her second collaboration with Miu Miu, presented Geste de Consolation at the Palais d’Iéna where Miu Miu stages its bi-annual runway shows. The project was the director’s first foray into making an exhibition, and the sampling of works packed a powerful punch. The four interconnected vignettes included a powerful 11-minute video directed by Diop; a purple-hued library and living room space with a curation of Diop’s favorite books and authors such as Chantal Akerman and Christina Sharpe; an ode to an African deity in Simone Leigh’s 16-foot-tall sculpture, Sentinel (Mami Wata); and a poignant photograph by Zanele Muholi. Just beyond the library in the makeshift auditorium, Ivorian choreographer Nadia Beugré arranged for live performances, while concerts were conducted by Angel Bat Dawid and the Sistazz of the Nitty Gritty.

The week even yielded a surprise appearance by Madonna, who was in town to celebrate her son Rocco Ritchie’s new exhibition Back on the Brush—a collection of 11 figurative expressionist paintings and sculptures that were shown at Pierre Augustin Rose. On view until November 7, the richly textured works are “rooted in observation, memory, and lived experience.” Says mom: “I love what he is doing!”
