401(k) deferral limit projected to rise $1,000 to $25,500 in 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Projected 2027 401(k) contribution limits from Milliman's final estimates show 10 deferral, catch-up and other employer plan thresholds rising ahead of official IRS figures

Inflation sets the pace for how much Americans can save in workplace retirement plans each year. The consumer price index rose 3.7% over the 12 months ended September 30, 2026. That figure comes from the CPI report the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics published October 14, 2026. It was faster than the 3.0% increase for the 12 months ended September 30, 2025.

The latest reading also ran above longer-term trends. The 10-year average annual change through September 30, 2025 was 3.2%. The 20-year average through the same date was 2.5%.

Those inflation readings feed into the annual cost-of-living adjustments for retirement plan limits. Milliman, the actuarial and consulting firm, used the September data to produce its final forecast of 2027 IRS limits. It puts the annual increase factor for those limits at about 3.5%.

The result is a set of estimated 2027 401(k) contribution limits that sit above 2026 levels. The employee deferral cap for 401(k), 403(b) and 457 plans would climb by $1,000. That cap governs how much a worker can steer from pay into a plan through elective deferrals in a year. Catch-up amounts, the maximum annual addition to defined contribution plans and several compensation thresholds would also move higher.

Every 2027 number in this list is a projection. The IRS had not published its official 2027 limits when Milliman released the forecast. The firm said only that publication was expected in the coming weeks. The official figures could differ from these estimates.

The 10 slides that follow cover 10 of the 11 items in Milliman's forecast table. Some are limits that shape what individual workers can put away, such as the deferral and catch-up caps. Others are thresholds employers use to test and administer their plans, such as the compensation limit and the highly compensated employee dollar amount. One covers defined benefit pensions, and another covers withdrawals tied to domestic abuse. Each slide gives the 2026 figure, the estimated 2027 figure and the change between them.

IRA limits are not part of this forecast. The estimates include no individual retirement account contribution caps, IRA catch-up amounts or IRA income phase-outs. None of those figures appear in this list.

The employee deferral limit for 401(k), 403(b) and 457 plans is projected to rise to $25,500 in 2027, up $1,000 from $24,500 in 2026. The estimate comes from Milliman, and the IRS has not yet published its official figure.

An elective deferral is the share of salary a worker chooses to send into a workplace retirement plan instead of receiving it as pay. The money moves through payroll, a set amount or percentage at a time. This annual cap governs the employee's own contributions.

Deferrals can go in before taxes, which lowers taxable income in the year the money is contributed. Many plans also offer a Roth option. Roth deferrals use after-tax dollars, and qualified withdrawals in retirement come out tax-free. Pre-tax and Roth deferrals both count toward the same annual limit.

Contributing the full estimated $25,500 evenly over a year works out to $2,125 a month. A worker paid every two weeks would need to defer a little under $981 per paycheck to get there.

The cap applies to the person, not to each plan. A worker who changes jobs midyear and joins a second 401(k) must keep combined deferrals across both employers under one annual limit. The new employer's payroll system may not know what went in earlier in the year. Keeping track of that running total falls to the employee.

Governmental 457(b) plans follow a different rule. Their deferral limit is separate from the shared 401(k) and 403(b) limit. A worker offered both a 403(b) and a governmental 457(b) can defer up to the full limit in each plan. Some public school districts and state and local governments offer that pairing.

Employer matching and profit-sharing contributions do not count against this cap. They fall under a separate, higher limit on total annual additions to an account.

The IRS adjusts the deferral limit for inflation in $500 steps, rounding down to the nearest step. The projected $1,000 increase equals two of those increments.

Workers age 50 and older could contribute an estimated $8,500 in catch-up contributions to defined contribution plans in 2027. Milliman projects this as a $500 increase from the $8,000 limit in effect for 2026.

Like every 2027 figure in Milliman's forecast, it is a projection, not an IRS-published limit.

Catch-up contributions sit on top of the standard employee deferral limit. They let older participants in 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457(b) plans put away more as retirement approaches. Eligibility begins in the calendar year a participant turns 50, regardless of the month of the birthday.

Adding the two limits together shows the full amount an older saver could defer. In 2026, a worker age 50 or older could defer $32,500 by combining the standard deferral limit with the $8,000 catch-up. Under Milliman's estimates, that combined total would rise to an estimated $34,000 in 2027.

The $500 step reflects how the tax code adjusts this limit. The catch-up amount is indexed to inflation and then rounded down to the nearest multiple of $500. Inflation must push the unrounded figure past the next $500 mark before the published limit changes. In some low-inflation years, the catch-up limit has stayed flat as a result.

Employers decide whether to offer catch-up contributions. Federal rules permit the feature but do not require plan sponsors to include it. A participant whose plan does not allow catch-ups cannot make them through that plan, even if old enough to qualify.

Catch-up contributions also get different treatment under the overall plan limits. They do not count toward the cap on total annual additions from employee and employer sources. As a result, they can push a participant's total contributions above that cap.

Two separate figures apply to some older savers. Participants ages 60 to 63 have their own, higher catch-up limit. A prior-year wage threshold determines when catch-up contributions must be made as Roth contributions. Milliman gives each its own 2027 estimate.

Workers ages 60 to 63 could contribute up to an estimated $11,750 in catch-up money to their workplace retirement plans in 2027, according to Milliman's final forecast. That would be $500 above the 2026 limit of $11,250. Milliman's 2027 figure is a forecast, and the IRS sets the official limit.

This age band has its own catch-up tier, set above the standard catch-up limit for defined contribution plans. The SECURE 2.0 Act, a retirement law Congress passed in December 2022, created the higher tier. It first took effect in 2025.

The tier covers only four ages: 60, 61, 62 and 63. A saver who turns 64 during the year drops back to the standard catch-up amount. Catch-up savers ages 50 to 59 use the standard limit. A saver in the 60-to-63 band uses the higher limit in place of the standard one, not in addition to it.

Like regular deferrals, catch-up contributions come out of a worker's paycheck through the employer's plan. Plans are not required to offer them. Whether a worker can use the higher tier depends on whether the employer's plan permits catch-up contributions.

Milliman projects the standard catch-up limit at $8,500 for 2027, up from $8,000 in 2026. Under those estimates, the 60-to-63 tier would sit $3,250 above the standard limit. That gap matches 2026. Both limits are projected to rise by the same $500.

Catch-up contributions sit on top of the regular employee deferral limit for 401(k), 403(b) and 457 plans. Milliman also projects a $1,000 rise in that deferral limit for 2027. If both estimates hold, a saver aged 60 to 63 could defer as much as $37,250 in 2027. Under the 2026 limits, the same combination came to $35,750.

That combined ceiling would rise by $1,500 from one year to the next. The regular deferral limit would supply $1,000 of the increase, and the catch-up tier the other $500.

The prior-year wage threshold that triggers Roth catch-up contributions is estimated to rise to $155,000 in 2027. That is up $5,000 from $150,000 in 2026. The 2027 figure is a Milliman projection, not a limit the IRS has published.

The threshold determines how some older workers can make catch-up contributions to an employer retirement plan. Workers whose wages from the previous year exceed the line must put their catch-up money into a Roth account. Workers at or below it keep the choice between pre-tax and Roth catch-ups, where their plan offers both.

The requirement comes from the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022, the retirement law Congress passed in December of that year. The law set the original threshold at $145,000 and tied it to inflation. That indexing is why the line moves from year to year along with other plan limits.

The test looks at wages, not income in general. It uses wages as defined for Federal Insurance Contributions Act, or FICA, payroll taxes. It counts only wages paid by the employer that sponsors the plan. Investment income does not count, and neither does pay from a different, unrelated employer.

The test also looks backward. It uses the previous calendar year, so pay earned in 2026 determines who falls above the 2027 threshold. A worker who crosses the line in one year faces the Roth requirement in the following year.

The difference between the two account types comes down to when tax is paid. Pre-tax catch-up contributions reduce taxable income in the year they are made. Withdrawals of that money are taxed later. Roth contributions go in after tax, and qualified Roth withdrawals, including earnings, come out tax-free.

For workers above the threshold, the rule removes the upfront tax break on catch-up money. It does not reduce how much they are allowed to contribute. The catch-up dollar limits themselves are set separately from this wage test.

Plan design also affects the outcome. A plan that offers no Roth option cannot accept catch-up contributions from employees above the threshold. A plan administrator can confirm whether a particular plan includes a Roth feature and how the rule applies to its participants.

The maximum annual addition to a defined contribution (DC) plan is projected at $75,000 for 2027, according to Milliman. That estimate is $3,000 above the 2026 limit of $72,000.

This cap sets the total amount that can go into one participant's DC account in a single year. It counts money from the worker and the employer together. That makes it a different measure from the employee deferral limit, which covers only what a worker chooses to set aside from pay.

Several types of contributions count toward the annual addition. Employee elective deferrals, both pretax and Roth, are included. So are employer matching contributions and employer profit-sharing or nonelective contributions. Voluntary after-tax employee contributions also count. Forfeitures reallocated to a participant's account count too.

Some money falls outside the cap. Catch-up contributions are not counted toward the annual addition limit. Rollovers from another plan or an IRA do not count either. Neither do investment earnings on money already in the account.

The dollar figure is not the only ceiling. A participant's annual additions also cannot exceed that person's compensation for the year. Whichever amount is lower applies. For a worker with modest pay, the compensation test can bind well before the dollar cap does.

The gap between this cap and the estimated 2027 deferral limit shows how much room remains for other money. For a worker who maxes out deferrals, up to $49,500 could come from employer contributions or after-tax savings. Plans that allow after-tax contributions and in-plan Roth conversions let some workers use that room. The approach is known as the mega backdoor Roth.

The annual addition limit generally applies per employer rather than per person. A worker in plans at two unrelated employers may have a separate annual addition limit at each one. Plans of related employers in the same controlled group are combined for this purpose. The employee deferral limit, by contrast, applies across all of a person's 401(k) and 403(b) plans combined.

Employer retirement plans could count up to an estimated $375,000 of a worker's annual pay in 2027, according to Milliman. That projection is $15,000 above the 2026 compensation limit of $360,000.

Milliman's figure is a projection, and the official IRS number could differ.

The compensation limit sets a ceiling on how much of an employee's pay a qualified plan may take into account in a given year. It comes from Section 401(a)(17) of the Internal Revenue Code. Earnings above the cap are disregarded for plan purposes.

The limit does not restrict what a person can earn. It restricts how much of that pay can feed into plan calculations. That distinction matters most for contributions and benefits tied to salary.

Employer contributions are often calculated with reference to pay. When a plan uses a pay-based formula for matching or profit-sharing contributions, it must stop counting at the compensation limit. A worker paid well above the cap receives contributions figured on the capped amount, not on full salary.

Defined benefit pensions follow the same rule. Many traditional pension formulas combine years of service with a measure of average pay. The pay used in that measure cannot exceed the limit for each year counted.

The cap also feeds into the nondiscrimination tests that qualified plans must pass. Those tests compare how a plan treats higher-paid and lower-paid workers. Capping compensation keeps very large salaries from skewing the results.

Most workers never reach the threshold. The limit is aimed at executives and other high earners. It shapes plan design at the companies that employ them. Some employers offer nonqualified deferred compensation plans to provide retirement savings on pay above the cap.

The tax code indexes the limit to inflation and adjusts it in increments of $5,000. The estimated $15,000 rise is the largest dollar increase in Milliman's forecast table for 2027.

The pay threshold that identifies a highly compensated employee is projected to rise from $160,000 in 2026 to $170,000 in 2027, according to Milliman's final estimates. That $10,000 increase is a forecast, not an announced IRS figure.

The HCE label shapes how employer retirement plans are tested. It does not set how much any single worker can contribute. Federal tax law bars 401(k) and similar plans from favoring higher earners over the rest of the workforce. The HCE definition sorts employees into two groups so that comparison can be made.

An employee generally counts as an HCE in one of two ways. The first is owning a significant stake in the business, either in the current year or the year before. The second is earning more than the dollar threshold in the prior year. Employers may also elect to limit the pay-based group to the top-paid slice of their workforce.

The threshold works on a one-year lookback. Pay earned during a given year determines who is treated as an HCE in the following plan year. Under that structure, the estimated 2027 figure would generally shape which employees are classified as HCEs for 2028 plan testing.

The classification feeds into annual nondiscrimination tests. The actual deferral percentage test compares the average salary deferral rates of HCEs with those of everyone else. The actual contribution percentage test does the same for employer matching and after-tax contributions.

When a plan fails one of these tests, a common fix is refunding excess contributions to HCEs. Those refunds are taxable income for the employees who receive them. Another option is an additional employer contribution to non-highly compensated workers. That contribution raises their average and narrows the gap.

Many employers sidestep these tests by adopting a safe harbor 401(k) design. Those plans commit to a required employer contribution. In exchange, they get relief from the deferral test and, for qualifying matches, the matching test.

A higher threshold can change who falls on each side of the line. Some non-owner employees paid between $160,000 and $170,000 could be classified as non-HCEs once the new figure applies. The IRS rounds the HCE amount down to a $5,000 increment. As a result, the figure can stay flat from one year to the next.

Milliman estimates the compensation threshold for officers who count as key employees will rise to $245,000 in 2027. That would be $10,000 above the $235,000 figure for 2026.

The figure matters for one part of federal retirement plan law. The Internal Revenue Code uses the term "key employee" for a small group of an employer's most senior people. Certain business owners fall into that group. So do officers whose annual pay exceeds the dollar threshold. An officer paid below it does not become a key employee through officer status alone.

The code also caps how many officers can count. No more than 50 officers are treated as key employees. Smaller employers face a lower cap tied to their headcount.

The classification feeds into the top-heavy rules in Section 416 of the Internal Revenue Code. Those rules test whether the share of plan benefits held by key employees exceeds a limit set in the code. A plan that crosses that line is considered top-heavy. The employer must then generally provide minimum contributions or benefits to employees who are not key employees.

Small businesses are the employers most likely to run into these rules. In a company with a few owners and officers, a large share of plan assets can sit in those people's accounts. For those employers, the officer pay threshold helps determine whose balances count on the key employee side of the top-heavy test.

The key employee threshold is separate from the highly compensated employee dollar amount, which applies to different nondiscrimination tests. An officer can be a highly compensated employee without being a key employee. The two figures are adjusted separately.

The officer threshold has its own rounding rule. The tax code rounds its inflation adjustment down to the next lowest multiple of $5,000. A modest rise in prices can leave the figure unchanged from one year to the next. A larger rise lifts it by $5,000 or more.

Employers check top-heavy status each plan year. The official 2027 officer threshold will come from the IRS, and it could differ from Milliman's estimate.

The maximum annual pension a defined benefit plan can pay is estimated to rise to $300,000 in 2027, up $10,000 from $290,000 in 2026. The projection comes from Milliman's final estimates for 2027. The IRS had not published the official figure when Milliman released its forecast.

A defined benefit plan is the traditional pension. Instead of building an account balance, the plan promises a specific payment in retirement. That payment is usually set by a formula tied to an employee's pay and years of service.

The employer generally funds the plan and carries the investment risk. If plan assets earn less than expected, the employer typically has to contribute more to cover the promised benefits. Participants do not pick investments the way 401(k) savers do.

This cap works differently from most retirement plan limits. It does not restrict how much goes into the plan each year. It restricts how much comes out, measured as the yearly benefit a participant can receive.

The limit comes from Section 415(b) of the Internal Revenue Code. A separate provision of the same section, Section 415(c), governs annual additions to defined contribution accounts.

The dollar figure is stated as a straight life annuity. That is a payment made every year for the retiree's lifetime, with nothing continuing to a beneficiary after death. A plan may pay a benefit in another form, such as a lump sum. In that case, the amount is converted to an equivalent annual figure for testing against the cap.

The dollar cap is not the only constraint. Section 415(b) also ties the maximum benefit to the participant's own average pay over their highest-paid consecutive years. For many people, that pay-based test produces a lower ceiling than $300,000.

Few workers approach the dollar ceiling. A $300,000 annual pension generally requires high pay, long service and a generous benefit formula. The limit matters most for highly paid participants, including owners of small businesses that sponsor their own pension plans.

Savers who have experienced domestic abuse may be able to take up to an estimated $10,900 from an eligible workplace retirement plan in 2027 without the usual early-withdrawal penalty. Milliman projects that cap will rise $400 from the $10,500 limit in place for 2026.

The official 2027 cap could differ once the IRS publishes its limits.

This withdrawal option is a recent addition to retirement plan rules. Congress created it in the SECURE 2.0 Act, which was signed into law in December 2022. The provision applies to distributions made after December 31, 2023. Lawmakers set the starting cap at $10,000 and indexed it to inflation.

The option is available in defined contribution plans, including 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457(b) plans. Employers are not required to offer it. Each plan sponsor decides whether to add the feature. Access therefore depends on the terms of a specific plan. A participant can check the summary plan description or ask the plan administrator whether it is offered.

The law defines domestic abuse broadly. It covers physical, psychological, sexual, emotional and economic abuse by a spouse or domestic partner. That includes efforts to control, isolate, humiliate or intimidate the victim. It also includes efforts to undermine the victim's ability to reason independently.

A participant must take the distribution within one year after the abuse occurs. The plan can rely on the participant's own written self-certification that they qualify. The law does not require the participant to hand the plan a police report or court order.

The penalty waiver does not make the money tax-free. Pre-tax savings withdrawn this way generally still count as taxable income for the year. Participants may repay the amount to the plan within three years. A repayment is treated like a rollover, which can let the saver recover income tax paid on the withdrawal.

The $400 increase is the smallest dollar change among the 10 estimated limits in this list.
