Community property, valuation and prenups: what decides who keeps a business in a divorce

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

How a business in a divorce gets divided when it was founded during the marriage, from community property rules to valuation, prenups and a hypothetical Jensen Huang example

A company started after the wedding can be the largest asset a couple owns. It is also one of the hardest to split. A bank balance can be divided down to the cent. A business cannot be divided so neatly. Its value can depend on a founder's judgment, on investor confidence and on years of shared financial risk that never appeared on a balance sheet.

What happens to a business in a divorce depends first on geography. In community property states such as California, property acquired during a marriage is presumed to belong to both spouses equally. Most other U.S. states follow equitable distribution, which aims for a fair division rather than an automatic 50/50 split. The same company could be divided on different terms depending on where a couple lives and files.

Nvidia $NVDA offers a clear way to see how these rules work on paper. Jensen Huang and Lori Huang met while studying engineering at Oregon State University. They married in 1985. He co-founded Nvidia in 1993, eight years after the wedding. The couple reportedly has no prenuptial agreement. No divorce filing has been reported. The example is hypothetical. Its value as an illustration comes from the timeline: the company was created well inside the marriage. Coverage of a possible split has focused on California's community property rules.

The rules behind a split go well beyond an equal-or-fair label. Premarital stakes, inheritances and gifts can remain separate property. Courts often rely on valuation experts to put a number on a private company. Public shares raise a different question: which date sets the price. Trusts and marital agreements can change the outcome. So can a spouse's unpaid work at home.

The 10 points below cover each of those issues, along with the buyouts and share offsets founders can use to keep control of a company. They close with past billionaire divorces involving the founders of Amazon $AMZN and Microsoft $MSFT. These are general principles. Specific outcomes depend on state law and the facts of each case.

In California, a company founded during a marriage is presumed to be community property, owned equally by both spouses. At divorce, the community estate is generally divided equally. The business, or its value, is shared no matter which spouse built it.

The rule comes from the California Family Code. Section 760 says property a married person acquires during the marriage while living in California is community property, unless a specific exception applies. Corporate shares, a partnership stake and a sole proprietorship all fall under that presumption if the business was formed after the wedding.

Whose name appears on the incorporation papers or stock certificates does not settle the question. A founder who files the paperwork alone, raises the money and runs the company still holds that stake for the marital community. A spouse who never worked at the company has the same ownership interest under the presumption.

Section 2550 directs the court to divide the community estate equally. The exception is when the spouses agree otherwise in writing or by stipulation in open court. Equal means equal in total value, not a requirement to cut every asset in half. A judge can award the company to one spouse and assign other community assets of matching value to the other.

California is one of nine community property states. The others are Arizona, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington state and Wisconsin. Assets a couple acquired while living in another state can receive similar treatment in a California divorce. The Family Code calls these quasi-community property.

Fault plays no role in the split. California became the first state to adopt no-fault divorce when the Family Law Act took effect in 1970. Today a petition generally rests on irreconcilable differences. A judge does not decide who caused the breakup before dividing the business.

The law also shapes how the company is run while the marriage lasts. Section 1100 gives the spouse who operates a community business primary management and control. That spouse generally must give the other prior written notice before selling all or substantially all of the business's personal property.

Divorcing spouses must exchange preliminary and final declarations of disclosure that list their assets and debts. For a founder, that disclosure includes the company stake. From there, the central question becomes what the community's interest in the company is worth.

Not every business stake held by a married founder is up for division. In California, an ownership interest held before the wedding, received as a gift or inheritance, or acquired after the date of separation is generally separate property. It stays with the spouse who holds it.

The rule comes from California Family Code section 770. It defines separate property as property owned before marriage and property acquired during marriage by gift, bequest, devise or descent. Section 771 adds that a spouse's earnings and accumulations after the date of separation are also separate. A company built with those post-separation earnings, before the divorce is final, would generally fall outside the community estate.

The harder cases involve a premarital company that keeps growing during the marriage. The original stake remains separate. The increase in value, however, can be partly community if the owner spouse's work drove that growth. In California, a spouse's labor during marriage belongs to the community.

California courts use two long-standing methods to split that growth. The Pereira approach comes from Pereira v. Pereira, a 1909 California Supreme Court case. It credits the separate estate with a fair rate of return on the original investment. The remaining growth is treated as community. Courts tend to apply it when the owner's personal effort was the main driver.

The Van Camp approach, from the 1921 California appellate case Van Camp v. Van Camp, works in the other direction. It sets a reasonable salary for the owner's services during the marriage and subtracts what the family already drew from the business. Any shortfall goes to the community, and the rest of the value stays separate. Courts lean toward this method when growth came mainly from market conditions or the nature of the business itself.

Inherited and gifted shares follow similar logic. Stock left to one spouse by a parent stays separate, and so do the dividends it produces. That protection can erode through commingling. Separate dividends might flow into a joint account that also holds paychecks. Over years of spending and redepositing, the line between the two pools becomes hard to draw.

The spouse claiming separate property generally carries the burden of tracing it back to its source. That usually means bank statements, capitalization tables, share certificates and transfer records. Gaps in the paper trail tend to work against the claim. Without clear records, a court will generally treat a disputed asset as belonging to both spouses.

Spouses can also change the character of property on purpose. For such changes made on or after Jan. 1, 1985, California requires an express written declaration. The spouse whose interest is adversely affected must make or accept it. A spoken promise to treat a founder's shares as joint property is generally not enough.

Most U.S. states do not split marital property down the middle by default. They follow equitable distribution. Under that system, a judge divides a business built during the marriage in whatever way the court finds fair, case by case. The result can be an equal split, or one spouse can receive more.

The process usually runs in three steps. First, the court sorts each asset into a marital or a separate category. Second, it puts a value on the marital portion. Third, it decides how to divide that value between the spouses. A company founded after the wedding generally falls into the marital category. The real dispute therefore tends to come at the valuation and division stages.

To decide what is fair, judges weigh factors set out in each state's statutes or case law. Common ones include the length of the marriage, each spouse's age and health, and each spouse's current income. Courts also look at future earning capacity and at what each spouse contributed to the marriage, including work done at home.

Earning capacity can shape how a founder's company is split. A spouse who spent years out of the workforce may have far less ability to earn after the divorce. A judge who weighs that gap can award the non-founder spouse a larger share of the marital estate.

Conduct with money can also count. Many equitable distribution states let courts consider dissipation, meaning one spouse wasted or concealed marital assets as the marriage ended. A founder might drain company accounts or sell equity cheaply to an ally. A judge who finds that kind of conduct can adjust the division to account for it.

A small group of states goes further. Instead of limiting division to marital property, courts there may reach assets either spouse owned before the marriage. In those places, even a business that predates the wedding is not automatically protected.

The flexibility comes at the cost of predictability. Because the result rests on a judge's discretion, two judges can weigh the same facts and settle on different splits. Many divorcing couples negotiate a settlement rather than leave that decision to a court.

A privately held company has no daily share price. A divorce court usually relies on a formal business valuation prepared by appraisers or forensic accountants. That figure sets how much of the marital estate the business represents and what the other spouse may be owed.

Appraisers generally work from three standard methods. The income approach estimates what the business will earn in the future and converts those earnings into a present value. Common techniques include discounted cash flow analysis and capitalization of earnings.

The market approach compares the company with similar businesses that have sold or with public companies in the same industry. The asset approach adds up what the company owns, subtracts what it owes and arrives at a net figure.

The right method depends on the kind of business. A profitable services firm with steady revenue tends to be valued on its earnings. A holding company that mostly owns real estate or equipment is often valued on its assets. Many appraisers run more than one method and reconcile the results into a single conclusion.

Each spouse may hire a separate valuation expert, and the two reports can land far apart. Judges then weigh the assumptions behind each figure. Those include projected growth rates, the salary the owner takes from the business and the risk premium applied to future earnings. In some cases the court appoints one neutral appraiser to limit cost and conflict.

Goodwill is a frequent point of dispute. Enterprise goodwill belongs to the business itself — its brand, customer contracts and systems — and can transfer to a buyer. Personal goodwill depends on the owner's own skills, reputation and relationships. States differ on whether personal goodwill counts as divisible property. The answer can change the final value considerably.

Discounts raise similar questions. A minority stake that cannot control decisions may be worth less than a simple percentage of the whole company. The same is true of shares that cannot easily be sold. Whether a court applies discounts for lack of control or lack of marketability varies by jurisdiction.

Venture-backed startups add another layer. The headline valuation from a funding round usually reflects the price of preferred shares, which carry rights such as liquidation preferences. A founder's common stock typically sits behind those preferences. Its value per share can therefore be lower than the round price suggests.

Shares in a publicly traded company are easier to value in a divorce than a private stake, because the market sets a price every trading day. The dispute usually shifts to timing and which date's price fixes the number.

A market price removes much of the debate seen in private-company cases. No appraiser has to project future cash flows or pick comparable businesses. The closing price on any trading day is public record.

Timing still matters for a founder whose wealth sits mostly in one stock. A volatile share price can rise or fall sharply over a long case. Months or years can pass between separation, trial and the day assets change hands. Each of those dates can produce a different total for the marital estate.

California generally values community assets as close to the trial date as practicable. A judge can pick an alternate date between separation and trial for good cause. Other states set their own rules on timing.

One common way to sidestep the timing fight is to divide the shares themselves rather than their dollar value. If each spouse receives half of the marital shares, both carry the same market risk afterward. Neither side gains or loses from a price swing on one chosen day.

Stock options and restricted stock add another layer. Founders and executives often hold grants that vest over several years. Part of a vesting schedule may fall during the marriage and part after separation. A court has to decide how much of each grant was earned through marital effort.

California courts have used time-based formulas for this, often called time rules. They compare the time worked during the marriage with the grant's vesting period. The result is a fraction of each grant treated as community property. The outcome can turn on whether a grant rewarded past work or was meant to keep an employee for future service.

Unvested awards often cannot be transferred under a company's plan. A court may instead order the employee spouse to hold the marital portion for the other spouse. Shares or proceeds are then delivered as the awards vest or are exercised.

Tax rules shape what each spouse actually receives. Under Section 1041 of the Internal Revenue Code, transfers between spouses incident to divorce generally do not trigger immediate tax. The receiving spouse takes over the original cost basis. Any built-in gain is taxed when those shares are later sold. Two blocks of stock with the same market value can carry very different after-tax worth.

Founders who serve as officers or directors also face securities rules. Insider trading policies, blackout windows and Securities and Exchange Commission filing requirements can restrict when shares are sold or moved. Those limits can affect how and when a settlement is paid out.

Putting company shares into a trust or holding entity does not by itself remove them from a divorce. Courts generally look past the name on the paperwork to who funded the trust, who controls it and who receives its benefits.

That distinction matters because many founders do not hold all their stock in their own names. Shares often sit in a revocable living trust, a family limited partnership, a limited liability company or another holding entity. Legal title may belong to a trustee or an entity. A divorce court tries to identify the economic interest instead. That means the right to dividends, sale proceeds and future growth.

A revocable trust is the simplest case. The person who created it can change or cancel it at any time. The assets inside are therefore still treated as the property of whoever put them there. In California, community property moved into a revocable trust during the marriage generally keeps its community character. Many married couples set up these trusts for estate planning, not to shield assets from each other.

Irrevocable trusts raise harder questions. Once assets go in, the person who transferred them usually gives up control over them. A spouse may argue that marital shares were moved without consent. Another argument is that the transfer was designed to shrink the marital estate. Courts can examine when the trust was created, what money funded it and whether both spouses agreed.

The answer often turns on tracing. Forensic accountants follow each block of shares back to its source. Stock bought or earned during the marriage starts out as marital property. Placing it in a trust does not automatically change that. Shares a founder inherited from a parent's trust may be treated differently, depending on how they were held afterward.

Public companies add a layer of disclosure that can help. Annual proxy statements filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission list the beneficial ownership of directors and named executive officers. Footnotes typically break out how many shares sit in personal accounts, family trusts or affiliated entities. That record gives both sides a starting map. It does not settle whether any block is marital or separate.

Holding companies and partnerships create a separate problem: control. A spouse may be awarded an interest in an entity without gaining any say over the stock it holds. The entity's operating agreement or partnership terms may restrict transfers, voting rights or distributions. Those terms shape what the awarded interest is worth in practice.

Trusts can also involve people outside the marriage. Children, other relatives or charities may be named as beneficiaries. Trustees owe duties to them. A judge dividing marital property generally cannot rewrite a valid trust that protects third parties. The court may instead award the other spouse a larger share of different assets to balance the outcome.

A prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can label a future company, and any growth in its value, as one spouse's separate property. That written contract can override the default rules a divorce court would otherwise apply to a business started during the marriage.

A prenuptial agreement is signed before the wedding. A postnuptial agreement does the same work after the couple is already married. Both are contracts between spouses, and both can cover property that does not exist yet. A founder who expects to launch a company can specify that any shares, stock options or ownership interests in the venture will stay separate.

Without such a clause, a company formed during the marriage falls under the state's default division rules. An agreement lets the couple choose a different result in advance.

The terms can go further than a simple separate-or-shared label. Some agreements keep the shares with the founder but promise the other spouse a fixed payment, a cut of proceeds from a future sale or other assets in exchange. Others set out how the business will be valued if the marriage ends. That can narrow a later fight over what the company is worth.

Courts do not enforce every agreement automatically. A judge typically asks whether both people signed voluntarily, whether each fully disclosed their finances and whether the terms were grossly unfair at signing. Separate lawyers for each spouse make an agreement harder to challenge. So does signing well before the wedding rather than days before it.

Postnuptial agreements can draw closer review. Married spouses owe each other duties of good faith that engaged couples do not. California treats spouses as fiduciaries in their financial dealings with each other. A postnup that hands one spouse a valuable company stake can face questions about whether the other spouse understood what was being given up.

These contracts also have limits. In the U.S., a prenup generally cannot decide child custody or reduce child support. Those questions stay with the court.

A spouse who never held a title or drew a salary at a startup can still have a legal claim to part of it. Many state laws treat homemaking, child care and support for a founder's career as contributions to marital property. How much those contributions count depends on which state's rules apply.

In equitable distribution states, the effect can be direct. The Uniform Marriage and Divorce Act is a model law that shaped divorce statutes in a number of states. It lists each spouse's contribution to acquiring marital property as a factor in dividing it. It also names contribution as a homemaker explicitly. A judge applying that kind of rule can consider who ran the household while the other spouse built the company. That labor can be treated as part of how the business came to exist.

Community property states work differently. In California, assets acquired during the marriage are generally split equally. A non-founder spouse's share does not rise or fall with how much he or she did at home or in the office.

Effort still matters at the edges. When one spouse brought a business into the marriage, California courts often use two formulas named for older cases, Pereira v. Pereira and Van Camp v. Van Camp. Both formulas separate growth driven by a spouse's work during the marriage from the return the original asset would have produced anyway. The portion tied to that work is treated as community property.

Contributions can also shape spousal support. California Family Code section 4320 directs judges to weigh how far the supported spouse contributed to the other's education, training, career position or license.

Unpaid work inside the company is a separate category. A spouse who kept the books, handled payroll or recruited early hires without pay may be able to show that the work added to the business's value. Courts generally look for evidence such as records, emails or testimony rather than broad claims.

Nvidia $NVDA's founding story shows the kind of contribution that rarely appears on a shareholder register. Gulf News cites founding accounts of Lori Huang's role in the decision. In those accounts, she would not agree to Jensen Huang leaving LSI Logic unless co-founder Chris Malachowsky also left Sun Microsystems at the same moment. Forbes reported that Huang has described her as part of Nvidia's story from the beginning. The Wall Street Journal reported that Huang told colleagues his wife handles nearly all household administration.

None of these accounts points to any dispute between the couple. If the division of Nvidia shares ever came before a California court, the equal-division presumption would generally be the starting point. A tally of who contributed what would not.

A founder who wants to keep a company through a divorce often does it through a buyout. The founder keeps the shares. The other spouse receives cash or other assets equal in value to their portion of the business.

This approach is often called an offset or an equalization arrangement. Suppose half of a business's value belongs to the other spouse. That spouse might take the family home, retirement accounts or an investment portfolio instead of stock. The aim is a balanced overall division, not an identical split of every asset.

The weak point of an offset is liquidity. A founder's wealth is often tied up almost entirely in company stock. There may be little cash or other property outside it. When outside wealth cannot balance the ledger, the parties need another route.

One route is a structured buyout. The founder pays the other spouse over a period of years, often under a promissory note secured by the business or its shares. The parties negotiate the payment terms, the interest and the consequences of a missed payment, or the court sets them. A structured payout lets the company keep operating without a forced sale.

A second route is dividing the shares themselves. Each spouse leaves the marriage owning stock, and the former spouse becomes a shareholder. For a public company, that can be relatively simple. For a private startup, it raises harder questions. Private shares can be difficult to sell. They often carry transfer restrictions as well.

Those restrictions matter. Many private companies have shareholder agreements, buy-sell agreements or bylaws that limit who can hold stock. Some give the company or the other owners a right to buy shares before they pass to an outsider, including an ex-spouse. A divorce settlement generally has to work within those documents.

Control is a separate question from value. A founder can give up economic ownership of shares while keeping the right to vote them. This is typically arranged through a voting agreement or an irrevocable proxy. The ex-spouse keeps the financial upside, and the founder keeps the decision-making power.

Investors and boards watch this arrangement closely. Venture investors often back a company partly on the expectation that its founder will keep running it. A sudden shift in voting power can unsettle a company's ownership structure. So can a large block of stock passing to someone outside the business. Either change can complicate future fundraising.

Taxes shape the structure too. Under Section 1041 of the U.S. Internal Revenue Code, transfers of property between spouses incident to a divorce are generally not taxed at the time of transfer. The receiving spouse takes over the original tax basis. A later sale of low-basis founder stock can therefore produce a large capital gains bill. As a result, a dollar of founder shares and a dollar of cash are not always treated as equal in negotiations.

When none of these options works, a court can order the business sold and the proceeds divided. Courts generally treat that as a last resort. A forced sale can erase value that belongs to both spouses.

Recent billionaire divorces offer a reference point. Jeff Bezos and MacKenzie Scott divorced in 2019 in Washington state, which also follows community property rules. Scott received roughly 4% of Amazon $AMZN's stock. Bezos kept voting control over her shares. The economic value of that stake moved to Scott, while the votes attached to it stayed with the founder.
