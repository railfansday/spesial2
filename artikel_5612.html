20 cars sold as the future that stalled, got cancelled or never reached buyers

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Top Gear's list of 20 futuristic cars shows each promise broke at a different point: the price, the factory, the balance sheet or the calendar.

Every generation of drivers is shown a car of tomorrow, and the idea behind it is seldom what kills it. The failure usually opens up between what a company promised and what it could build, price and pay for before time and money ran out. That gap was widest when investors put a price on the future before it existed. Nikola, a truck startup, reached a peak valuation of $30 billion in 2020, above Ford $F Motor, according to CNBC. Startups and their backers in that period modeled their hopes on Tesla $TSLA, but one industry analyst argued that Tesla was a poor guide. "They looked at Tesla's success, and Tesla was more of an anomaly than an example," Sam Abuelsamid, principal research analyst at Guidehouse Insights, told CNBC in 2024. A company valued that high before it has built much has little time for the slow, costly work of making cars.

The failures look different on the surface. Some cars cost more than buyers would pay, some were cancelled before anyone could buy one, and some still haven't reached customers. Many of the newest companies, however, took the same shortcut to public money and met the same end. Nikola went public in June 2020 through a merger with a blank-check company, known as a SPAC, according to CNBC. By 2024, Fisker had joined Proterra, Lordstown Motors and Electric Last Mile Solutions among the SPAC-backed companies that went bankrupt, according to CNBC. These collapses belong to one wave of financing, not to a string of unrelated blunders, and in many cases the flop belonged to the company and its money more than to the vehicle. Several of these stories haven't finished yet. For those cars, "flopped" means the promise has failed so far, not that it has failed for good.

Top Gear set out to show the hyped, futuristic cars that failed to succeed commercially, from the DeLorean and Chrysler's Turbine Car to Aston Martin's Cygnet and Tesla's second-generation Roadster. Each of these futures broke at a different point, whether at the price, in the factory, on the balance sheet or on the calendar. The slides that follow look at where each promise broke, which shows readers what to doubt the next time a carmaker sells a car as the future.

No customer has turned down Aptera Motors' three-wheeled solar car, because the company has yet to deliver a single one. The company planned to ship its first 60 Launch Edition cars in 2025, according to Electrek. That year passed without deliveries, and Aptera no longer offers a firm date. "We are committed to commencing production and delivery as soon as possible. However, the exact timing remains uncertain," the company's website said, as reported by Top Gear. The failure here is a launch that keeps moving, not a car that buyers turned down.

Aptera has responded to the delays by cutting the cost of starting production, but even the smaller plan needs money the company does not have in hand. Electrek reports that co-CEO Chris Anthony describes the revised plan as a cheaper route to putting cars with customers. The reduced bill is still steep for a company that has never sold a car, and Aptera now needs about $25 million just to begin production. It held $10.1 million in cash at the end of June 2026, well short of what even the leaner start requires. Reaching production therefore depends on raising more money, and the main risk to the car lies with investors rather than with its engineering.

The part of the promise that people doubted most, a car that charges itself, has held up. Testing by TÜV Rheinland found that the built-in solar panels can supply around 40 miles of driving a day in sunny conditions, according to Electrek. On bright days, short daily trips could run on sunlight alone. Regulators are not the obstacle either, because the car received its EPA Certificate of Conformity in June. If Aptera fails, it will fail for lack of funding, not because a solar car was a bad idea.

Anyone thinking of putting money down should watch Aptera's financing news, not its solar panels.

The Lightning GT was pitched as proof that drivers could go electric without giving up glamour. Car outlets called it a "green supercar," according to Top Gear. Autocar reports that the company priced it at £120,000 and planned to have it on sale by the end of 2009. At that price, only wealthy drivers could have bought one. That date passed with almost nothing built, and a museum says only two cars were apparently ever made. Buyers never turned the Lightning GT down, because most of them never had a chance to buy one.

The battery was what made the electric half of the pitch possible. The company chose a 36Kw pack from NanoSafe, built on nanotitanate cells, Autocar reports, and it paired that pack with a body meant to match any supercar on looks. A launch with a price and a sale date is a promise to build cars in numbers, and the Lightning GT never reached that stage. Whatever stopped the company, the promise broke somewhere between the announcement and a production run.

Calling the Lightning GT only a flop misses what its backers set out to prove. "We want to prove that electric cars can be aspirational products that offer an ownership experience equal to any normal supercar," Ian Sanderson, chairman of the Lightning Car Company, told Autocar. The car did show that an electric car could look good and still be a normal kind of car, according to a British Motor Museum assessment reported by Top Gear. By that view, the Lightning GT may have done more for the idea of an attractive electric car than for the company that built it.

A handsome electric car that almost nobody could buy shows that getting attention is not the same as delivering a product.

Ford $F introduced the F-150 Lightning as a finished arrival, not a cautious experiment. "The truck of the future is here," Ford said at the pickup's 2021 debut. Ford sold the truck on something a gas pickup can't offer, because owners can use it to power a home during an outage. Ford ended production of the all-electric Lightning by the end of 2025, according to Top Gear and WardsAuto, only a few years after that debut. The Lightning earns its place among these failures because Ford stopped building it soon after calling it the future, not because it never reached buyers.

The end of the truck is one part of a larger reversal inside Ford. Ford wrote down $19.5 billion on its electric-vehicle investments, according to WardsAuto. Ford took that loss on its whole electric plan, not on a single model. The truck's ability was never the problem, and Ford said at launch that the Lightning had more torque than any F-150 before it. Ford President and CEO Jim Farley says the decision to drop it came from what buyers want and from Ford's push for higher profits. The truck broke on Ford's balance sheet, so the flop belongs to the strategy more than to the nameplate.

Ford is keeping both the Lightning name and its factory. Ford plans to make the replacement, an extended-range version that carries a gas engine on board, at its Rouge Electric Vehicle Center in Dearborn, Mich., according to WardsAuto. The new truck will come from the same plant under the same name, with a different way of getting its power.

Ford is betting that range was the weakness buyers cared about. The Lightning with the largest battery tops out at 320 miles, WardsAuto reports, and Ford promises more than 700 miles for its replacement, according to Top Gear. That longer figure is Ford's own claim for a truck it has not yet launched, and no driver has tested it.

The all-electric Lightning has failed, but whether the name succeeds in its second life is still an open question.

Aston Martin built the Cygnet to lower its emissions under European Union rules, and it pitched the small car as a luxury answer to getting around the city. A car with that job only helps if people buy it in steady numbers year after year, and Aston planned on selling about 4,000 a year, according to Top Gear. Buyers never came close, and fewer than 150 Cygnets sold in total over the car's whole run, far short of what Aston hoped to sell in a single year. The count is disputed, because The Manufacturer counts 150 sold in the U.K. alone between the 2011 launch and the end of production in 2013. Either way, sales that small could do little for the emissions goal Aston had built the car to serve.

Buyers could see what they were paying extra for, and it was a badge rather than a better car. Underneath, the Cygnet was the Scion iQ city car. It cost three times as much as the Toyota $TM iQ, according to The Manufacturer, and for that money buyers got a car with about 97hp. "The premium supermini market is a good place to be at the moment but Aston got it wrong in thinking putting a grill and a fancy interior on what was basically a Toyota iQ would make people buy it," Ian Fletcher, an automotive analyst at IHS, said in 2013. Fletcher saw room for a small luxury car, and Aston tried to fill it by relabeling a Toyota instead of building a car of its own.

Aston ended the Cygnet after a three-year run, while the company's losses were growing. Its pre-tax loss widened to £24.6 million in 2012, The Manufacturer reports, and Cygnet production stopped by the end of 2013. Aston did not keep the car going in the hope that buyers would warm to it.

Buyers saw the Cygnet as a Toyota with an Aston Martin price, and the car never sold enough to do the job Aston built it for.

Audi found real buyers for the A2, but far fewer than a premium small car could reach. Audi had built 176,377 A2s by mid-2005, according to Audi figures reported by Top Gear, and that total fell short of the high hopes the company first held for the car. Mercedes-Benz sold about one million of its A-Class, according to Retro Motor. Drivers did not ignore the A2, but they never bought it in the numbers Audi had hoped for.

The aluminum body that made the A2 look like the future also made it costly to build. Each A2 that Audi sold carried an alleged loss of £4,000, according to Retro Motor. The problem was the distance between what the technology cost and what people would pay for a small car. Audi had a car of tomorrow ready to drive, and buyers would not pay what it cost to make.

The engineering behind the A2 has held up better than its sales. One version, the A2 three litre, emitted 81g/km of CO2, according to Retro Motor, a low figure for any car. Since the A2, BMW has been the only carmaker to build a car in this class with a lightweight body, the i3. Audi's approach found few followers, and the expense of going first may help explain why. The record suggests Audi was early with the idea rather than wrong about it.

Being early still counts as a failure, because no carmaker can keep building a future that reportedly loses money on every sale.

Jaguar ended the C-X75's promise before a single customer could decide whether to buy one. The car was a real commitment, not a show-stand fantasy. Jaguar confirmed in spring 2011 that it would put the supercar into production, according to Motor Sport Magazine. The company aimed it at a tiny circle of wealthy buyers, with plans for a run of 250 cars at about a million dollars apiece, according to Top Gear. Jaguar never intended a run that small to earn volume profits. It planned the car to make the brand look like the future, and a company short of money usually cuts that kind of showpiece first.

Jaguar reversed course the following year and blamed the wider economy rather than the car. The program ended in 2012, and Jaguar said weak economic conditions around the world left no room to launch a new supercar, according to Motor Sport Magazine. The economy explains only part of the decision. With essentially three model lines, or four counting the F-Type, Jaguar had a thin base from which to fund a car that was unlikely to earn back its cost. The C-X75 ended in a corporate retreat, not a market judgment, and no buyer ever got the chance to pay for one.

Jaguar spared itself a repeat of its own history by pulling out early. Andrew Frankel, a senior contributing writer at Motor Sport Magazine, argues in a 2012 assessment that Jaguar's XJ220 sold poorly, as did rivals from the Bugatti EB110 to the McLaren F1. "It's sad that the car has been killed but it is hard to see it as anything other than the right decision," Frankel said.

Jaguar promoted the C-X75 as its most advanced car ever, according to Top Gear, yet the car never left the company's hands. Jaguar cancelled the road car before selling any to customers, Motor Sport Magazine reports, and kept prototypes for use as test beds.

For this car, the future stayed in Jaguar's engineering work and never reached the road.

Honda $HMC put a luxury-car price on an electric car that could do far less than a luxury car. The EV Plus carried an MSRP of about $110,000 in today's money, according to Top Gear, but its range was about 80 miles. That range covered local errands and a daily commute, but not the long trips people expect from a car at that price. A battery-powered Honda was a sound idea, but Honda priced the EV Plus far above what it could do.

Honda never charged that sticker price, because it kept the car off the open market. SlashGear reports that Honda offered the EV Plus only on a lease and never sold one outright, and that it built only 340 of them. That was too few to show whether drivers wanted the car at all. Even the lease was steep, with a monthly payment of about $1,000 in today's money, a premium-car bill for what was a commuter car. The EV Plus didn't fail with buyers so much as Honda never put it in front of enough of them to fail.

California regulators were already pushing carmakers toward electric cars when Honda built this one. The state's Air Resources Board set up its zero-emission vehicle program in 1990, SlashGear reports, and Honda destroyed the EV Plus cars before 2000. Honda built the car in small numbers, leased it rather than sold it, and destroyed the cars within the same decade. That record looks more like a response to the state program than a serious bid to win buyers.

When a carmaker builds just enough of a future car to satisfy a regulator, buyers should treat that car as a test and not as a product.

Fisker, Inc. sold the Ocean electric SUV as a car to enjoy, not only as a way to save on fuel. Top Gear reports that the SUV's best-known feature was a setting called "California Mode," which let the driver open all the glass at once. A pitch built on how a car feels depends on the first people who drive it, and reviewers reported problems with the Ocean. One showpiece feature can win attention, but it cannot persuade buyers once the people testing the car are writing about its faults.

Fisker left the factory work to Magna, which built the Ocean under contract. That arrangement produced real volume, with more than 10,000 Oceans built in 2023, according to CNBC. Fisker sold only 4,700 of them that year through its own direct-sales model, which left the company with far more SUVs than buyers would take. It ended 2023 holding $530 million in inventory, and much of the cash it needed to stay in business was tied up in unsold cars. Hiring Magna spared Fisker the cost of owning a factory, but it did not spare Fisker the cost of owning cars nobody bought.

Fisker tried to fix how it sold the Ocean only after the car was already on the market. It switched from direct sales to dealerships in January 2024, CNBC reports, and in February it warned that it might not be able to keep operating. Fisker then filed for Chapter 11 on June 17, 2024, in the same year it had changed how it sold the Ocean.

An analyst who had expected more from the company placed the blame on its leaders rather than on the SUV. "The problem in the case of Fisker that I underestimated was … the incompetency of the senior management," Sam Abuelsamid, principal research analyst at Guidehouse Insights, told CNBC.

Fisker managed to build the Ocean in volume, and then it failed to sell it.

The Karma was a startup's bet that a new company could sell the luxury car of tomorrow and build a business around it. Fisker Automotive built the car in the 2010s, according to Top Gear. In a report published in 2014, CNBC puts Karma sales at about 1,800 against an initial projection of 11,000. The car started at around $100,000, which placed it in the luxury class. A business planned around the forecast could not survive on the real sales. Fisker Automotive was gone by 2013, within the decade its car was meant to define.

The 2013 collapse ended the business, but a buyer still judged the car worth saving. Wanxiang Group paid $149.2 million for Fisker's assets, CNBC reports in a 2014 story, and the car later returned under the Karma Automotive name. Wanxiang paid that sum for the Karma's design and brand even though the company behind them had failed. The first failure belonged to Fisker Automotive, and the car got a second chance to prove the original promise.

That second chance tested whether the same car could succeed with a richer backer and more time. Wanxiang planned to bring the car back by 2015, according to CNBC. Years after that target, reviewers judged the relaunched Karma GS-6 a failure. "In the end, the Karma GS-6 failed to score highly in any of our key criteria," Motor Trend said in 2021.

Wanxiang could buy a bankrupt car, but it could not buy a better verdict, and the Karma flopped once as a startup's product and again as a rescued one.

Tesla $TSLA has held buyers' money for its second-generation Roadster for years without handing over a single car. Each deposit was $50,000, according to Top Gear, a large sum for a car that is still not available. Tesla began collecting those deposits in 2017, CNBC reports, and Tesla CEO Elon Musk now says production will start in 2028. Those buyers committed their money long before production, and what they bought was Tesla's word on when the car would arrive.

The Roadster's failure so far is a failure of the calendar. Tesla has missed every delivery date it has targeted for the Roadster, according to CNBC. Musk later named April 1, 2026, as the car's debut, a date he stated rather than a confirmed launch. That date falls on April Fools' Day, and he acknowledged the cover it gave him. "I have some deniability, because I could say I was just kidding," Musk said. A date that its own author can disown as a joke is not a commitment, and any future Roadster timeline deserves the same doubt.

Deposit holders still lack the basics that anyone would want to know before paying in full. Tesla has not announced a price, CNBC reports, and it is unclear whether the car will be street-legal. Buyers have put $50,000 toward a total they have never seen, for a car that may not work as an everyday road car. Musk has also said Tesla did not design the Roadster with safety as its main goal. The further the Roadster moves from an ordinary road car, the harder it becomes to say what buyers were promised in 2017.

Tesla is again asking customers to commit before the car exists. The company is taking reservations in the U.S., Canada and China, according to CNBC. As long as customers keep signing up, Tesla can miss a date without ending the project. The flop label fits the schedule but not yet the result, because this promise is late rather than cancelled.

Whether the Roadster flopped will be settled by one test: whether a Roadster ever reaches a paying customer's driveway.

Faraday Future sold the FF 91 on raw acceleration. When the company first showed the luxury electric car in 2017, it advertised a run from 0 to 60mph in under 2.4 seconds, according to Top Gear. The car's identity rested on that advertised figure from the start. Years later, Faraday Future reports that it expected to hand over just one additional FF 91 2.0 Futurist Alliance in December 2025. A single car in a month is far from the volume a carmaker needs to build a business on its flagship.

The bigger risk to the FF 91 is whether its maker can keep paying its way. Outside money keeps the company running, and its financing totaled approximately $82 million by the close of 2025's third quarter, according to Faraday Future. Even so, the company warns investors that it may not be able to stay in business and that it could seek bankruptcy protection if new financing doesn't come through. With its maker's survival in doubt, whether the FF 91 has flopped for good remains an open question.

Faraday Future already presents its flagship as a step toward a different car, with a planned upgrade from the FF 91 to an FF 92, according to Faraday Future. That plan treats the FF 91 as a stage on the way to something else rather than the destination the company sold it as.

The FF 91 did not fail on the speed Faraday Future promised. It failed on the money and time the company needed to turn that speed into a car people could buy.

Nikola built real semitrucks, but it never built enough of them to pay for the company. Its trucks count among the hyped futuristic vehicles that failed to succeed commercially, according to Top Gear. For a company pitched as the future of freight, its output stayed small: Nikola started producing semitrucks only in 2022, CNBC reports, and had made 600 by the end of the third quarter of 2024. Nikola also recalled many of its vehicles for defects, at a cost of tens of millions of dollars. For a young truck maker, every faulty truck adds repair costs before it earns any trust from buyers.

Nikola's pitch won serious backing from inside the auto industry. General Motors $GM took a $2 billion stake in Nikola, according to CNBC. That support did not protect the company from questions about the man who started it. The short seller Hindenburg Research first publicized controversies around Nikola founder and former chairman and CEO Trevor Milton. The doubts that hurt Nikola centered on its founder, not on its trucks. Once investors stopped believing the story, 600 trucks, many of them recalled, could not support a company funded on its promises.

Nikola's remaining cash fell short of what the business needed to keep going. The company held $198 million at the end of the third quarter of 2024, according to CNBC, and still filed for Chapter 11. "Unfortunately, our very best efforts have not been enough to overcome these significant challenges, and the Board has determined that Chapter 11 represents the best possible path forward under the circumstances for the Company and its stakeholders," Nikola CEO Steve Girsky said. Nikola plans to auction and sell its assets, so the company is breaking itself up rather than restructuring to keep building trucks.

The trucks Nikola did manage to build will outlast the company, as lots in an auction.

The trouble with Lordstown Motors' Endurance started with the buyers the company said it already had, long before the electric pickup could prove anything on the road. Lordstown hyped the truck as part of the future, according to Top Gear, but much of the demand behind it was never real. The SEC reports that its pre-orders were nonbinding and that most came from companies with no fleets and no intent to use the trucks themselves. An order that commits no one, placed by a company with nowhere to put a truck, signals passing interest rather than a customer. Most of the market Lordstown appeared to have was never there to lose.

Lordstown's leaders treated being the future as being first, and in that race a company could gain more from looking ready than from being ready. "We allege that, in a highly competitive race to deliver the first mass-produced electric pickup truck to the U.S. market, Lordstown oversold true demand for the Endurance," Mark Cave, associate director of the SEC's Division of Enforcement, said. When a carmaker builds its pitch on winning a race, the order count it shows belongs to that pitch.

The reckoning over that demand came only after the company had collapsed. Lordstown filed for bankruptcy in 2023, according to the SEC, so no working carmaker was left to answer for the truck. In 2024, Lordstown agreed to settle with the SEC, and the settled order set a disgorgement of $25.5 million. The investigation was ongoing at that point, and the account of how Lordstown sold the truck's future remained unfinished even after the truck itself had failed.

The next time a carmaker announces a large order count, the question worth asking is how many of those buyers have committed to pay.

The Wrangler 4xe was an electrified model that an established off-road brand built, sold and then ended. Jeep introduced the plug-in hybrid in 2021, according to Top Gear. It paired a battery that owners charge with a combustion engine. With that design, Jeep offered buyers electric driving without asking them to give up a gas engine, and it hedged its bet instead of going fully electric. Stellantis $STLA, Jeep's parent company, announced in early 2026 that it would discontinue the model. The 4xe reached buyers and was offered for only a few model years before its own maker stopped it. The model ended because Stellantis chose to end it, not because buyers never had the chance to own one.

Whether the 4xe flopped is still an open question. Stellantis has described the 4xe as a best-seller, according to Top Gear. That description comes from the company that made the model, and buyers should weigh it as a claim rather than a proven result. Jeep also faced a stop-sale on the 4xe, a period when the model could not be sold. That interruption counts against the idea of a dependable electrified Jeep, whatever its sales were. The stop-sale and the discontinuation are separate events, and neither should be read as the cause of the other.

The 4xe shows that a major automaker can bring an electrified model to showrooms and call it a best-seller, and it can still drop the model within a few years. That risk falls on buyers in a different way than a startup collapse does, because the company behind the car remains in business.

For buyers, a maker's sales claims are no promise that the model will still be on sale.

Chrysler built only 55 Turbine Cars, a tiny number for a car meant to change how everyone drives. In the 1960s, Chrysler wagered that a turbine engine, a jet-style powerplant that many in the industry then saw as a possible successor to the piston engine, belonged in an ordinary road car. Chrysler built just 55 of them, according to National Museum of Transportation figures reported by Top Gear. A run that small never put the turbine engine in front of the mass of drivers it would have needed to reach to replace the piston engine. Chrysler made a large promise about the way people might drive one day, and it delivered that promise in the dozens.

Chrysler treated the Turbine Car as a serious machine and not as a sketch of tomorrow. Its turbine engine was rated at 425lb ft of torque, a figure that shows real engineering behind the idea. Chrysler also had Ghia, an outside design house, supply the body. Hiring a separate firm for the styling shows that Chrysler wanted the car to look finished, not like an engineering test rig. The company put real effort into both the engine and the design. That effort leaves the number of cars Chrysler made as the clearest sign of where the promise fell short.

A powerful engine and a coachbuilt body did not add up to a car built in numbers, and the turbine never replaced the piston engine.

Chevrolet put its most famous sports-car name on a future it never turned into a commercial success. Chevrolet brought the CERV III to the Detroit Auto Show around 1990 with a Corvette badge on it, according to a Car and Driver account reported by Top Gear. The company showed the car at a major industry show rather than keeping it out of sight as a secret prototype. The Corvette badge tied the car to Chevrolet's best-known sports car at the start of a new decade. That public showing under a famous badge earns the CERV III its place among hyped futuristic cars that failed to succeed commercially. Chevrolet put the car in front of the public under its best-known name, and it never turned that car into one that sold.

The future the CERV III suggested was a change in the basic layout of the Corvette, not a new fuel or a new kind of company. The CERV III was a mid-engine car, with the engine placed in the middle of the car. With that layout and a Corvette badge, people took the car as a sign of what the next Corvette might be. Chevrolet left the public to guess at the rest, and the guess never became a car in a showroom. The promise ended somewhere between the show stand and the dealership. Buyers who see a concept car unveiled at an auto show are looking at a promise. A trusted badge doesn't turn that promise into a car anyone can buy.

Chevrolet showed the public a mid-engine Corvette in 1970, and that car never became a commercial success. The XP-882 appeared at the New York Auto Show in June 1970, according to Top Gear, as a Corvette with its engine placed in the middle of the car. That layout was the car's claim on the future. A Corvette built that way would have changed how Chevrolet put its sports car together, not just how the car looked on a show stand. Chevrolet chose one of the country's major auto shows as the place to reveal it, so the idea reached a large audience of reporters and car fans at once.

The XP-882 counts among hyped futuristic cars that failed commercially because Chevrolet never turned that public showing into a car that sold. Visitors in New York could walk around a mid-engine Corvette, but a concept car gives a shopper nothing to buy. Crowds and attention at a show also tell a carmaker little about whether drivers will pay for the car once it has a price, a factory and a sales plan behind it. The distance between a show car and a showroom car is where this promise stopped. Chevrolet had a working idea to show in 1970, and it had a famous name to attach to that idea, yet neither of those things put the car on the road.

A mid-engine Corvette on a show stand in 1970 was a promise, and Chevrolet never asked buyers to pay for it.

The Borrego did not last long on sale in the U.S. Kia had chosen 2009 as the SUV's model year, and that same year it dropped the Borrego from the American market, according to Top Gear. The car never got a second model year in the U.S., and that quick exit puts the Borrego among the hyped cars that failed commercially. Carmakers rarely give up on a brand-new model so soon. Kia's fast retreat suggests the SUV fell well short of what the company expected from American buyers.

Kia built the Borrego as a serious bid for American families, not as a small trial. The SUV had three rows of seats for seven passengers, which aimed it at households that need space and placed it in the mainstream of the U.S. market. Kia also gave it a 337hp V8, so the carmaker backed its pitch with real hardware instead of a token entry. Kia said the Borrego was "set to be extremely competitive in the midsize SUV segment." That confidence did not keep the SUV on sale in the U.S. beyond 2009. A roomy cabin, a large engine and a bold claim from the maker added up to a forecast of success, and in the U.S. that forecast did not come true.

General Motors $GM leased its electric car to a small group of drivers instead of selling it to the public. About 1,100 customers leased an EV1, according to Top Gear. For a carmaker of GM's size, that is a trial rather than a launch, and it is why the EV1 counts as a commercial failure. GM later repossessed every one of the cars. The drivers who had an EV1 did not simply park it and move on. GM took the cars back.

The repossession sets the EV1 apart from a car that buyers passed over in the showroom. The people who drove one had already taken the car into their daily lives, and none of them kept it once GM collected the fleet. The EV1 reached the road, and then every car left it.

The car also fell short of what an everyday family car needed to do. The EV1 had a real-world range of less than 100 miles, which suited commutes and local errands but not the longer trips a household expects its main car to handle. That limit may help explain why the program stayed so small, with a modest group of committed drivers rather than a broad base of families trading in their gas cars.

A driver offered the next car of the future on a lease should remember that a lease is not ownership, and that GM took back every EV1 it put on the road.

The DeLorean's promise was visible from across the street. Its wedge-shaped body, gullwing doors and stainless-steel skin made it look like a car from the future before anyone turned the key. Very few people ever owned one, though. DMC built about 9,000 DeLoreans in total after the first production cars appeared in 1981, according to Top Gear. A run that small left the car a rarity rather than the start of a new way of building cars. Its maker sold a vision of tomorrow, but almost nobody got to drive it.

The styling promised more than the car could deliver. A DeLorean needed around nine seconds to reach 60mph, which is a modest pace for a car designed to look fast. Buyers paid for the stainless steel and the doors that opened upward, and underneath they got an ordinary drive. The DeLorean shows that a futuristic surface doesn't make a futuristic car. Anyone weighing a car sold on its looks should ask what the engineering beneath that design can actually do.

Most people know the DeLorean from fiction, not from the road. Its best-known role is the car in Back to the Future, and that film, rather than sales or performance, keeps its name familiar decades later. DMC sold a future it couldn't build in any real numbers, and the movie gave the car the lasting fame the company never earned in the market. The version of tomorrow that people remember belongs to a story on screen, not to anything owners drove.

When the next car of the future arrives, judge it by what it delivers on the road, not by how it looks in the showroom.
