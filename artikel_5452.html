How much to tip in 20 countries around the world — and where tipping is rude

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Tipping guidance for 5 regions, from rounding up in Germany to skipping tips in Japan, where staff may take a gratuity as an insult.

The check lands on the table after a meal overseas, and a routine decision turns into a puzzle. Back home, most Americans know the drill. They glance at the total, run the math, and add a gratuity without a second thought. Abroad, the same reflex can misfire. Money carries different meanings from one culture to the next, and the gap shows at the moment a diner must decide how much to leave, or whether to leave anything at all. A guess in either direction has consequences. Leave too little in a place where staff count on gratuities, and they come up short. Leave too much in a place that does not, and a gesture meant as thanks can land as an awkward misstep.

Wages explain much of the difference. In many countries, servers earn a living wage, so tips make up a small slice of their income or none of it. Visitors from the U.S. who trim their usual amount can feel stingy, even in places where the smaller sum is the norm. A large tip can come across as rude in some settings. A second trap sits on the bill itself. Many restaurants abroad print a service charge on the check, and a customer who misses that line can pay for the same service twice. Reading the receipt and knowing the local custom shape what dinner costs in the end. Both habits also spare everyone at the table an uncomfortable pause when the server returns.

Capital One $COF published its guide to tipping around the world on Aug. 7, 2024, as advice for travelers wondering when a tip belongs and how large it should run. Its guide draws on seven outside travel and finance publications: Condé Nast Traveler, Europe Up Close, Big Think, Western Union, Wise Bread, SmarterTravel, and Excellence Resorts. The guide sorts its advice into six sections, five built around world regions and one devoted to cruises and all-inclusive resorts. This article takes up the five regional sections and sets the cruise and resort advice aside: Europe, Central and South America, the Middle East, Asia, and Australia and New Zealand. Each slide walks through the country groupings the guide lists for one region, along with the percentages it suggests and the conditions that change them.

Gratuities in Europe run from nothing at all to 15% of the bill, and the country on a traveler's itinerary decides where a given meal falls on that scale. Moving from Germany to France means swapping one code of table etiquette for another, so a single habit cannot cover a multi-country trip. Knowing which group a destination belongs to matters more than any one continental rule of thumb.

Rounding up the check or adding 5% to 10% is standard practice in Austria, Germany, Italy, and Greece. Capital One $COF suggests this is likely the norm a traveler meets most often across the continent. The type of venue determines how much. At a relaxed café or casual restaurant, a diner can leave the leftover change or an extra euro on the table. A formal restaurant calls for 5% to 10%. In these four places, a few coins can settle a casual meal, but the tab at a formal dinner carries a percentage worth calculating.

Sit-down restaurants in France and the United Kingdom look for 10% to 15%, so the minimum there matches the maximum for the round-up group. A service fee printed on the bill changes the math. Its presence means the gratuity sits inside the total, and diners owe nothing on top. Anyone who adds 15% to a bill listing that charge ends up tipping twice.

Businesses aimed at tourists break from these national patterns, having come to anticipate an American-style tip of 15% or more. Capital One leaves the decision in those spots to each traveler's best judgment. The tourist rate begins where France and the United Kingdom top out, and it exceeds the formal-dining ceiling in Austria, Germany, Italy, and Greece by at least 5 percentage points. Carrying the tourist rate into an ordinary café in Italy or Greece, where leftover change suffices, would overshoot local custom by a wide margin.

Restaurant tips across Central and South America top out around 15%, and the guide wants them handed over in local currency. Diners in parts of the region leave gratuities as Americans do, yet the sums run smaller than those back in the U.S. Visitors should keep the tip off the credit card slip and leave their U.S. dollars in the wallet. Local cash stays the default whenever travelers can manage it.

Brazil and Costa Rica ask for nothing extra. In those two countries, the gratuity either comes built into the bill or holds no place in local dining culture. Capital One $COF offers both explanations for the pair without matching a reason to each country. Either way, a visitor can pay the total and walk away without adding anything.

Argentina, Chile, Ecuador, and Nicaragua set the baseline at about 10% for good service. Some restaurants in those four countries print a service fee on the bill. Diners who spot one can add another 5% to 10% on top, a gesture the guide reserves for exceptional service. The extra functions as a bonus and carries no obligation. A standout meal where the fee appears on the check can cost that fee plus up to 10% more.

Mexico and Peru follow the U.S. pattern, with waiters there using tips to supplement their pay. Travelers $TRV need not match their usual American amount, and the bank's advice puts the right range at 10% to 15%. Its upper end sits 5 percentage points above the 10% or so that Argentina, Chile, Ecuador, and Nicaragua expect. A diner in either country should leave something, scaled down from American norms. A traveler who tips by U.S. reflex will overpay at most tables in the region, and in Brazil and Costa Rica any tip at all exceeds what custom asks.

## Middle East swings from 10% in Egypt to 20% in Qatar

Gratuity expectations in the Middle East climb from 10% in Egypt and Jordan to as much as 20% in Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates. Customs across the region diverge from one country to the next. Some governments write tipping rules into law. Others follow habits close to American ones.

Egypt and Jordan call for 10% to 15% of the bill. A service charge on the check does not release diners from that obligation. Visitors in both countries should leave a gratuity on top of the added fee. The charge and the tip count as separate items, and travelers owe both. A diner who reads the printed fee as the whole story will underpay by the full customary amount.

Restaurant bills in Israel often fold in the gratuity. Patrons there should scan the check before reaching for their wallets. A built-in charge still does not close the account, according to the guide. Leaving a couple of notes in local currency for the server remains the norm. All three countries expect something past the printed fee, though the size of that extra differs. In Egypt and Jordan, the charge leaves the full customary tip still owed, while in Israel a small cash token beside the bill satisfies local practice.

Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates set the bar at 15% to 20% for good service. The higher range depends on how well the staff did, a condition the guide leaves off the Egyptian or Jordanian amount. Americans in those three countries can tip the way they would at home. On a $100 restaurant bill, the gratuity works out to $15 to $20, compared with $10 to $15 in Egypt or Jordan. Hotel stays in Dubai follow a separate pattern. Guests there pay gratuities through the bill and owe nothing more.

The 15% floor in Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates matches the ceiling in Egypt and Jordan. A traveler who tips at the top of the scale in Egypt would meet the bare minimum in Saudi Arabia.

## Asia turns a tip into an insult in China and Japan

In China, Japan, and South Korea, a tip left on the table can strike the server or the restaurant owner as an insult. Wait staff in all three countries draw a regular wage with no link to gratuities. A few coins meant as thanks can offend the worker who finds them or the business that signs the paychecks. No other region in this selection comes with a warning that the gratuity itself can backfire. Travelers $TRV in these three countries spend a little less on meals as a result. The printed total marks the end of the transaction.

A 10% service charge often appears on restaurant checks in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. Local custom asks diners to add loose change or a few extra dollars on top of that charge. Visitors in these three countries can treat the listed fee as the main payment for service and leave a small token beside it. Diners in this group pay for service, yet much of the reward arrives as a line item.

Restaurants in India, the Philippines, Taiwan, and Vietnam sometimes fold the tip into the total. A diner in these four countries should scan the receipt before any cash comes out. Where it shows no gratuity, about 10% of the amount owed matches what servers expect. The guide places Taiwan in this cluster, apart from China, Japan, and South Korea. A person touring Taiwan and Japan on one trip needs two different habits at the table, with nothing owed in one and roughly 10% in the other.

Three of the 10 Asian countries the guide names expect nothing, and the other seven settle around 10%. India, the Philippines, Taiwan, and Vietnam leave that share to the diner whenever the bill lacks it. Restaurants in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore often print the 10% on the check, and the customary spare change or few extra dollars nudges the total past that mark. A traveler who reaches for 15% or 20% out of U.S. habit would outpace the roughly 10% baseline the guide sets across most of the continent.

A diner at a nicer restaurant in Australia or New Zealand can leave 10% to 15% and have it read as polite, in a region that grew up without a tipping habit. The guide describes local norms as muddled in recent years, with customs moving toward those of the U.S. and parts of Europe. The upscale range shows where outside habits have taken hold, and the guide presents it as good manners with no suggestion that diners owe it.

Casual stops sit outside the change. A coffee shop or bar calls for no gratuity, according to the guide. A traveler grabbing a coffee or a drink can pay the menu price and walk out. Tipping in Australia remains a matter of choice, and most venues there do not expect it. Visitors to New Zealand face no requirement to tip at restaurants and bars, though they may choose to reward good service. A polite gesture at a nicer restaurant stops short of an obligation, so the guide's restaurant range and the local advice fit together.

Bills in both countries arrive without a built-in service charge. Hotels and restaurants across Australia omit such fees, although some venues may add one for a large group. New Zealand hotel and restaurant bills carry no added service charge either. Outside a big group booking, a traveler has no built-in fee to subtract before deciding what to leave. Whatever a diner adds lands on top of a bill that charged nothing extra for service.

The guide's final word on the region hands the decision to the traveler. It advises going with instinct and tipping whatever amount feels right, a looser instruction than the percentage ranges it lays out for regions such as Europe and Asia. A traveler who skips the gratuity at a coffee shop and adds one after a strong meal at a nicer restaurant stays within everything the guide describes.
