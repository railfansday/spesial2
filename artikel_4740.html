This 108-Year-Old’s Daily Routine Includes 2 Easy Leg Exercises You Can Do at Home

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Susan Young Browne, who still lives independently, begins every morning with this movement.

Earlier this year, Susan Young Browne from Delaware celebrated her 108th birthday—the news of which travelled across the world. Everyone wanted to know the retired teacher’s longevity secrets. How did Susan, who had just renewed her driving license until 2033, retain such a good quality of life?

Well, movement, it seems, has played an integral part. Firstly, Susan, who still walks unassisted, told TODAY that she continues to attend chair dancing classes at a local community centre.

But she’s also committed to consistent, everyday movement.

Susan begins every morning with a 10-minute stretching, strengthening, and balancing routine.

With flexibility, mobility, strength and balance all being key markers of longevity, Susan’s routine is a reminder that workouts do not need to be long and complicated to have far-reaching benefits.

Another key part of Susan’s daily exercise routine that likely contributes to her impressive healthspan (the number of years spent living in good health and free from chronic disease)? Her focus on leg strength.

“I get up and stand against the wall and exercise my legs. I make sure my legs are exercised,” Susan told TODAY.

Not only do strong legs help us move freely and reduce fall risk, but a growing body of evidence also points to an association between lower-body strength and cognitive function—another key element of longevity.

One oft-cited study found that older adults with greater leg strength had 34% lower odds of low cognitive function than those with weaker legs. Meanwhile, a 10-year study of more than 300 twin sisters reported “a striking protective relationship” between leg power and cognition. The twin with greater leg power at the beginning of the study maintained their cognitive function better and showed fewer age-related brain changes over the following decade.

While the exact mechanisms behind this association remain unclear, among scientists’ hypotheses are that stronger muscles may improve blood flow to the brain, that resistance training may influence nervous system function, and that lower-body strength can help preserve walking speed and mobility—both of which have been associated with a lower risk of dementia.

Using a wall for support, Susan can be seen balancing on one leg, which is a simple, effective, single-leg exercise that supports strength and balance.

Another simple move that only requires a wall? As the name suggests, a wall sit.

“Defined as an isometric exercise—an exercise where the working muscle doesn’t change length during the exercise and the joint doesn’t move—wall sits are super safe to perform, and put very little stress on the spine,” personal trainer Aimee Victoria Long previously explained to Women’s Health.

Mainly targeting the quadriceps (the muscles at the front of your thigh that support knee stability and everyday functional movements such as walking, getting up from a chair, and climbing stairs), wall sits can also improve endurance, balance, core, and lower-back strength.

Another simple, no-equipment exercise that supports functional lower-body strength is the humble bodyweight squat. Plus, one study even found that doing 60 seconds of squatting exercises during long periods of sitting may help preserve cognitive function.
