Why Airline Joint Ventures Are Accused Of Keeping Transatlantic Fares High

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Three antitrust-immunized joint ventures now dominate transatlantic aviation: United Airlines with Lufthansa and its Star Alliance partners, Delta Air Lines with Air France-KLM and Virgin Atlantic, and American Airlines with British Airways and Iberia. A Wendover Productions video says they control roughly 75% of New York to Europe flights and labels them cartels, a framing that belongs to the video rather than to regulators.

The underlying accusation is more precise: partners who share revenue and set fares together stop competing on the nonstop routes where their networks overlap. Defenders say the same coordination lowers prices for most travelers who connect. Both claims have empirical support, and evidence from the Department of Justice and a study commissioned by the Department of Transportation shows where each holds, where it weakens, and why the argument has persisted for years.

An alliance alone does not permit pricing coordination, since carriers in an arm’s-length codeshare each set their own prices, with the operating airline keeping the revenue and the marketing partner collecting a booking fee. Antitrust immunity changes that arrangement by allowing partners to decide fares, schedules, and other competitively sensitive matters jointly across the routes included in their agreement.

The joint ventures go further by pooling revenue, as partners in a metal-neutral structure share ticket revenue on covered routes under a capacity-based formula, leaving them indifferent about which airline flies a given passenger. A study for the Department of Transportation described the result as carriers behaving like a single airline on the routes in question. American, British Airways, and Iberia argued in 2009 that this removes the incentive to keep a partner’s nonstop flight out of the other carrier’s reach, because a customer choosing it would otherwise transfer revenue away from the selling carrier.

Joint ventures exist partly because ownership rules in the United States and European Union preclude cross-border mergers, making revenue-sharing agreements the closest available substitute for a single airline. DOT approves them under a public interest standard that can weigh foreign policy goals. The video adds that the pooled partners publish matching prices on overlapping flights, citing United and Brussels Airlines selling the same 6:00 PM Brussels departure at the same fare, which it presents as evidence of price fixing.

In a 2011 discussion paper, Antitrust Division economists William Gillespie and Oliver Richard analyzed fares from 2005 to 2010 on 115 routes linking the 20 largest cities in the United States and the European Union. They counted immunized partners serving the same route as a single competitor and found that average one-way fares were $31 higher on routes with one independent nonstop competitor than on routes with two, $62 higher than with three, and $88 higher than with four.

Those gaps equal roughly 7% of the average fare for each competitor lost, and each was statistically significant at the 99% confidence level. The authors read the result as consistent with the ordinary antitrust presumption that reducing competition raises prices, and they observed that nonstop fares averaged 8.3% above connecting fares, yet most economy passengers on the sampled routes still chose nonstop service.

The paper also documents how concentrated the market has become, since three immunized groups, one in each alliance, carried more than 82% of US-EU passenger traffic in the year ending June 2009, and by late 2010 more than half of the sampled routes had a single independent nonstop competitor. Because foreign-carrier fares for itineraries without a US carrier are not public, the economists treated US-carrier fares as representative, a limitation that qualifies any reading of the data. The authors stressed that the paper reflects their own views rather than an official Justice Department position.

Defenders of the structure rely on double marginalization, the idea that when two unaffiliated carriers price their own legs of a connecting trip, each ignores how its markup reduces demand for the other, so the combined fare ends up too high. Partners that share revenue internalize that effect, which should lower fares for itineraries that require both carriers.

A study by economists Jan Brueckner and Ethan Singer, performed under contract to the Department of Transportation and released in 2019, tested the idea on fares from 1997 to 2016. It found that joint venture itineraries were priced about 7.2% below interline tickets, and that their fares were statistically indistinguishable from those of single-carrier trips. On transatlantic routes, the joint venture discount reached roughly 9% across cabins.

The Justice Department paper offered a rebuttal rather than a denial, since its economists agreed that alliances reduce connecting fares relative to interline tickets yet found that within SkyTeam and Star Alliance, immunized itineraries were no cheaper than non-immunized alliance itineraries, implying the pricing benefit does not require immunity. In the 2019 study, alliance partners without immunity priced just 0.7% below interline tickets, compared with 6.4% for immunized partners, which supports the claim that immunity matters. The two bodies of work therefore agree that connecting fares fall under alliances and disagree mainly on whether immunity is what makes them fall. The Justice Department economists added that 68% of US-EU passengers connected in 2009 across all itineraries, which is why the efficiency claim matters even though nonstop routes carry the higher fares.

The same Transportation Department study also found evidence that critics cite, reporting that in the later years of its 1997-2016 sample, granting immunity or joint venture status to two previously unaligned carriers on an overlapping route was equivalent to removing a competitor, with fares rising accordingly. The effect appeared in economy class, where an additional immunized or joint venture pair raised fares by about four to 13% depending on the specification, while business class fares showed no such overlap effect. That finding is notable because most earlier studies had found no nonstop effect, a point the authors flag as unusual.

The authors nonetheless concluded that alliances are beneficial on balance, based on a simulation of removing two joint ventures. On one route, nonstop passengers would save about $2.1 million a quarter while connecting passengers would pay about $3.1 million more; on another route, nonstop passengers would save roughly $469,000, while connecting travelers would pay about $10.8 million more. Brueckner and Singer also noted that the exercise ignores convenience gains such as single check-in and shorter layovers, which would further favor alliances.

Several cautions apply: the simulation covers two unnamed routes, the study pools regions, and the authors call removing a single joint venture unrealistic because it would affect other routes. Studies using structural models, which the authors cite, found no evidence of collusion on nonstop fares, so the literature remains divided on whether partners truly price together.

Critics argue that the harm persists because new competitors struggle to enter the overlapping routes. Slot scarcity at hubs such as Heathrow, where the video says slots sell for tens of millions, makes an incumbent partnership’s capacity hard to challenge. DOT has sometimes required remedies, including the divestiture of four Heathrow slots by the oneworld applicants, before granting immunity in 2010. The Justice Department economists also noted that applicants offered few specifics on new service, observing that American launched Dallas/Fort Worth to Madrid more than a year before receiving immunity.

Carve-outs, which bar partners from coordinating fares on specific overlapping routes, have a weaker record. DOT excluded routes such as Frankfurt to Chicago from United and Lufthansa’s 1996 grant, yet the exclusions apply only to tickets sold at US points of sale, and the Brueckner and Singer analysis found carve-outs typically had no measurable fare effect. DOT has also said that carve-outs can inhibit the efficiencies of a metal-neutral venture, and it has not required them when partners proposed one.

Immunity can also reshape who competes at all, as a study cited by Brueckner and Singer found that nonaligned carriers’ traffic between alliance hubs and other cities fell as alliances formed, which its authors read as foreclosure, and another found that immunity lowers partners’ costs, making them more formidable and potentially discouraging entry. The video counters that non-immunized partnerships, such as codeshares with Qatar Airways, Emirates, and Riyadh Air, deliver network integration without antitrust waivers.

A federal appeals court recently signaled how much analysis a regulator must show before curtailing an immunized venture. On August 20, 2026, the Eleventh Circuit vacated DOT’s order ending the Delta and Aeromexico joint venture because the agency relied on conditions at a single airport instead of conducting the comprehensive country-pair and city-pair analysis it had always used before. Any future challenge to a transatlantic venture would therefore need route-level fare evidence of the kind the Justice Department economists assembled, and that evidence now looks dated, since the Justice Department paper uses data through 2010 and the Transportation Department study stops at 2016.

The next test is already forming, since on November 4, Lufthansa asked DOT to complete its review of a request to add ITA Airways to the United, Lufthansa, and Air Canada venture. A decision will show whether regulators weigh nonstop harm against connecting benefits when partners expand a venture rather than when they first form one.

The research points toward a narrower remedy than the all-or-nothing debate suggests, because harm concentrates in economy-class nonstop overlaps, business fares show little overlap effect, and connecting passengers gain from integration. Conditions that tie immunity to measurable capacity or slot commitments on specific overlapping routes, with published fare data to verify results, would test the claim that partners deliver benefits unaided competition would not. Until such data exist, both the cartel label and the efficiency defense will remain easier to assert than to prove.
