The 10 Best American Shows in TV History, Ranked

My favorite thing about television is that it can make somebody's stupid little habits feel important. A man adjusts his tie before lying to his wife. A child keeps waiting for a parent to say something encouraging. Or Kieran Culkin’s Roman Roy does disgusting things in the bathroom to continue living depressed.

American television has given us extraordinary spectacles, groundbreaking comedy, and dramas that changed the entire medium. But the shows I return to most aren't necessarily the ones with the biggest twists or most prestigious reputations. They're the ones that give me excellent lessons. That understands people so precisely that I feel personally implicated in their decisions. And yes, I'm still furious about things certain fictional characters did twenty years ago.

Lucy Ricardo (Lucille Ball) wants something more exciting than being a New York housewife, much to the exhaustion of her bandleader husband Ricky (Desi Arnaz). I Love Lucy turns that simple conflict into magnificent comedy, frequently involving their neighbors Fred (William Frawley) and Ethel Mertz (Vivian Vance). Lucy's ambitions aren't unreasonable. It's her methods of achieving them that tend to become spectacular disasters.

I still laugh watching Lucy and Ethel desperately stuff chocolates into their mouths when the candy factory's conveyor belt speeds up. There's no complicated joke to explain. Two brilliant performers gradually lose control of an increasingly ridiculous situation. But Ball's real achievement was making Lucy's desperate hunger for attention strangely lovable. She could be selfish, dishonest, and gloriously theatrical, yet her disappointments felt recognizable. Seventy years later, that physical comedy remains unbelievably sharp. Some sitcoms owe this show practically their entire existence.

Homer Simpson (Dan Castellaneta) works at a nuclear power plant despite demonstrating little evidence that he should be trusted near one. His long-suffering wife Marge (Julie Kavner) keeps their household functioning, while Bart (Nancy Cartwright), Lisa (Yeardley Smith), and Maggie navigate childhood in the delightfully dysfunctional town of Springfield. The Simpsons transforms an ordinary American family into a bottomless source of cultural satire, absurdity, and occasionally surprising tenderness.

Give me “Marge vs. the Monorail” and I'll happily defend the show's funniest writing for an hour. The town is talked into buying an obviously suspicious transportation system, and practically every exchange contains a joke worth remembering. Yet “Lisa's Substitute” affects me more deeply. Mr. Bergstrom recognizes Lisa's intelligence and loneliness, and his farewell note tells her something she's desperately needed to hear. That's the magic of the show's greatest years. It can make Homer an unbelievable idiot, then have him express genuine love for his daughter in a way that completely destroys me.

A man boards an airplane and becomes convinced something is destroying its wing. Another discovers that his childhood hometown might offer an escape from his unhappy adult life. Ordinary people encounter extraordinary situations in The Twilight Zone, Rod Serling's anthology series about fear, morality, loneliness, and humanity's talent for making terrible decisions.

I have an enormous soft spot for “Walking Distance.” Martin Sloan (Gig Young) returns to the town where he grew up and somehow encounters his younger self. What begins as a beautiful fantasy about recovering childhood becomes a painful lesson about the impossibility of living in the past. Serling understood that a science-fiction premise didn't need elaborate machinery to become unforgettable. Sometimes all it required was a lonely man desperately wanting one more afternoon of happiness. The show can be terrifying, funny, devastating, or cruelly ironic. Its imagination still embarrasses productions with a hundred times the resources.

There are practically no respectable people in the lawless mining settlement of Deadwood, and I wouldn't have them any other way. Saloon owner Al Swearengen (Ian McShane) controls much of the town through intimidation and cunning, while former marshal Seth Bullock (Timothy Olyphant) struggles to establish something resembling order. Deadwood watches a filthy collection of opportunists, fugitives, workers, and fortune seekers slowly become a community.

McShane is absolutely magnificent. Al can threaten somebody's life, deliver an outrageously profane monologue, and then demonstrate an unexpected concern for the town's survival. David Milch, who created the series, writes dialogue that sounds almost Shakespearean without diminishing the characters' vulgarity. I particularly love how Bullock and Al gradually recognize the necessity of cooperating despite despising each other's methods. There's something strangely beautiful about civilization emerging from such unpleasant circumstances. Nobody becomes a saint. They simply begin recognizing that other people's lives are connected to their own.

Logan Roy (Brian Cox) has built a media empire, and his four children have spent their lives competing for his approval and eventually his position. Succession introduces Kendall (Jeremy Strong), Shiv (Sarah Snook), Roman (Kieran Culkin), and Connor (Alan Ruck) as extraordinarily wealthy adults whose emotional development appears to have stopped somewhere around adolescence.

A phone call from an airplane abruptly forces the siblings into a situation that their money, influence, and practiced cruelty cannot control. They stumble over words, interrupt each other, and desperately try to communicate feelings they've spent their entire lives avoiding. It's breathtaking acting. I love how Jesse Armstrong's writing makes these people simultaneously hilarious and pathetic. Their boardroom victories are often elaborate attempts to win a father's affection. They possess practically everything imaginable, except the emotional security that might allow them to enjoy any of it. The ending was heartbreaking. This is probably one of the greatest shows of all time.

Don Draper (Jon Hamm) is an extraordinarily successful advertising executive whose polished appearance conceals a carefully constructed identity. At Sterling Cooper, he sells beautiful versions of American life while struggling to maintain anything resembling genuine happiness in his own. Mad Men also follows Peggy Olson (Elisabeth Moss), Joan Holloway (Christina Hendricks), and the colleagues navigating an industry transformed by social change during the 1960s.

I will never stop thinking about the Kodak Carousel pitch. Don uses photographs of his family to sell nostalgia, describing the emotional power of returning to a place where we felt loved. It's a wonderful presentation, made devastating by how badly he's damaging those very relationships. Matthew Weiner, who created the show, understood that Don's professional brilliance came partly from recognizing desires he could never satisfy himself. And Peggy's growth is equally extraordinary. She enters the agency as a secretary and slowly discovers how much of herself she's unwilling to surrender for approval. This show understands ambition, loneliness, and the exhausting business of pretending to be somebody else.

Before becoming Saul Goodman, Jimmy McGill (Bob Odenkirk) is a struggling lawyer desperate to establish himself while living under the judgment of his successful older brother Chuck (Michael McKean). His relationship with fellow attorney Kim Wexler (Rhea Seehorn) offers something dangerously close to stability. Better Call Saul follows Jimmy's transformation while demonstrating how much damage two intelligent, affectionate people can cause when they start enjoying their own dishonesty.

I genuinely think “Chicanery” contains some of the finest acting ever produced for television. Chuck's courtroom testimony deteriorates into an eruption of resentment, exposing how deeply his feelings toward Jimmy have shaped both their lives. Yet the tragedy belongs to both brothers. Jimmy wants Chuck's respect, but his methods keep justifying Chuck's worst assumptions. And Kim! Seehorn makes her gradual moral compromises almost physically painful to witness. Vince Gilligan and Peter Gould created a prequel that somehow made the destination more heartbreaking because we understood exactly what Jimmy was throwing away. I could watch these people negotiate a legal technicality for an entire hour.

Walter White (Bryan Cranston) is a high-school chemistry teacher diagnosed with lung cancer who begins manufacturing methamphetamine to provide financial security for his family. He partners with former student Jesse Pinkman (Aaron Paul), gradually building a criminal enterprise that places everybody around him in danger. Breaking Bad turns a desperate financial decision into one of television's most horrifying studies of pride.

What absolutely destroys me is watching Jesse continue searching for affection from a man who repeatedly manipulates him. Walt recognizes Jesse's need for approval and exploits it whenever convenient. Meanwhile, his wife Skyler (Anna Gunn) becomes trapped in circumstances that Walt keeps insisting are for the family's benefit. Cranston makes Walt's transformation frightening because each terrible decision arrives with an explanation that sounds reasonable to him. By the time he finally acknowledges how much he enjoyed the power, the damage is almost impossible to comprehend. I love this show for refusing to let intelligence excuse cruelty. Walt's greatest talent becomes the weapon he uses to ruin everything.

Four criminal masterminds. One of them was always going to be yours. Here is who comes for you, why they picked you out of everyone available, and what they want from the encounter.

Your nemesis does not want to beat you. He wants to know you completely, and he is patient enough to wait for it. The frightening part is how good the conversation is.

Your nemesis is not after money, territory or revenge. He is after a demonstration, and you are the person he has chosen to prove his point on.

Your nemesis respects you, which sounds flattering until you work out what it means. He chose you because nobody else was worth his time.

Your nemesis has no personal interest in you whatsoever, and that is precisely what makes him unsurvivable. You are not a rival. You are a stop on the way.

Tony Soprano (James Gandolfini) runs a New Jersey criminal organization while attending therapy for panic attacks. His psychiatrist Dr. Jennifer Melfi (Lorraine Bracco) attempts to understand a patient whose anxieties involve his mother, his marriage, his children, and the people he's ordered killed. The Sopranos places a deeply troubled family man inside a profession that rewards practically every one of his worst instincts.

The argument between Tony and Carmela (Edie Falco) in “Whitecaps” is devastating. Years of resentment come pouring out, and neither can escape the uncomfortable truths the other knows. They're married people who understand exactly where to hurt one another. Gandolfini and Falco make the confrontation feel almost indecent to witness. That's David Chase's extraordinary achievement. He never lets us forget Tony's brutality, even while making his personal struggles painfully recognizable. I can laugh at Tony complaining about his family, then feel revolted when he inflicts suffering upon somebody else. Few television characters have demanded so much sympathy while doing so little to deserve it.

Baltimore's drug trade, police department, shipping industry, political establishment, schools, and newspapers become the interconnected subjects of The Wire. Detective Jimmy McNulty (Dominic West) initially investigates a drug organization led by Avon Barksdale (Wood Harris), but the series gradually expands far beyond any single investigation. The institutions remain, even as the people attempting to change them come and go.

Season 4 is the reason I will defend this show forever. Four boys enter a school system where their futures are already being shaped by poverty, street violence, and decisions made by adults who rarely understand their lives. Watching Randy (Maestro Harrell) desperately seek help as his circumstances deteriorate is heartbreaking. And Bubbles (Andre Royo), a man struggling with addiction, eventually reaching a point where he can join his sister's family upstairs for dinner feels like an extraordinary victory. The police may celebrate an arrest, politicians may announce progress, but somebody's child still has to walk home through the same dangerous neighborhood. That's why The Wire belongs at number one.
