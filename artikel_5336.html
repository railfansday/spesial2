Airbnb CEO Brian Chesky says staff call him a ‘control freak’—but that he learned it from Apple’s Steve Jobs

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Airbnb’s billionaire CEO Brian Chesky recently admitted that some of his workers probably call him a “control freak.” But he says he’s in good company, as he picked up the habit from studying Apple’s Steve Jobs.

“People called him a control freak. He probably was a control freak,” Chesky told Time. “He held people to extremely high standards. He spent a lot of time recruiting. He was in the details.”

When asked whether Airbnb staff would say the same, Chesky responded: “I think some people do.” He also spends 3 hours a day recruiting and tells his hiring managers to leave if they don’t like his input.

But he insists that doesn’t mean he’s micromanaging his workforce—and he even talked to the man who worked closest to Jobs to prove it.

Apple’s former design chief, Jony Ive, worked alongside the late cofounder for nearly 15 years in designing iconic products like the iMac, iPod, iPhone, iPad, and Apple Watch. And so Chesky once point-blank asked Ive what it was like working for Jobs.

“Johny said to me, ‘I never felt like Steve was micromanaging,” Chesky recalled, adding that Ive’s felt like they were just “constantly partnering” because Jobs wasn’t outright telling him what to do. He just wanted to be in on the details. They’d even have lunch together every day. “Constantly working back and forth,” Chesky added.

And that’s where the Airbnb boss draws the line. He said “micromanagement, where you’re like telling people what to do” is a last resort—and probably an indicator of how well they’re going to do at his company.

“If I’m telling somebody what to do, they’re probably not successful,” Chesky added. “So the best thing is we’re partnering. I’m asking questions. What if we did this? What if we did that? Now I’m not doing that with my chief accounting officer, I’m not doing that with a tax attorney. I’m doing that in areas where I think it can add value, like product, marketing, operations, things like that.”

Chesky has defended this idea before, arguing that “micromanagement” isn’t about control; it’s about helping people think bigger and move faster.

In a previous interview, Chesky explained that being in the details shouldn’t diminish anyone’s autonomy or make your reports feel like the boss is hovering over their shoulder or undermining them. Instead, it should make them feel like their manager is invested in them.

“So here’s the question: If I’m in the details with somebody, am I making them better or am I disempowering them? And I hope that when people feel like I’m involved in projects, they feel like I’m helping them push to think bigger,” he told CNBC.

With over 9 million active listings in over 150,000 cities in 220 countries and over 8,200 employees, Chesky also argues that getting into the weeds is necessary to get things done swiftly.

“There’s a paradox where being in the details sounds like micromanagement; it sounds like it’s slowing teams down,” the 45-year-old entrepreneur continued in the CNBC interview. “But when you’re in the details, you can actually help make decisions faster.”

Essentially, layers of approval, endless meetings, and hours of wasted worker time can be saved when someone with real sign-off power is in the room.

“The number of people in organizations that [have] to get through managers and managers and managers to approve something, but then all these leaders have to agree to something, there’s a bunch of meetings—peers can’t make fast decisions,” Chesky added. “Only a leader can make a quick decision in a room. I bring everyone in the room, everyone makes a recommendation, and we can make a really, really fast decision.

“I think that’s the key of a leader, to make decisions,” he said.

Orianna Rosa Royle is the Success associate editor at Fortune, overseeing careers, leadership, and company culture coverage. She was previously the senior reporter at Management Today, Britain's longest-running publication for CEOs.
