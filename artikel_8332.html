Walker’s Haute Route: From Mont Blanc to the Matterhorn

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

The Walker’s Haute Route is one of the most spectacular long-distance hikes in the Alps. Stretching 137 miles, it connects Chamonix beneath Mont Blanc with Zermatt beneath the Matterhorn, crossing numerous high mountain passes through France and Switzerland along the way.

Duration: 10–14 days Distance: 137 miles Starting Point: Chamonix, France Ending Point: Zermatt, Switzerland Total Elevation Gain: Approximately 45,930 ft, depending on the route Difficulty: Demanding alpine hiking, requiring a high level of physical fitness Helpful Planning Resources: Snow depth maps, protected areas where camping and bivouacking are prohibited

We set out in mid-July, before the peak hiking season begins. Many mountain huts are still closed, extensive snowfields remain on some of the higher passes, and the Grand Désert is still covered in snow. At the same time, we find ourselves in the middle of an extraordinary heat wave, with temperatures reaching up to 100°F. It’s a combination that makes this trek more varied and challenging than we expected.

Wildflower-covered alpine meadows, turquoise glacial lakes, ibex, marmots, secluded campsites, and breathtaking views of Mont Blanc, the Weisshorn, and finally the Matterhorn make the Walker’s Haute Route one of the most beautiful hikes we’ve experienced in the Alps so far.

Randa – Visp – Martigny – Vallorcine – Chamonix

After driving to the Mattertal the previous day, we take the train to Chamonix in the morning, where we will start the Walker’s Haute Route through the French and Swiss Alps. We leave our van at the campground in Randa and walk to the train station, beginning a four-hour journey involving four different trains through some spectacular scenery.

The first train through the Matter Valley is crowded, with no seats available, so Ryan and I end up in the bike compartment. There aren’t any bikes or people, so we jokingly declare it our VIP lounge.

Visp is busy but surprisingly straightforward. We quickly find our next platform and even manage to grab seats. The difficult part isn’t navigating the station but dealing with everything at once. I have to make a phone call while searching for seats in the right compartment, all while a child nearby screams at the top of their lungs. It’s one of those moments where my brain quietly decides it has had enough.

In Martigny we meet an Australian woman who’s hiking the Tour du Mont Blanc. Her knee has started acting up, so she’s taking public transport to shorten a stage while still making it to the refuge she booked. Hopefully she’ll still get to enjoy the experience despite the setback.

Our next train to Vallorcine is considerably louder. A large group of teenagers fills most of the carriage, and the bathroom is closed while we both really need to take a leak. Thankfully, the final train towards Chamonix is much quieter, with panoramic windows looking out over steep valleys and dramatic cliffs. The bathroom technically works this time, although the wet floor and questionable smell make it not very appealing.

By the time we arrive in Chamonix, it’s already lunchtime. Before doing anything else, we head straight to the restaurant we discovered last year on our Tour du Mont Blanc. For around €18 we get a full meal with unlimited salad, which is still one of the best deals in town. Ryan orders fish with potatoes, asparagus, and white wine sauce, while I happily stick with lasagna. Somewhere between multiple trips to the salad bar, I discover that butter and cucumber make a surprisingly excellent combination.

We need to talk about French butter. It’s so good! The crunchy salt brings a whole other layer of joy. Last year, Ryan thought this would be soft cheese and took a big bite out of it before realizing it’s even better than that.

One more stop remains before we can finally start walking. We visit a gear shop to buy an ice axe for Ryan. Hopefully we won’t need it, but having them gives me a little extra peace of mind for the snowy sections ahead.

Panoramic window on the train to Chamonix

Chamonix – Argentière – Le Tour – Just Below Col de Balme 11.4 miles / 4,265 ft elevation gain / 5 hrs

At 3 pm, we leave Chamonix behind and officially begin the Walker’s Haute Route. At first, the trail is less of a walker’s route and more of a biker’s route. But little by little the crowds disappear, and the further we get away from town, the better we feel. We enter the forest, following the river with the snow-covered granite spires of the Dru towering above us. Ryan finds the first tick of the trip attached to his leg, which is a less welcome reminder that summer has arrived.

When we pass a railway tunnel, I check the timetable and realize the train we arrived on will be passing in just a few minutes. We scoot onto the hillside above the tunnel and wait. When it finally emerges, the loud horn echoes through the valley, startling both of us. This must be one of the most beautiful train rides in the world, and we get a nice shot of the train passing by.

Train from Chamonix & the small town of Le Tour

The town of Argentière comes and goes. We could stop at the campground there, but we neither feel like dealing with people nor stopping just now. Instead, Ryan heads into the supermarket while I wait outside, slightly overwhelmed by a few minor problems piling up (my phone battery has died, the charging cable I packed doesn’t work, and the busy streets are becoming increasingly exhausting).

Ryan returns with the essentials: cheese, chips in another new flavor we have to try, and Orangina. Sadly, no baguette.

Beyond Argentière, the route becomes less pleasant for a while, weaving between roads, fences, and small paths. As we gain elevation, the scenery changes. We first climb through the forest, then cowbells echo across the hillside, wooden chalets sit surrounded by flower-covered meadows, and the mountains begin to dominate the view again.

We pause on a bench for a while before continuing through Le Tour, where flower boxes spill over balconies. From there we start the long evening climb towards Col de Balme.

Just before sunset we spot a deer with a bright white muzzle standing beside the trail, munching on some grass, while we’re quietly watching each other. Another one appears only a few minutes later, trotting along the dirt road below us.

We try to catch the last sunlight below the pass, but the mountains win that race. Instead, just below the col, we find a perfectly sheltered campsite with one unforgettable view: Mont Blanc glowing orange in the fading evening light. Dinner tastes even better with that view.

Just Below Col de Balme – Buvette du Glacier – Fenêtre d’Arpette – Forest Before Relais d’Arpette 9.4 miles / 4,593 ft elevation gain / 8 hrs

The morning begins with the short remaining climb to Col de Balme. The first rays of sunshine light up the surrounding peaks as we cross into Switzerland. Instead of following the standard Walker’s Haute Route into Trient, we take the Les Grands alternate, staying high above the valley.

I immediately know we made a good decision, as we see loads of TMB hikers on the path below. We don’t see people for the next few hours, but pink alpine flowers that cover the slopes, buzzing with bees and dotted with butterflies. The trail traverses steep mountainsides with uninterrupted views across the Trient Valley. A few lingering snowfields remain from winter. Two of the traverses look intimidating at first, but by carefully kicking steps into the snow with our microspikes, we make our way across safely. Alternatively, it would be possible to avoid the steepest sections by walking around them.

The trail rolls along the mountainside. It isn’t technically difficult, but it certainly keeps us working with one steep climb followed by an equally steep descent.

Only as we descend towards the Buvette du Glacier do we start seeing other hikers again. Along the way, I continue my ongoing struggle with the French language. Every time I manage to ask a simple question, I receive a long, enthusiastic answer delivered at full speed that leaves me behind with a question mark on my face. This time, I just wanted to know if Buvette du Glacier is open, as we’re looking forward to lunch.

And then a beautiful sight: the hut down by the river is open. Without hesitation we order apricot tart, sandwiches smeared with butter and topped with pickles, cured ham and salami, and cold cola. Everything tastes fantastic after the warm morning. We stretch out in the shade for a while, knowing the biggest climb of the day is still waiting above us.

After crossing a suspension bridge above the roaring glacial river, the trail turns uphill towards Fenêtre d’Arpette. The climb is long, but every switchback brings us closer to the Glacier du Trient. Soon the glacier fills the valley ahead, its thick ice spilling down the mountainside. Meltwater disappears beneath the glacier into a snow cave before emerging as the river below.

By a little cabin where we refill our water, we meet a young Frenchman with his grandfather. We notice the grandfather doesn’t carry a backpack and would soon find out when we see the young man heaving two backpacks lashed together on his shoulders. We have a chat with him, and he explains that his grandfather’s knees are hurting a lot. What a kind gesture.

Ryan, meanwhile, still can’t reliably identify the stinging nettles he didn’t grow up with in Southern California. So, I keep calling out things like “Stinging nettle to the right!”. It becomes a running commentary for the rest of the climb.

As the afternoon progresses, clouds begin building over the surrounding peaks. Thankfully, they also block the sun, making the final ascent noticeably cooler. We spot our first ibexes of the trip on a rock above the trail, effortlessly navigating terrain that would leave us clinging to the rocks.

Just below the pass, I discover a perfect pink marshmallow lying on the trail. At almost exactly the same moment, Ryan finds a one-euro coin. At the top, I happily eat my marshmallow. If you ask me, I got the better deal.

Dark clouds are moving in quickly, so we hurry to get down. The upper section is almost entirely boulders. Lower down, one remaining snowfield offers a much faster route. Ryan happily glissades down the snowfield, while I prefer to run down the soft snow. Not long afterward we hear the first marmot whistle of the hike, the sometimes startling alarm system of the Alps. They should have a sign in the rocks that this area is protected by the marmot security company.

The descent through the next boulder field is slow and careful. Thunder rumbles somewhere in the distance, but the expected storm never reaches us. Instead, only a light drizzle follows us down the valley. What strikes me most is the silence. There are no birds singing, no rushing streams nearby, and no wind. Just complete stillness.

Later that evening we find a sheltered campsite in the forest where someone has built a fire ring despite Leave No Trace principles. All sorts of flying insects buzz constantly around us, cuckoos are calling out, and a deer barks somewhere deeper in the woods.

As darkness settles over the valley, the forecasted storm has still not arrived. Sometimes the mountains decide to keep the drama for another day.

Forest Before Relais d’Arpette – Champex-Lac – Orsières – Below Mont Brûlé 12.3 miles / 5,020 ft elevation gain / 6.5 hrs

We wake to birdsong, pack up, and descend through a peaceful forest alongside a babbling brook as it tumbles over moss-covered cascades towards Champex.

The village bakery turns out to be exactly what hungry hikers hope for. We settle in for a leisurely petit déjeuner buffet, recharge both ourselves and our electronics, and leave with a fresh baguette and some pastries for the trail. While Ryan restocks our groceries, I enjoy the luxury of simply sitting still for a while.

Before leaving the mountain village, we head down to the lake. Our “shower” consists of walking into the water, splashing ourselves as thoroughly as possible, and calling it good enough. Hiker trash hygiene at its finest.

Refreshed for all of five minutes, we continue downhill towards Orsières. The valley is like an oven. Even though we’re only descending, the heat is relentless. By the time we reach Orsières, our brains feel like they’re braising like a cheap piece of meat on a Sunday. We escape into the grocery store for cold drinks and sit in the shade contemplating the climb ahead, watching the restaurant in front of us setting up public viewing for the ongoing football world championship.

Most Walker’s Haute Route hikers continue through the valley from here. Instead, we choose the Mont Brûlé alternate, immediately turning uphill again to regain the mountains on the other side of the valley.

The climb begins gently enough, weaving through quiet villages, roads, and open meadows. A thermometer by beekeeper’s boxes lets us know that it’s 36°C. Every fountain becomes an oasis. We drink deeply, soak our hats in the cold water, and linger for a minute before pushing on to the next patch of shade. We also get an occasional spray from the sprinklers that water the grass.

The shaded forest feels wonderfully cool. Each time we emerge into the sun, I convince myself it must have cooled down by now. It never has. Cuckoos call from somewhere deep in the woods while broad-leaved plants sway beside the trail, almost looking as though they’re waving us onward. Eventually the forest opens, and wildflowers cover the alpine meadows while cowbells echo across the slopes. This is what summer in the Alps sounds like.

Far above us, gliders circle through the warm afternoon air. Occasionally, the faint whistle of one cutting through the sky reaches us. Their silhouettes and that eerie sound remind me of giant pterodactyls soaring over the mountains.

The trail climbs steadily towards the pass, and with every step, the panorama grows wider. Behind us, the jagged peaks of the Mont Blanc massif fill the horizon – Grandes Jorasses, Mont Dolent and Aiguille d’Argentière dominating the skyline. Mont Blanc itself remains hidden, but in a landscape like this, it hardly matters.

We decide to call it a day here and pitch our tent in a nice sheltered spot at the saddle. Ahead, the route continues towards Mont Brûlé while another towering peak reveals a glacier tucked beneath its upper slopes, patches of snow still clinging stubbornly to the mountain despite the summer heat.

After spending most of the day climbing through scorching temperatures, it finally feels like we’ve returned to the high mountains where this route truly belongs.

Mont Brûlé – Col de Mille – Cabane Brunet – Fionnay – Cabane de Louvie – Col de Louvie – Lac du Grand Désert 17.1 miles / 7,382 ft elevation gain / 10.5 hrs

The morning begins with one of those campsites you almost regret leaving. We get up to watch the sunrise. Glaciers catch the first light across the valley, and we pack up to climb to the summit of Mont Brûlé. We refill our water at Cabane de Mille and continue along a beautiful balcony trail towards Cabane Brunet.

The views are spectacular, and so is the heat. Before long we come across a small alpine lake that looks impossible to resist. We throw down our packs and jump straight in. The water is painfully cold, but afterward life is perfect for a few glorious minutes. We’re watching some small trout swimming in the lake and munching on some chips before continuing.

Ten minutes later we’re sweating again. The sun is relentless, and every rare shadow cast by a boulder feels like stepping into air conditioning. Cabane Brunet appears like an oasis in the desert. We have cold Coca-Colas in the shade, recharge a few electronics, and postpone leaving the shade for a while.

The descent towards Fionnay is knee-bustingly steep, partly eroded, and overgrown. The trail dives through fragrant pine forest where warm needles and resin scent the air, while mosquitoes remind us we’re not the only ones enjoying the day.

Then we step back into full sunshine. It’s hotter than a coyote in a Christmas sweater. Below us, two reservoirs shimmer in the sun while the next climb rises above Fionnay, and it’s straight up. It’s a slightly discouraging sight in this heat.

After crossing flower-filled meadows where the trail disappears beneath the vegetation, we finally reach the valley floor. It looks like a paradise for ticks, but we manage not to catch any. We cross the road, followed by another steep uphill, but at least it’s mostly shaded in the forest.

Fionnay is wonderfully quiet, with barely anyone around. We sit down by the only open restaurant in town and order Croûte au fromage, a wonderful Swiss dish that features bread soaked in white wine, topped with a generous amount of melting cheese, and baked until bubbling and golden. To balance out all that cheese, we also order a salad.

We’re not looking forward to the next climb in the heat, but we really need to push it. The entire area around the upcoming Lac de Louvie is protected, meaning camping isn’t allowed. If we want to spend the night legally, we have to cross Col de Louvie this evening.

So back uphill we go. The climb is steep, and we’re walking from patches of shade to the next. Every breeze earns an audible “Ahhh…” from both of us. Some parts are secured by ropes drilled into the rocky wall.

Late in the afternoon we reach Cabane de Louvie beside the lake and reward ourselves with yet another Coca-Cola while chatting with two hikers from New Zealand. The cabin also comes with a cat, but it doesn’t feel very social today.

Climb to Lac de Louvie & Cabane de LouvieClimb to Lac de Louvie and Cabane de LouvieJust as we’re getting ready to leave, the hut guardian comes outside.

“Where are you going?” she asks. “Col de Louvie” I reply. “Now?”

She explains that it’s a three-hour climb. I confirm. It’s 5 pm, so we have plenty of daylight. Then she goes on, saying that camping isn’t allowed. I explain that we’ll camp after the pass, outside the protected area. I clarify that we know the regulations and will camp beyond the boundary.

Then comes another warning. There’s still lots of snow. There would be no footsteps. Nobody has crossed yet. I mention that we happen to know someone who successfully crossed just last week. She gives up, waves us off, and disappears back inside.

We had already heard stories about her discouraging hikers from attempting the pass, so the conversation doesn’t surprise us. Still, I’m relieved when we finally leave the hut behind.

The climb gains another 750 meters to the pass. Marmots whistle from the rocks around us while chamois move across the steep rocky slopes as if gravity simply doesn’t apply to them.

A group of ibex watches us from the cliffs above us. We stop for a moment to admire them until the first rocks come down. The ibex keep kicking loose stones as they move around the slope. None of them are huge, but they bounce downhill, gathering speed with every impact, and several come directly towards us. Suddenly we’re no longer wildlife watching. We’re trying to get out of the line of fire as quickly as possible.

Rockfall doesn’t have to involve boulders to be dangerous. Even a fist-sized rock bouncing downhill at speed can cause a serious injury. It’s easily the most frightening moment of the Walker’s Haute Route for me, and I’m relieved once we’ve finally passed below them.

Soon, we’re crossing some soft snowfields that send us punching through every few steps, and we’re carefully trying to avoid some really deep-looking holes. Contrary to the warning, the route clearly shows signs of recent hikers. There are footprints where we’d expect them. Behind us, however, the sky is becoming increasingly threatening. Dark clouds gather over the massif, seemingly drifting past us but close enough that neither of us wants to linger.

Descent to the Grand Désert from Col de Louvie

We reach Col de Louvie without further incident. The real challenge begins on the other side as the Grand Désert is still in winter mode. Half-frozen lakes stretch across the plateau. Snowfields cover nearly everything, and each step sinks unpredictably into the soft snow.

Postholing becomes unavoidable. Twice my leg disappears so deeply that my shoe gets stuck, and Ryan has to dig me out. A little later he gets trapped himself, and it’s my turn to pull.

Cold snow works its way into our shoes, and the temperatures are dropping quick as soon as the sun is gone. The constant postholing leaves our legs aching while our hands go numb from digging ourselves out. Finally, our feet get soaked by crossing an icy meltwater stream, and big raindrops start to hit us.

Somewhere around here, we lose the trail entirely as it’s buried under the snow, but at this point it hardly matters. Our priority is descending below the snowline, where camping becomes possible again while thunder rumbles closer. By the time we finally spot snow-free ground beside a lake, I also notice two tents already standing there. We hurry down, literally running to make it in time before the storm hits.

Never has a campsite looked so inviting. I pitch the tent in record time, abandoning any thoughts of perfection. The only thing that matters is to get warm and dry inside. We can fix it later.

Ryan tells me he feels like he’s assisting a surgeon.

“Stake!” I call out. He hands me a stake. “Pole!” A trekking pole appears in my hand. “Tighten!” Done.

We crawl inside just before 9 pm. Only moments later, the storm arrives. Heavy rain drums against the tent walls while thunder and lightning roll across the mountains. Fortunately, the worst of it stays just far enough away.

Neither of us cares. I’m happy as a clam to be out of it. We stuff sour gummy candies into our mouths while warming up in our sleeping bags and resting while darkness settles in. We’re exhausted from the long day in the heat, the climbing, and the snow, hiking from sunrise to sunset. I would have just skipped dinner altogether, but Ryan cooks up some pasta on our little stove anyway. He’s right. After a day like this, we need every calorie.

Lac du Grand Désert – Col de Prafleuri – Cabane de Prafleuri – Col des Roux – Refuge de la Barmaz 6.2 miles / 2,838 ft elevation gain / 5.5 hrs

The morning begins where yesterday ended: in deep, soft snow.

From our campsite below the snowline, it takes us an entire hour to get back to our route. Navigation is difficult, as the trailblazers painted on the rocks are often hidden by the snow, and footprints are scarce.

Every few steps, one of us sinks into another posthole. The snow has become so wet that it barely supports our weight anymore, especially around rocks, where hidden voids seem to wait for unsuspecting hikers. After crashing knee-first onto a rock hidden beneath the snow, I quickly learn to treat every boulder with suspicion. Ryan gets trapped once again, having to dig himself out, yelling curses to the world in the process. I can’t help laughing a bit, though.

Climb through the snowy Grand Désert to Col de Prafleuri

The terrain keeps us busy. One accidental glissade is followed by another one on purpose. At one point we carefully step across a wide melt gap between rocks and the snowfield before quickly committing to a steep glissade, the fastest and safest way to get off the rather unstable slope.

Even water breaks come with questionable decision-making. We cross a stream only a few meters before it plunges over a cliff as a roaring waterfall. Ryan decides this is the perfect place to refill his water bottle. I decide not to watch.

Nearly three hours after leaving camp, we finally reach Col de Prafleuri. We collapse onto the rocks, relieved to be finished with the endless snowfields while happily working our way through sandwiches. Looking back, it was exactly the kind of experience hikers affectionately call Type 2 Fun: miserable while you’re doing it, strangely satisfying almost immediately afterward.

The descent couldn’t be more different. Real trail appears beneath our feet again. Dirt instead of snow. We can already spot Cabane de Prafleuri far below and some enormous solar panels reflecting the bright sun.

Near an abandoned stone building, we discover two chamois enthusiastically licking the walls, leaving behind silhouettes of saliva the size of their bodies. Apparently the stones contain enough minerals to make them worth the work.

The hut itself is still closed for the season, but the cozy winter room is open. Even the toilet comes with spectacular mountain views and a few chamois wandering across the hillside outside.

View of Col des Roux & Cabane de Prafleuri

From there we continue towards Col des Roux. Compared to the morning, the pass is almost relaxing. Most of the remaining snow can easily be avoided. Along the climb we meet a 72-year-old Swiss woman hiking in the opposite direction, carrying an impressively small pack with an ice axe, dressed in shorts and long gaiters for the snow. Easily the coolest person we meet on the WHR. Last year she completed a 700-kilometer trek starting in St. Moritz and is now on her way to Chamonix by herself. She doesn’t seem impressed by the difficulty of this. Then she wonders about our trail runners and if we have wet feet all the time. And yes, we do have wet feet a lot, but we don’t mind much. We ask about the snow conditions at Col de Riedmatten, our next major obstacle.

“It’s fine.” Exactly the answer we were hoping for.

Beyond the pass, the landscape opens to reveal Lac des Dix. The enormous turquoise reservoir stretches through the valley like a Norwegian fjord, enclosed by steep rock walls with countless waterfalls cascading down into the glacial water.

Standing here, it’s impossible not to admire the engineering behind it. The lake is held back by the Grand Dixence Dam, the tallest gravity dam in the world. Built in the 1950s, it relies entirely on its immense weight (around 15 million tonnes) to withstand the pressure of the water behind it. More than 100 kilometers of tunnels collect meltwater from 35 glaciers across the Swiss Alps, some of it even pumped uphill, turning the reservoir into a giant natural battery that supplies hydroelectric power to roughly half a million Swiss households.

Our slow progress through the snow this morning changes our plans for the day. Rather than pushing all the way to Arolla and risking another afternoon thunderstorm on the next pass, we stop at the unmanned Refuge de la Barmaz. It turns out to be one of our favorite places on the entire Walker’s Haute Route.

We have the whole hut to ourselves. Ibex wander across the slopes outside while marmots scurry around everywhere. So many fluffy butts. But there’s also small things like plants holding tiny pools of water in their leaves that sparkle like diamonds in the afternoon sun, and fuzzy caterpillars crawl through the grass.

Inside, large metal jugs are provided for fetching water from the spring outside. Walking back with one in each hand makes me feel like a milkmaid from a century ago. Only with internet reception. And raspberry syrup from the hut. Life is good.

Right on cue, another thunderstorm arrives. Heavy rain drums on the roof while we stay dry inside, drinking tea and playing games. When the storm finally moves on, the mountains begin to glow beneath the clearing sky, clouds drifting around the craggy peaks. Later that night, countless stars appear overhead.

Sometimes stopping early turns out to be the best decision of the day.

Refuge de la Barmaz – Col de Riedmatten – Arolla – La Gouille – Les Haudères 15 miles / 3,593 ft elevation gain / 8 hrs

The morning begins with an easy stroll along Lac des Dix, a welcome change after yesterday’s challenge. Marmots seem to be everywhere. Hundreds of them whistle, scurry through the grass, and disappear into their burrows.

Then we hear some rather unusual noises. Curious, we round a corner and accidentally interrupt a marmot couple in the middle of their private moment. One disappears into the rocks while the other casually starts eating as though nothing had happened.

At the end of the lake, a powerful waterfall crashes down the cliffs, sending spray across the trail. Then it’s time to ascend again. My body needs a moment to realize we’re now in climbing mode.

The Walker’s Haute Route never disappoints. Every few minutes I find myself blurting out things like, “Oh my gosh,” or “This is so beautiful!” Towering glaciers spill down the mountainsides like cappuccino foam, feeding another brilliant turquoise lake below. I honestly struggle to put the scenery into words. I’m in awe.

The climb to Col de Riedmatten is straightforward. A few lingering snowfields remain near the bottom, but otherwise the route is dry. The final section is extremely steep and prone to rockfall, making me glad we’re climbing it rather than descending.

On the way down from the pass, I’m flooded with gratitude. I love my life. I love this life. I’m aware that I’m privileged to be able to do this. But this is where I feel at home. This is where I feel at ease. Where nothing else matters. Past, future, endometriosis, bone necrosis, depression, traumatic experiences. Everything vanishes, and I’m just… here. Alive and well in this moment. Very well.

Along the way we meet Adrian, who is hiking in the opposite direction from Zermatt. He confirms that the route ahead is in good condition and recommends stopping for lunch at the Grand Hôtel in Arolla.

That turns out to be excellent advice. After descending into Arolla, we trade our usual hiker meals for something entirely different. Sitting in the hotel’s immaculate garden, still dusty and smelling distinctly like thru-hikers, we enjoy what feels like a fine dining experience for surprisingly reasonable prices. I order slow-cooked pork belly with parsnip variations, coconut emulsion, and a rich jus that is especially delightful. Ryan chooses fresh pasta with truffle cream, poultry reduction, Parmesan foam, and generous truffle shavings. What a treat for two filthy hikers.

A fancy meal at the Grand Hôtel in Arolla

Before leaving town, I search unsuccessfully for a replacement charging cable. Looking back, maybe I should have bought the one for 25 CHF in Champex after all.

The afternoon climb out of Arolla is warm, humid, and lined with stinging nettles. Eventually we reach Lac Bleu, which is indeed very blue, which results from a combination of algae and clay minerals. It’s absolutely stunning, but it’s also Saturday, and since the lake is only about a forty-minute hike from the trailhead, it’s considerably busier than most other places we’ve visited on the Walker’s Haute Route. Beautiful or not, I urgently need to take care of business number two.

We leave, and just a few steps away, we see this little hut with a sign for tarte aux abricots. And there’s a toilet, so win-win. The ladies running this place are like the grandmothers you always wanted. Ryan practices some phrases of French on them, and we get the tarte. We like it so much that we order another slice. Apricots are practically a culinary specialty in Valais, where around 95% of all Swiss apricots are grown. In my home region of Bavaria, the seasonal equivalent would be Zwetschgendatschi, a traditional plum cake. Of the three apricot tarts we had on the Walker’s Haute Route, this one would turn out to be the best.

The final descent towards Les Haudères isn’t especially memorable. One side of the narrow trail is lined with stinging nettles, the other with an electric fence. Ryan gets zapped, and I get the stinging nettles. Neither of us wins that one. Luckily there’s a spring only a few minutes later where I can cool the burning sting.

We stop by the bakery and grocery store to get supplies, and as usual, I find the supermarket overwhelming. By the time we reach the campground, I’m completely done. The receptionist spends more than twenty minutes on the phone while we wait in the afternoon heat. Children scream from the swimming pool beside the office. I’m overheated and increasingly nauseous. All I want is a shower.

After pitching the tent, I barely want to move. Heat exhaustion has finally caught up with me. I drink electrolytes, and then Ryan slowly walks me over to the shower block. I feel much better after the shower, the first after six days.

The campground is surprisingly quiet for a Saturday evening. Only the following morning do we learn why: a major cycling event is taking place nearby, and most of our fellow campers have gone to bed early for the race.

Les Haudères – La Sage – Col du Tsaté – Lac de Châteaupré – Lac de Moiry – Col de Sorebois – Above Zinal 11.7 miles / 7,808 ft elevation gain / 8.5 hrs

After several brutally hot days, this morning feels almost refreshing. We leave Les Haudères early and begin the long climb towards Col du Tsaté while the air is still pleasantly cool. It doesn’t stay that way for long, but at least we’re getting lots of today’s elevation gain out of the way before lunchtime.

The trail climbs steadily through alpine meadows before reaching a small lake so perfectly still that it mirrors the surrounding mountains. We don’t hesitate for long before jumping in.

The water is freezing and exactly what we need. We dry off in the sun while eating fresh bread with thick slices of butter we packed out from yesterday’s resupply. Surprisingly, it’s still quite solid. Today turns out to be an excellent day to carry a stick of butter around.

Clouds begin drifting across the sky just as the final steep climb towards the pass begins. The relentless sunshine disappears, making the ascent far more pleasant than we’d expected.

At Col du Tsaté we linger for a while, enjoying the panorama. Above us, a couple picks their way across an impossibly steep scree slope with no visible trail whatsoever. French and Swiss hikers really seem to operate on another level sometimes.

We descend towards another chain of brilliant blue lakes with Glacier de Moiry dominating the skyline. The last few passes have been easy, with no snow slowing us down. Only one small snowfield remains near the end of this descent, just long enough for a quick, fun run down the soft snow.

Halfway down the valley, we’re interrupted by a loud crash. Then another. Up on the mountain across the valley, rocks tumble down a steep cliff, sending clouds of dust into the air. Every few minutes another section breaks loose, the echoes rolling through the mountains long after the rocks have disappeared.

At Lac de Châteaupré, the atmosphere changes completely. The glacier-fed lake is stunning, its characteristic milky turquoise color created by fine glacial sediment suspended in the water. But unlike the solitude we’ve enjoyed for most of the hike, this place is busy. A parking area sits beside the lake, buses arrive regularly, and a steady stream of hikers heads uphill towards Cabane de Moiry. That hike is no joke, 450 m of elevation gain, and it’s getting quite steep and rocky in the end. But you get an eye-level view of the glacier and probably some good food as a reward.

For us, the crowds are reason enough to keep moving. As soon as we leave the lake behind, the trail becomes quiet again. A beautiful balcony path follows the shoreline high above Lac de Moiry, accompanied by spectacular views and what feels like hundreds of marmots. Every few minutes another furry little face pops out of the grass before disappearing again.

We stop once more for another piece of bread and butter overlooking the lake. And then it starts hailing on us a bit, passing quickly. Apparently even the weather wants a bite.

At the end of the reservoir, we lose a little elevation before beginning the final climb to Col de Sorebois, accompanied by more marmots. Together with the sweeping views over the lake below, I’m in heaven.

By now, though, my legs are beginning to feel the accumulated elevation. More than 2,300 meters of climbing in a single day eventually catches up with me. We reach the pass with just enough daylight left to think about camp. There’s only one problem: we’re almost out of water. Fortunately, not far below the pass, we find a small stream where we refill everything before pitching the tent beside a pond.

View of Lac de Moiry from the climb to Col de Sorebois

We finish just as heavier rain arrives, soon turning into hail. Safe inside the tent, neither of us minds. The best part of the evening, however, has nothing to do with mountains.

Ryan discovers a hoverfly inside the tent. We name him Hubert. Somehow he’d spent the entire day inside our tent. Even though I’d shaken it out that morning, he’d survived being packed away, albeit in pretty rough shape.

First, Ryan assumes he’s dead, then notices the tiniest signs of life. Over the next hour, he carefully nurses him back to strength with drops of water before collecting tiny flowers for him to feed on. Slowly, Hubert starts moving again. Eventually our little hitchhiker even manages a few short flights around the tent. We decide that’s a good sign.

We’ll see how Hubert is doing tomorrow. For now, we enjoy the sunset painting the sky red and reflecting in the small pond beside our tent.

Above Zinal – Hôtel Weisshorn – Cabane Bella Tola – Lac de la Bella Tola 15.8 miles / 4,757 ft elevation gain / 7.5 hrs

The first marmots announce the start of a new day. Hubert, our hoverfly companion from yesterday evening, has spent the night inside the tent with us. Before packing up, Ryan gently coaxes him out from a corner of the inner tent. Hubert climbs onto his finger, Ryan reaches outside, and together we watch him disappear into the morning air.

Hoverflies only live for about four weeks. I hope the rest of his are good ones.

Our first destination is Zinal. Breakfast consists of exactly what we’ve come to expect from Swiss villages: an excellent bakery, delicious tea served in beautiful porcelain, and more pastries than any reasonable person needs. We order a brioche filled with vanilla custard while we recharge our electronics, then somehow also end up with another apricot tart. At this point we’ve developed a simple rule. If you see an apricot tart, buy it. You won’t regret it.

Afterward, we visit the supermarket to continue our scientific comparison of local cheeses and potato chips. Today’s selections include Bébris, Tomme, and chips with french fries sauce flavor. Highly recommended.

One thing we don’t manage to find is a gas canister. Apparently the two outdoor shops in Zinal have somehow forgotten the “outdoor” part of their business. One of them does, however, have a magnificent long-haired black cat lying peacefully on the counter. No gas. Excellent cat. I’ll take it.

Leaving Zinal immediately reminds us that villages are always found at the bottom of valleys. The climb is steep, the day is hot, and regular Orangina breaks in the shade become essential.

Eventually the gradient eases, and we make a spontaneous decision. Instead of following the standard Walker’s Haute Route over Col de Forcletta, we take the alternate via Hôtel Weisshorn and Meidpass. It turns out to be one of the best decisions of the hike.

First, we have a late lunch at the historic Hôtel Weisshorn, first opened in 1883 and rebuilt after a fire in 1891. Every beam and every stone had to be brought up the mountain by mule. The piano, however, required six men. History aside, we’re mainly interested in lunch. The menu du jour begins with vegetable soup followed by roast beef with salad and fries, washed down with a Coke. The contrast between the chilled beef and the hot fries is delightful. I even have enough leftovers for dinner. Oh, and the view is spectacular, too, sitting high above the valley.

If you’re tempted to stay, a double room including breakfast costs around €160 which is remarkably reasonable by Swiss standards. Just don’t expect to book it at the last minute.

The afternoon is wonderfully gentle. After several days of relentless climbing, the gradual trail feels luxurious, and we can actually enjoy views of mountains and rocks that get better and better the higher we get. The slopes are covered in blooming pink with blooming alpine roses and patches of bilberries, and for the first time on this hike, we also catch a glimpse of the Matterhorn.

But it’s also hot as usual, and I feel like a baked potato. We pass a pig farm that radiates quite a smell in the heat. We both have the same thought when we see a stream of water gushing out of a pipe by the trail, creating a natural shower. I strip down and get underneath it. Ryan opts to go fully clothed and make it a laundry day too.

As refreshing as the shower is, another mystery keeps bothering me. For the third time on this trip, I notice a strong smell that reminds me of bile soap. My sensitive nostrils can’t ignore it, and the intense scent bothers me. Ryan, on the other hand, thinks it smells good. Some plants might be responsible. Perhaps Alpine clover? I can’t identify it with certainty. It may be the compound coumarin, which occurs naturally in various alpine plants. I’d still love to know the answer.

We continue climbing past Cabane Bella Tola before reaching the small alpine lake that will become tonight’s home. It’s wonderfully quiet. One of my favorite parts of carrying a tent is exactly this freedom. We can change our plans on a whim, take alternates simply because they look interesting, and stop whenever we find the perfect campsite. Tonight, that campsite overlooks Lac de Bella Tola.

We’re having sandwiches for dinner, and mine is filled with leftover roast beef and spring onions that make it a culinary highlight.

Lac de la Bella Tola – Meidpass – Gruben – Augstbordpass – Viewpoint Above the Mattertal Valley 11.9 miles / 4,888 ft elevation gain / 7 hrs

The day begins with the gentle climb to Meidpass while the morning air is still pleasantly cool. The pass rewards us with another beautiful panorama before we descend past the small Meidsee. One lingering snowfield remains, but it’s easy to cross and soon gives way to grassy slopes and scattered alpine hamlets as we continue down towards Gruben.

By now we’ve developed a routine whenever we pass through a village. Find shade, recharge electronics, recharge ourselves.

The church in Gruben provides all three. We sit in its shade, watching swallows dart in and out of their nests high above us while we stuff our faces with chips and our devices recharge inside. (Don’t worry, we leave a donation.)

Then it’s time for the last pass on the Walker’s Haute Route. Thankfully, the lower part of the climb winds through shaded forest because the heat remains relentless.

By lunchtime we stop beside a rushing mountain stream. We’ve run out of bread, so today’s menu becomes an improvised charcuterie board of cheese, salami, and potato chips. We cool our feet and soak our hats in the cold water before continuing upward.

The trail eventually disappears into a maze of enormous boulders. Somewhere in the middle we realize we’ve drifted off route. The official trail continues farther to the left, but neither of us has any enthusiasm for climbing back down just to climb again. Instead, we carefully traverse the boulder field until we reconnect with the trail near the top of Augstbordpass.

Just below the pass, we see a woman carrying a mountain bike uphill. Considering the terrain, I’m almost more impressed by that than by the climb itself. I have absolutely no idea how she plans to ride down the other side, but I’m sure she’s handling it well. Swiss people are just badasses, it seems.

We head down from the pass, past another lake. What appears straightforward at first becomes an undulating balcony trail. It’s scattered with more boulders and some rather sketchy lingering snowfields that we need to traverse carefully, making our progress slow and taking everything out of me. I’m very hot, although it’s cloudy, even looking like rain, and I just feel depleted. I want to lie down. I want to eat. Preferably both at the same time. At least we both feel the same way.

Then a proper dirt trail finally appears beneath our feet, I’m disproportionately relieved. It’s much faster than carefully navigating through snow and boulders. We round a corner, and the Matter Valley opens up below us, where the peaks of Weisshorn and Dom dominate the skyline.

Not long afterward, we find the perfect campsite overlooking the valley. It’s still a long descent to St. Niklaus, and with no campground down there and it being too far to go up again for us today, there’s little point in pushing farther today. We’re done.

Dinner is… creative. Potato and asparagus soup mixed with ramen noodles, accompanied by the remaining cheese and salami. Surprisingly, it’s much better than it sounds.

Europe is in the grip of a major heatwave, and after more than a week of hiking through it, we’re definitely feeling the effects. Earlier today I read that your sweat rate doubles in extreme heat, reaching up to three liters per hour during exercise. But heat training can also boost performance. So, it helps to frame it that way. Maybe all of this suffering is just exceptionally effective training.

Only two more days to go, then our alpine sauna comes to an end.

Viewpoint Above the Mattertal Valley – St. Niklaus – Herbriggen – Miesboden 11.6 miles / 4,429 ft elevation gain / 7 hrs

Today is our last full day on the Walker’s Haute Route.

We manage to start hiking at six in the morning. After yesterday’s early night, getting out of the tent feels unexpectedly easy, and the cool morning air reminds us that hiking early really is the way to go during a heatwave.

The long descent towards St. Niklaus passes through the hamlet of Jungen, where an unattended honesty stand offers drinks in a basket next to a still-closed restaurant. We happily grab a Coke, leave four Swiss francs in the cash box, and continue downhill. By the time we reach St. Niklaus, the grocery store has just opened, and their bread is still warm. Life is good.

Ahead of us, there’s a choice to make regarding the Europaweg. The original Europaweg was a highlight of the WHR, but the route was changed in 2021 due to constant rockfall that kept damaging the trail. Unfortunately, the new route now involves a steep climb to Gasenried, followed by an undulating forest trail before dropping to the valley floor at Herbriggen, where another stiff climb follows.

So, there’s basically three options now: 1) Complete Europaweg as described above 2) Complete Valley route (a good option in bad weather) 3) Hybrid: Walk the Valley Route to Herbriggen and then go up to Europaweg

We choose the latter, which is popular because it gets you the most scenic section of the Europaweg while avoiding the less rewarding detours.

We follow the valley road towards Herbriggen. It’s not the most exciting stretch of trail, but it gets us where we want to be. Just before the village, we discover a pizza restaurant. We sit in the shade, drink more Coke, recharge our electronics, and wait until the kitchen opens for lunch. Lunch is pizza. Dinner becomes a takeaway calzone. Future us will be truly grateful.

As expected, the further walk to Herbriggen is hot, and we’re frying in the sun that radiates off the asphalt. We walk by a reservoir that’s fenced off, like a sick joke. We look at it longingly while trudging on.

The following climb turns out to be the hardest climb of the entire Walker’s Haute Route. Not because it’s technically difficult or because it’s especially steep, but because of the excruciating heat, involving 1,300 m of elevation gain straight up.

The forest provides some shade, but the heat finds us anyway. I make some awful noises on the steepest parts. I’m suffering. Every hundred meters of elevation gain, I need another break. By this point I begin wondering whether I died somewhere back in the valley and this endless climb is actually my personal version of hell.

Fortunately, a shaded spring becomes the perfect sanctuary. A small wooden platform with benches sits beside it. Above it, a giant rosary with a picture of Jesus hangs from the rock wall. I drink as much cold water as I possibly can and press the cold water bottle against my skin. Together with some more food, it helps a lot for the rest of the climb.

A cool, shady spring becomes a welcome refuge

Several airy staircases cling to the cliffs, and below us is a roaring stream in a canyon with gushing waterfalls. Definitely not a place for anyone with vertigo.

A bit later we meet Laurens, a Belgian thru-hiker heading in the opposite direction. He’s only on day two of an ambitious two-month journey linking the Walker’s Haute Route, the Tour du Mont Blanc, the GR 5 to Nice, the Tour des Écrins, and finally the GR 20 on Corsica.

Sounds hard and hot, but after looking up his hiking resume online, I have no doubt about his abilities to rock this. Like us, he hiked the HRP last year but even yo-yoed it by taking the GR 11 all the way back. He also hiked the Colorado Trail in 2024, when we did the CDT, and he happens to know two Belgians we hiked it with. The thru-hiking world is small.

Also, I learn one of the most useful pieces of advice ever: “Pain is just bread in French!”

Higher up, the scenery changes once again, and the air finally becomes a little cooler. Glaciers hang above us while countless waterfalls crash into torrenting streams that disappear into the canyons below.

Near the end of the day, we cross a few heavily eroded traverses before eventually reaching Miesboden, where we enjoy the rest of the afternoon. Our last campsite of the Walker’s Haute Route overlooks the Weisshorn across the valley.

I’m glad the day is over, as I struggled so much with the heat today. We have our calzone that we thoughtfully bought several hours earlier, accompanied by a generous selection of trail snacks.

We enjoy the views until loud crashes echo through the mountains once more. A rockfall, or perhaps an icefall, sends dust cascading down the glacier beneath Weisshorn. But it’s not over with nature’s evening entertainment just yet. We’re watching the sunset paint the sky, followed by heat lightning flickering in the clouds above Weisshorn.

Long after darkness falls, the sound of glacial waterfalls continues until we finally drift off to sleep.

Campsite and sunset at Miesboden with a view of Weisshorn

Miesboden – Europahütte – Charles Kuonen Suspension Bridge – Täschalp – Tuftra – Sunnegga – Zermatt 14.5 miles / 3,281 ft elevation gain / 7 hrs

The cool morning air is one last gift before another brutally hot afternoon, and we happily take advantage of it. Today’s stage follows the famous Europaweg high above the Matter Valley. There are no major climbs left, just a series of smaller ascents and descents as the trail traverses the mountainside. The route is spectacular, and for hours we don’t see another person.

The trail winds across boulder fields, through a tunnel blasted into the cliffs, and over countless bridges, culminating in the Charles Kuonen Suspension Bridge. At 494 meters long, it’s one of the longest pedestrian suspension bridges in the world. Standing in the middle, looking 85 meters down into the valley while the bridge gently sways beneath every step, is just the right amount of unnerving.

Then, around a bend in the trail, it finally appears: the Matterhorn. After ten days of hard work, it’s a glorious sight. As we continue, it keeps changing its shape, depending on the angle. Transforming from a broad wedge-shape into the sharp, iconic silhouette everyone knows. It’s a mountain that never quite looks the same twice.

Beginning the Walker’s Haute Route beneath Mont Blanc in Chamonix and finishing beneath the Matterhorn provides a fitting frame for a spectacular hike.

Water has been plentiful for the entire hike, but after two consecutive dry camps, we’re searching for somewhere to refill our bottles the last two mornings. The first opportunity requires climbing down a rope to reach the stream. I’ve never had to rappel for water before, but Ryan is excited to give it a go and successfully retrieves it.

Unfortunately, there is a constant noise from helicopters circling the valley, and it makes it difficult for me to think. I start to get grumpy, and I find myself wishing there were someone I could file a formal noise complaint with.

Eventually we reach the quiet hamlet of Täschalp. The helicopters disappear. Cows graze peacefully in the meadows while a glacial stream rushes past, offering another welcome opportunity to cool ourselves down. We enjoy this little paradise, mentally preparing for the crowds waiting below in Zermatt.

By lunchtime we hope to celebrate with a proper meal at the restaurant at the Sunnegga gondola station, but it’s closed for unknown reasons. Oh, the disappointment. We settle for the last of our bread and cheese on a nearby bench instead.

By now the heat is almost unbearable again, and I feel like a melting candle. And getting close to town, I become very aware of my body odor. I don’t remember the last time I stank so much. We really need a shower and laundry.

The descent into Zermatt somehow manages to become even hotter. Every meter of lost elevation on this dusty trail seems to add another degree. I’m nauseous.

Exactly ten days after setting off from Chamonix at 3 pm, we eventually arrive in Zermatt at 3 pm. Mission accomplished.

Mission accomplished! Arrival in Zermatt and the end of the Walker’s Haute Route

We head straight for the hotel I’d booked two days ago, and I apologize for our smell to the poor receptionist Max, explaining that we just walked here from Chamonix. He seems to be used to it. Then it’s shower and sink laundry. I’m done.

Freshly clean at last, we celebrate the end of the Walker’s Haute Route with something decidedly less Swiss: Döner. It’s one of the few affordable meals in Zermatt. Discounted supermarket cake completes the feast.

Thunderstorms and heavy rain roll through the valley only a short while later.

One thing has become abundantly clear over the past ten days. We somehow have a talent for walking into heatwaves. Last year it happened on the Tour du Mont Blanc and again on the HRP. Now it’s happened on the Walker’s Haute Route as well. Extreme heat has become an increasingly common part of hiking in the mountains, and it can change these routes dramatically.

This time, though, we’re simply grateful to have finished before the next storm arrived.

The Walker’s Haute Route is not an officially marked long-distance trail and has no fixed, mandatory route. Instead of following its own dedicated trail markers, it uses existing hiking trails through the French and Swiss Alps. Over time, a commonly accepted standard route has emerged, but there are numerous alternates crossing different mountain passes and following different trails. This allows you to approach the hike as something of a “choose your own adventure” experience.

Recommended Alternates: Les Grands, Mont Brûlé/Fionnay, Meidpass
