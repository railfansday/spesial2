Nasdaq Stock Market Correction: These 6 Words From Warren Buffett Are More Relevant Than Ever

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

The Nasdaq recently fell into correction territory.

After years of unusually high gains fueled by artificial intelligence (AI) stocks, the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC -2.49%) recently entered correction territory as investors reassess the outlook for the AI industry.

On Oct. 6, the Nasdaq reached a record closing high of 27,599. By Nov. 5, the index had fallen to 24,315, representing a 12% decline. Although the Nasdaq has recovered a bit, the tech-heavy index still sits roughly 8% below its record.

This turbulence has created an interesting dilemma for investors. Should you sell your stocks before another potential drawdown, or take advantage of falling share prices?

Luckily, Warren Buffett offered an answer decades ago. One of his most famous investing principles is particularly relevant today.

The technology sector is facing several overlapping challenges. First, November's midterm elections resulted in Democrats winning control of both chambers of Congress. Throughout the campaign, electricity affordability, data center construction, and the environmental impact of AI infrastructure became a prominent political issue.

With Democrats assuming congressional control in January, investors are evaluating the possibility of increased regulatory scrutiny and changes to infrastructure permitting. This matters for hyperscalers like Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta Platforms, which have collectively committed hundreds of billions of dollars of capital to build data centers.

If new regulations lead to rising costs or project delays, big tech may choose to slow down their infrastructure spending. In turn, this could flow downstream and affect semiconductor manufacturers and other businesses supporting the AI ecosystem. Of course, potential policy changes have not yet materialized. Moreover, congressional control does not automatically translate into restrictions on data center development.

Another concern weighing on the Nasdaq involves Nvidia (NVDA -4.12%). On Nov. 12, the company's founder and CEO, Jensen Huang, resigned following reports concerning his personal relationships and questions surrounding potential foreign-intelligence compromises.

Nvidia's board of directors appointed CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO while it searches for a permanent replacement. The leadership transition introduces uncertainty for perhaps the most influential company in the AI industry.

Nvidia's scandals have also fueled scrutiny around other semiconductor companies and the broader AI landscape.

Throughout his career, Buffett consistently emphasized the importance of separating a company's underlying value from short-term fluctuations in its stock price. In Berkshire Hathaway's 1986 shareholder letter, he explained his approach with six simple words: "Be greedy when others are fearful."

This philosophy has become a cornerstone of value investing. Buffett doesn't view falling stock prices as an automatic reason to sell. Rather, the "Oracle of Omaha" evaluates whether a company's competitive advantages, earnings potential, and long-term intrinsic value remain intact.

If a financially strong business suddenly begins trading at a discount to its intrinsic value, market volatility can actually create an attractive opportunity to buy the dip.

The goal is to identify businesses with durable economic moats, strong balance sheets, dependable cash flow, and attractive long-term growth prospects. Consider companies such as Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Nvidia.

These businesses all have competitive advantages, including strong customer relationships, proprietary technologies, established ecosystems, and significant financial flexibility. Although their stock prices may fluctuate with changing expectations about the pace of AI spending, their underlying businesses are not necessarily deteriorating at the same pace.

This creates an arbitrage opportunity to purchase high-quality businesses at more reasonable valuations. By gradually building positions through dollar-cost averaging, investors can reduce the risks associated with investing a large sum immediately before another sell-off.

Most importantly, investors should maintain a long-term perspective. As the chart below illustrates, the stock market has endured its share of setbacks. The grey columns represent recessions, including those witnessed during the aftermath of the dot-com bubble, the Great Recession, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

The theme here is that the Nasdaq eventually recovered and established new record highs. These trends underscore the importance of remaining invested through volatility. In the long run, history suggests the Nasdaq's correction will look like another temporary setback in the stock market's long-term upward trajectory.
