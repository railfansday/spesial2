What causes rhabdomyolysis: 10 triggers beyond extreme exercise

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 triggers show what causes rhabdomyolysis beyond hard workouts, from statins and alcohol to heat stroke, infections and venoms, and how muscle breakdown can threaten the kidneys

Rhabdomyolysis has a reputation as a gym injury. The word tends to surface in news reports after a punishing spin class, a military training drill or a football practice that pushed too far. Yet plenty of cases have nothing to do with a workout. They start in a medicine cabinet, at a party, during a heat wave, in a hospital ward or on a kitchen floor after a fall.

The condition is the breakdown of skeletal muscle fibers. When those fibers rupture, their contents leak into the bloodstream. Three of those substances get the most attention from doctors. Myoglobin is the protein that stores oxygen inside muscle. Creatine kinase is an enzyme that serves as a marker of muscle damage in blood tests. Potassium is an electrolyte that, in excess, can disrupt the heart's rhythm.

Myoglobin is what puts the kidneys at risk. It can clog and injure the tiny tubules that process fluid filtered from the blood. Acute kidney injury is the main serious complication of rhabdomyolysis.

Knowing what causes rhabdomyolysis helps explain why it can go unrecognized at first. The classic signs are muscle pain, weakness and dark urine the color of tea or cola. Not every patient has all three. Someone who collapsed at home, or who is confused from fever or intoxication, may not be able to describe symptoms at all.

The triggers span a wide range. Some poison muscle cells directly. Others starve muscle of oxygen or fuel, overheat it or force it into relentless contraction. Medications, recreational drugs, infections, mineral imbalances, metabolic emergencies, inherited gene variants, animal venoms and food toxins all appear below. More than one trigger can act in the same person at once, such as alcohol combined with hours of lying immobile.

The 10 triggers that follow are a selection, not a complete or ranked list. Physicians recognize many other causes, including reactions to certain anesthetic drugs and to some antipsychotic medications. Each slide covers one type of trigger and explains how it harms muscle.

This article is not a tool for self-diagnosis. Rhabdomyolysis needs prompt medical care. Anyone with unexplained muscle pain or weakness and dark urine should seek emergency treatment, particularly after a fall, a new medication, a heat illness or a serious infection.

Sustained pressure on muscle can trigger rhabdomyolysis without any exercise. Crush injuries and hours spent motionless after a fall or loss of consciousness can cut off blood to muscle until the fibers begin to break down.

A crush injury happens when a heavy weight pins part of the body. Earthquakes, collapsed buildings, mine accidents and vehicle crashes are typical settings. British physicians Eric Bywaters and Desmond Beall described the pattern in 1941. They had treated people pulled from rubble during the London Blitz who later developed kidney failure.

The damage unfolds in two stages. While the weight stays in place, compressed muscle loses its blood supply and its cells start to die. When the pressure lifts, blood flows back in and carries the contents of those injured cells into general circulation.

That release makes the condition dangerous. A sudden surge of potassium can disturb the heart's rhythm soon after rescue. Myoglobin reaching the kidneys can cause acute kidney injury over the following hours and days. Doctors call this body-wide illness crush syndrome.

The same process can occur without a disaster. An older adult who falls at home and cannot get up may lie on the floor for hours. Body weight alone presses on the muscles of the hips, shoulders and limbs that rest against the ground.

Anyone who stays unconscious and still for a long period faces a similar risk. That includes people who pass out after a stroke or a drug overdose. Long surgical procedures that hold a patient in one fixed position can also compress muscle.

Swelling can make the injury worse. Many limb muscles sit inside tight sheaths of connective tissue called fascia. When injured muscle swells within those sheaths, pressure can climb high enough to restrict blood flow further. This problem is known as compartment syndrome.

Anyone found after a long period trapped or immobile needs prompt medical care, even if they feel well at first. Kidney and heart complications can develop after the person has been freed.

Statins, the cholesterol-lowering drugs taken by millions of people, can cause rhabdomyolysis, though this severe form of muscle injury is rare. The risk climbs with higher doses and when a statin is combined with certain other medicines, such as the fibrate gemfibrozil.

Statins work by blocking HMG-CoA reductase, an enzyme the liver uses to make cholesterol. Common examples include atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin, pravastatin and lovastatin. Doctors prescribe them to lower LDL cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke.

Muscle side effects from statins sit on a spectrum. At the mild end are aches, cramps and stiffness without measurable muscle damage. Further along is myopathy, in which weakness or pain comes with signs of muscle injury in blood tests. Rhabdomyolysis is the far end, with widespread breakdown of muscle fibers.

Certain drug combinations raise the risk. Gemfibrozil, another lipid-lowering drug, interferes with how the body clears several statins. That interference raises statin levels in the blood. Some statins, including simvastatin and lovastatin, are broken down by a liver enzyme called CYP3A4. Medicines that block that enzyme, such as the antibiotic clarithromycin and some azole antifungals, can push statin levels higher. Large amounts of grapefruit juice can have a similar effect on those particular statins.

Cerivastatin, sold by Bayer as Baycol in the U.S., was withdrawn from the market in 2001. The withdrawal followed reports of rhabdomyolysis, including fatal cases. Many of those reports involved patients who were also taking gemfibrozil.

Dose matters as well. In 2011, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration recommended restricting use of the highest 80-milligram dose of simvastatin because of its greater risk of muscle injury.

Other factors can make a person more vulnerable. These include older age, kidney or liver disease and an underactive thyroid. Taking several medications at once also adds to the chance of a harmful interaction.

Unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness while taking a statin should be reported to a doctor. Dark, tea-colored urine alongside those symptoms calls for prompt medical care, because rhabdomyolysis can damage the kidneys.

Heavy drinking can trigger rhabdomyolysis without any strenuous exercise. Alcohol injures skeletal muscle directly, and a bout of intoxication can add hours of immobility and fluid loss on top of that damage.

Alcohol and the compounds the body produces while breaking it down can damage muscle cell membranes. They can also disrupt the cells' energy supply. When a membrane fails, the cell's contents spill into the bloodstream. Myoglobin, creatine kinase and potassium all escape this way.

Physicians call the sudden version of this injury acute alcoholic myopathy. It can follow a single bout of very heavy drinking. Affected muscles, often in the arms and legs, become painful, swollen and tender. They may also feel weak. A separate chronic form develops slowly in long-term heavy drinkers. It mainly causes muscle wasting and weakness rather than abrupt breakdown.

Immobility adds a second route to injury. A person who passes out may lie in one position on a hard floor for many hours. The weight of the body compresses the muscles underneath and restricts their blood flow. Intoxication also blunts the discomfort that would normally make a sleeping person shift.

Fluid and mineral balance can suffer too. Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, a hormone that signals the kidneys to conserve water. Lower vasopressin means more urine and a higher risk of dehydration. Long-term heavy drinkers frequently eat poorly. Their bodies can run short of minerals that muscle cells need to work normally. That deficit leaves the tissue more fragile.

Abrupt withdrawal after prolonged heavy use creates its own risk. Withdrawal can bring tremor, intense agitation and, in some people, seizures. Each places heavy demands on muscle that may already be damaged.

These mechanisms can act together. A single night can combine direct toxic injury, hours of compression and dehydration.

Myoglobin released from the damaged muscle can harm the kidneys. Dehydration from drinking can add to that strain.

Severe muscle pain, swelling or weakness after heavy drinking needs prompt medical care. So does unusually dark urine.

Cocaine, amphetamines and MDMA can cause rhabdomyolysis by pushing the body into extreme agitation, nonstop muscle activity and overheating. The resulting muscle damage can injure the kidneys. Stimulant-related rhabdomyolysis is a medical emergency.

All three drugs stimulate the central nervous system. They raise heart rate, blood pressure and muscle tone. A person under their influence may pace, thrash, struggle or keep moving for hours without rest. Muscle held in that state burns energy faster than the body can resupply it. Its cells then begin to fail.

Overheating compounds the damage. Stimulants increase the body's heat production and can interfere with the normal ways the body sheds heat. High body temperature injures muscle cells on its own. A user who becomes hyperthermic therefore faces two sources of muscle injury at once.

Cocaine carries an additional risk. It narrows blood vessels, which can reduce blood flow to muscle tissue. Muscle deprived of oxygen-rich blood is more vulnerable to injury, especially while it is also working hard.

MDMA, sold as ecstasy or molly, is often taken at clubs, raves and music festivals. Those settings can combine hours of dancing, crowded rooms and warm air. That environment adds to the drug's own heat-raising effect.

Methamphetamine belongs to the amphetamine family and has been linked to the same pattern of agitation and hyperthermia. Synthetic cathinones, sometimes sold as "bath salts," act in a similar way. They have also been tied to rhabdomyolysis.

High doses of stimulants can also provoke seizures. Each convulsion forces muscles into repeated, forceful contractions. That adds to the strain already caused by agitation and heat.

Physical restraint during severe agitation can add to the problem. A person who is held down may keep straining against the restraint for a long period, working muscles that are already under stress.

Danger signs after stimulant use include confusion, a very high body temperature, a seizure, muscle swelling and dark urine. Anyone showing them needs emergency medical care. Kidney injury can develop even after the drug's immediate effects appear to wear off.

Heat stroke can trigger rhabdomyolysis because extreme body temperature damages muscle cells directly. The muscle breakdown it causes can happen whether or not the person was exercising when the heat took hold.

Heat stroke is generally defined as a core body temperature above 104 degrees Fahrenheit, or 40 degrees Celsius, along with signs that the brain is affected. Those signs include confusion, slurred speech, agitation, seizures and loss of consciousness. It is a medical emergency, distinct from the milder condition known as heat exhaustion.

Heat stroke takes two forms. Classic heat stroke, also called nonexertional heat stroke, develops over hours or days of exposure to hot weather. It tends to strike older adults, young children and people with chronic heart, lung or kidney disease. Exertional heat stroke affects people working hard in the heat, such as laborers, soldiers and athletes. Both forms can lead to muscle breakdown.

At very high temperatures, the proteins and membranes inside muscle cells stop working normally. Cell membranes become leaky, and the cells struggle to produce the energy they need to stay intact. As the fibers die, their contents spill into the bloodstream.

Heat stroke also places other stresses on the body at the same time. Heavy sweating and poor fluid intake can leave a person dehydrated. Dehydration reduces blood flow to the kidneys. That leaves the kidneys less able to cope with the myoglobin arriving from damaged muscle. Severe cases can also injure the liver and disrupt the blood's ability to clot.

Some common medicines make it harder for the body to shed heat. Diuretics can deepen dehydration. Drugs with anticholinergic effects, including some antihistamines and older antidepressants, can reduce sweating. Certain antipsychotic medications can interfere with temperature regulation. People taking these drugs face higher risk during heat waves, especially if they live alone or lack air conditioning.

Older people living in poorly ventilated homes are among the most exposed during heat waves. A person found confused, very hot and unresponsive on a hot day may have both heat stroke and muscle injury.

Anyone who shows signs of heat stroke needs emergency medical care right away. Doctors can then look for muscle breakdown and watch closely for kidney damage.

A convulsive seizure that lasts too long, or a cluster of seizures without recovery in between, can break down skeletal muscle and cause rhabdomyolysis. The danger comes from relentless muscle contraction, not from the abnormal brain activity itself.

Status epilepticus is the medical term for this kind of extended seizure activity. Doctors generally treat a convulsive seizure lasting five minutes or longer as status epilepticus. The same label applies to repeated seizures without a return to normal awareness between them.

During a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, muscles across the body stiffen and then jerk rhythmically. Every contraction burns energy. When the contractions continue for many minutes, muscle cells exhaust their supply of ATP, the molecule that powers them.

Without enough energy, the pumps that control the flow of sodium and calcium across muscle cell membranes stop working properly. Calcium builds up inside the cells. That activates enzymes that damage the cell's internal structure, and the fibers begin to break apart.

Damaged fibers spill their contents into the bloodstream. Myoglobin, creatine kinase and potassium all leak out. The kidneys face the greatest threat, and acute kidney injury is the main concern after a prolonged convulsive seizure. A sudden potassium surge adds a separate risk to the heart.

Prolonged convulsions add other stresses. Body temperature can climb during a long seizure. Breathing may be impaired, which lowers oxygen delivery to tissues. Lactic acid accumulates as muscles keep working without enough oxygen. Each of these can deepen the injury to muscle.

The type of seizure matters. Nonconvulsive seizures involve abnormal brain activity with little or no visible shaking. They place far less strain on muscle. The risk of rhabdomyolysis is concentrated in seizures with sustained, forceful contractions.

Status epilepticus has many possible causes. They include stopping or missing antiseizure medication, stroke, head injury, brain infections such as encephalitis and very low blood sugar. It can also be the first sign of epilepsy in someone with no prior history of seizures.

After a long seizure, a person may notice deep muscle aches, weakness or unusually dark urine. Those symptoms can signal muscle breakdown and need prompt medical assessment, even once the seizure itself has stopped.

Any convulsive seizure lasting five minutes or longer is a medical emergency. Calling emergency services quickly matters both for stopping the seizure and for limiting harm to the muscles and kidneys.

Viral and bacterial infections can break down skeletal muscle, even in people who have not exerted themselves at all. Influenza, COVID-19, HIV and sepsis are among the infections that have been linked to rhabdomyolysis.

Influenza is a well-documented example. Both influenza A and influenza B have been tied to the condition. Ordinary flu aches do not mean rhabdomyolysis. Concern rises when muscle pain becomes severe, weakness sets in, or urine turns dark brown or tea-colored. That dark color can signal myoglobin, a muscle protein, passing into the urine.

Clinicians have described several ways an infection can injure muscle. A virus may invade muscle cells directly. The immune system's response can damage muscle tissue through inflammation. High fever and dehydration can place additional strain on muscle. They also burden the kidneys, which must clear the released proteins.

COVID-19 added a newer entry to the list. During the pandemic, clinicians published case reports of rhabdomyolysis in patients with the disease. Some of those patients were seriously ill in the hospital. Others showed muscle breakdown alongside more familiar respiratory symptoms.

HIV has also been associated with rhabdomyolysis. In people living with HIV, the picture can be complicated. Other infections and the medicines used to manage the illness may also affect muscle. That overlap can make the exact trigger harder to pin down in an individual case.

Sepsis is the body's extreme, dysregulated response to an infection. It can drive blood pressure down and reduce the oxygen reaching muscle tissue. Toxins released by some bacteria can harm muscle cells as well. Legionnaires' disease, caused by Legionella bacteria, is one bacterial infection with a recognized link to rhabdomyolysis.

Kidney damage is a central concern in this setting. Sepsis can already strain the kidneys before any muscle breakdown begins. Myoglobin released from damaged muscle adds a second burden on the same organs.

Infection symptoms such as fever, fatigue and body aches can mask the early signs of muscle injury. Anyone with an infection who develops severe muscle pain, marked weakness or dark urine needs prompt medical care.

Low blood potassium, low blood phosphate, severe hypothyroidism and diabetic emergencies are recognized causes of rhabdomyolysis. Each one disrupts the chemistry muscle cells need to stay intact. Muscle can then break down without any injury, drug or hard workout.

Potassium keeps muscle cell membranes electrically stable. It also helps blood vessels inside working muscle widen to deliver more oxygen. When potassium falls too low, a state called hypokalemia, that blood flow response weakens and muscle fibers become more fragile.

Hypokalemia often develops quietly. Common routes include prolonged vomiting or diarrhea and diuretic medicines that flush potassium out through the urine. Very large amounts of real black licorice can also drain potassium. The culprit is glycyrrhizin, a compound in licorice root that makes the kidneys excrete more potassium.

One detail can hide this cause. Dying muscle cells release their own potassium into the bloodstream. A blood test taken after the damage has started may therefore show normal or even high potassium. A deficit can still be what set off the breakdown.

Phosphate is a building block of ATP, the molecule cells burn for energy. When phosphate drops too low, a state called hypophosphatemia, muscle cannot power itself properly. Its cell membranes can then fail.

Severe phosphate drops often occur during refeeding syndrome. This happens when a person who has been starved or badly malnourished starts eating again too quickly. The sudden demand pulls phosphate into cells and out of the blood.

The thyroid matters too. Severe hypothyroidism slows muscle metabolism and can cause a muscle disease called hypothyroid myopathy. In rare cases it progresses to rhabdomyolysis. Untreated hypothyroidism is also a recognized risk factor for muscle injury in people taking statins.

Diabetes emergencies round out this group. Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state both involve very high blood sugar, heavy fluid loss and large shifts in electrolytes. That combination can injure muscle cells.

Treatment of these emergencies can also lower phosphate and potassium further. Insulin moves both minerals out of the blood and into cells, which adds strain on muscle. Dehydration compounds the danger. Kidneys short on fluid are less able to clear myoglobin, the muscle protein that can injure them.

Muscle pain, weakness or dark urine in someone with one of these conditions calls for prompt medical care. Blood tests can identify the imbalance and the muscle damage together.

Some people inherit genetic conditions that leave their muscles short of fuel. For them, rhabdomyolysis can follow modest exertion, fasting or illness. McArdle disease, carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency and mitochondrial disorders are among these inherited causes.

McArdle disease is also known as glycogen storage disease type V. It is named for Brian McArdle, a British physician who described it in 1951. People with the condition lack a working form of myophosphorylase. Muscles use this enzyme to break down stored glycogen for energy.

Without that enzyme, muscles struggle during the first minutes of activity and during short bursts of intense effort. Typical symptoms include cramps, pain, stiffness and early fatigue. Many patients describe a "second wind." This is the point where symptoms ease as the body shifts toward fats and blood sugar for fuel. Pushing hard through the early stage of exercise can lead to muscle breakdown and darkened urine.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase II deficiency, often shortened to CPT II deficiency, affects a different energy pathway. The CPT II enzyme helps move long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria, where they are burned for energy. When the enzyme falls short, muscles cannot draw on fat stores well.

That gap matters most when the body depends heavily on fat. Prolonged exercise, fasting, cold exposure and fever are recognized triggers. People with CPT II deficiency often exercise normally between attacks. Their episodes tend to follow long rather than brief effort. That pattern sets the condition apart from McArdle disease.

Mitochondrial disorders form a broader group. They disrupt the cell structures that produce most of a muscle's energy. Some forms cause exercise intolerance and recurrent rhabdomyolysis, at times alongside problems in other organs.

Sickle cell trait is an inherited blood condition rather than a muscle disorder. It is associated with rhabdomyolysis during strenuous exertion. The trait means carrying one copy of the sickle cell gene. It is distinct from sickle cell disease, and most people with the trait have no symptoms in daily life.

Repeated episodes, attacks after ordinary activity or a family history can point doctors toward an inherited cause. Genetic and enzyme testing can confirm several of these conditions. Any episode of severe muscle pain with dark urine needs prompt medical care.

Some venoms and foods carry toxins that attack muscle directly, and they are documented causes of rhabdomyolysis. Bites from certain snakes, mass stings from a bee or wasp swarm, poisoning from wild quail and a fish-linked illness called Haff disease all belong in this group.

Snake venom is the best-known example. Many venoms contain myotoxins, which are proteins that damage the membranes of skeletal muscle cells. Sea snakes are known for venom of this kind. Some Australian land snakes, including tiger snakes, can produce the same effect. A person bitten by one of these species may develop aching, weak muscles within hours of the bite.

Insect stings cause harm through volume rather than a single potent dose. One bee sting does not break down muscle in a healthy person. A swarm attack that delivers a very large number of stings is a different matter. Honeybee venom contains melittin, a peptide that ruptures cell membranes. It also contains enzymes that add to the damage. Wasps and hornets can cause similar injury when they sting in large numbers.

Coturnism takes its name from Coturnix, the genus that includes the common quail. It occurs after people eat wild quail, and it has been described for centuries around the Mediterranean. Cases tend to cluster during the birds' migration seasons. The exact toxin has not been confirmed. One long-standing explanation holds that migrating quail eat seeds that are harmless to the birds but toxic to people.

Haff disease is rhabdomyolysis that follows a meal of certain fish or shellfish. It is named after the Königsberg Haff, a lagoon on the Baltic coast where outbreaks were recorded in the 1920s. Later cases have been tied to buffalo fish in the U.S. and to crayfish in China. Investigators have not identified the toxin responsible. It appears to survive cooking. A thoroughly prepared meal therefore does not rule it out.

Anyone who develops muscle pain or dark urine after a venomous bite, a swarm attack or a meal of wild quail or fish needs emergency care. Toxin-related cases carry the same kidney risk as rhabdomyolysis from any other cause.
