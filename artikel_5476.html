Recording Academy explained: who votes on the Grammys and what the group does

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

What is the Recording Academy? This guide explains the U.S. nonprofit behind the Grammy Awards, from its 1957 founding and member voting to MusiCares and the Latin Grammys

Plenty of television viewers know the Grammy trophy on sight. Far fewer know the organization that hands it out. That organization is the Recording Academy, and it works mostly out of view for the rest of the year. Knowing how it operates explains a lot about the Grammys — who picks the winners, why the rules change and what the Academy does between ceremonies.

So what is the Recording Academy? Its formal name is the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences. It is a U.S. nonprofit membership organization made up of people who make records for a living. Its members include performers, songwriters, producers and engineers. The Grammy Awards are its best-known program, but they are only one part of what the Academy does.

The distinction matters most at voting time. Grammy winners are not decided by chart position, sales or streaming numbers. They are chosen by the Academy's voting members. That makes the Grammys an award given by peers. When an artist criticizes the Grammys, or when the Academy rewrites its nomination rules, the questions center on how a professional membership body runs itself.

The Academy's work also extends well beyond a single awards night. It runs charitable and educational programs connected to music. A museum bearing the Grammy name operates alongside it. It lobbies federal lawmakers in Washington, D.C., on issues that affect music creators. A separate sister organization runs a parallel awards show for Latin music.

Its history adds context. The Academy dates to the late 1950s. Its top leadership changed hands more than once in 2019 and 2020. Its awards process has also faced a public boycott by a major artist.

The Recording Academy is a U.S. nonprofit organization of music professionals, and it is the group that runs the Grammy Awards. Its formal name is the National Academy of Recording Arts and Sciences, though it generally goes by the shorter name.

People often use the two names interchangeably, but they refer to different things. The Recording Academy is the organization. The Grammy Awards are a program it operates, and they are its best-known one. Keeping the two apart makes it easier to follow how decisions about the awards get made.

The Academy is a membership body made up of people who work in recorded music. Its members include performers, songwriters, producers and engineers. Performers are the singers and instrumentalists heard on a recording. Songwriters create the music and lyrics. Producers guide how a recording comes together, from arrangement choices to its final sound. Engineers handle the technical work of capturing and mixing audio.

That range shapes what the organization represents. It speaks for the people who make recordings, not only the artists whose names appear on them. Many of its members work behind the scenes and are rarely seen by the public.

Its nonprofit status separates it from record labels, streaming services and other music companies. A nonprofit has no shareholders and does not pay out profits to owners. The Academy functions as a professional association, not as a business that sells music.

The distinction has practical consequences for anyone reading music news. When a report says the Recording Academy changed a rule or added a category, it describes a decision by the organization. The televised Grammy ceremony is the public face of that work, but the Academy operates year-round.

The longer formal name still appears in official contexts. Either name refers to the same body: the U.S. peer organization of music professionals that stands behind the Grammy trophy.

The Recording Academy was founded in 1957 by figures in the Los Angeles music business. It held its first Grammy Awards ceremony on May 4, 1959, honoring recordings from 1958.

The Academy existed for about two years before it handed out a single trophy.

The first ceremony took place in two cities. The Academy held events on the same date in Beverly Hills, in the Los Angeles area, and in New York.

The awards that night looked back a full year. Every recording honored at the 1959 ceremony came from 1958.

One song won two of the night's major prizes. Domenico Modugno's "Nel Blu Dipinto Di Blu (Volare)" won Record of the Year. The same song also won Song of the Year.

Album of the Year went to a different winner. Henry Mancini took the award for The Music from Peter Gunn.

Modugno and Mancini are the first names in the Grammy record books. Every ceremony since 1959 has added to the list of winners that began with them.

The Recording Academy's members are working music professionals, and they form the pool from which Grammy voters are drawn. Those members are organized through regional chapters in U.S. music hubs, including Los Angeles, Nashville and New York.

Membership is built around the people who make recordings. Performers belong alongside songwriters. Producers belong alongside the engineers who capture and shape sound in the studio. Several people credited on the same hit record can all be members of the same organization.

That structure makes the Academy a peer group. It is not a fan club, and it is not a trade body for record labels. The people who judge a piece of work come from the same trades as the people who made it. A songwriter's work is weighed by other songwriters and by the performers, producers and engineers who work with songwriters.

Voting membership is tied to professional work in recorded music. Buying concert tickets or following an artist does not make someone eligible. The Academy sets its own rules for who qualifies. The line it draws separates people who work on records from people who listen to them.

Regional chapters give members a local base. The U.S. recording business is spread across several cities rather than concentrated in one. A chapter connects members with peers who work in the same city.

The chapter system means a session engineer in Nashville and a producer in Los Angeles can belong to the same national organization. Each still has a chapter closer to home. Grouping members by city organizes the membership locally. It does not divide the Academy into separate regional organizations.

The answer to who votes on the Grammys follows from this setup. The electorate is the Academy's professional membership, grouped by region. It is not the general public, and it is not a panel of critics.

Grammy winners are picked by the Recording Academy's voting members, not by sales charts or streaming totals. The vote happens in two stages: a nominations round that sets the field of contenders, then a final voting round that decides the winners.

That design ties each result to the judgment of peers in the music business. A record can spend months at the top of the charts and still miss a nomination. A release with modest sales can still take home a trophy.

The nominations round comes first. Voting members cast ballots to narrow the eligible releases down to a set of nominees in each category. Those names are then announced as the year's Grammy nominees.

The final voting round follows. Members vote again, this time choosing only among the announced nominees. The result of that second ballot decides who receives the trophy at the ceremony.

The two rounds are separate decisions with separate outcomes. The final ballot lists only the nominees. A release that misses the shortlist in the first round has no path to a win in the second. Each nominee also starts the final round on equal footing, whatever its sales or streaming record.

The process changed in 2021. That year, the Academy ended its anonymous nominations review committees. The change applied to the nominations stage, the first of the two rounds.

For listeners reading the results, the two-round structure offers a practical guide. A nomination shows that the work drew enough support from voting members to reach the shortlist. A win shows that it then came out ahead of its fellow nominees in a separate final vote.

The Grammy takes its name from the gramophone, and the trophy is a gilded miniature of one. The award's name and its physical form both point to the same object: a machine built to play recorded sound.

"Grammy" is a shortened form of the word gramophone. The nickname ties the award to recorded music. Recorded work is the field the Recording Academy covers, and it is what the awards honor.

The statuette carries the same reference in physical form. A winner does not receive an abstract figure or a plaque. The prize is a scaled-down gramophone.

The pairing gives the award one consistent identity. The word read out during the broadcast and the object handed to a winner describe the same thing. A viewer who sees the trophy on stage is looking at a model of the device the name refers to.

The General Field holds the four top Grammy Awards: Album of the Year, Record of the Year, Song of the Year and Best New Artist. Two of them, Record of the Year and Song of the Year, are easy to confuse but reward different people.

The Recording Academy sorts its Grammy categories into groups called fields. Each field gathers related categories under one heading. The General Field is the group that contains the top awards.

Song of the Year honors the songwriters of a track. The award is about the composition — the words and music as they were written. A composition stays the same no matter who performs it. Its writers are the people this award recognizes.

Record of the Year honors the recording itself. Its credit covers the performers, producers and engineers who made that specific version of the track. The award recognizes how a piece of music was performed and captured. That credit places producers and engineers, who usually work behind the scenes, among the people honored in a top category.

The split exists because a song and a recording are not the same thing. A songwriter who never sings on a track can still be honored for Song of the Year. A performer who did not write the track can still be credited for Record of the Year. One track can compete in both categories. Each award then goes to a different group of people.

Album of the Year covers a full album rather than a single track. Best New Artist honors an artist rather than a specific piece of work. That makes it the only one of the four built around a person instead of a recording or composition.

Read together, the four General Field awards look at music on different scales: one written song, one recorded performance, one complete album and one artist's emergence.

The Latin Recording Academy is a sister organization to the Recording Academy, with its own base in Miami and its own members. It runs the Latin Grammy Awards, which were first held in 2000.

The two groups share the Grammy name, but they are separate bodies. The Latin Recording Academy keeps a membership of its own, apart from the Recording Academy's.

That split determines who picks the winners. Latin Grammy voters come from the Latin Recording Academy's membership. They are not drawn from the members who vote on the main Grammy Awards.

The Latin Grammys are a distinct awards program. They are not a category or a field inside the Grammys, and each show holds its own ceremony.

A Latin Grammy and a Grammy come from two different member organizations. The Recording Academy runs the Grammys. The Latin Recording Academy runs the Latin Grammys from its Miami base.

The Grammy Museum opened in 2008 at L.A. Live in downtown Los Angeles. It operates alongside the Recording Academy, the nonprofit membership organization that runs the Grammy Awards.

The museum and the awards are separate programs. The museum does not nominate recordings or choose winners. Those decisions belong to the Academy's voting members.

The museum also runs on a different schedule from the awards. The Grammy ceremony happens once a year. A museum stays open between ceremonies. That gives the Grammy name a permanent public location.

Los Angeles was the first site. A second location opened eight years later. Grammy Museum Mississippi opened in 2016 in Cleveland, Mississippi.

The Mississippi site carries the state's name in its title. That name sets it apart from the original Los Angeles museum.

With both sites open, the Grammy Museum has locations in two U.S. states, California and Mississippi.

MusiCares is a nonprofit that provides health and human services to people who work in the music industry. The Recording Academy founded it in 1989.

The Grammy Foundation is a separate nonprofit affiliated with the Academy. Its work centers on music education and music preservation.

MusiCares serves the people who make music, not the recordings they release. Its remit is health and human services. That places it outside the awards process. It does not nominate, judge or vote on anything.

The charity was created 32 years after the Academy's 1957 founding. By 1989, the Academy had been presenting Grammys for three decades, dating back to the first ceremony in 1959.

The Grammy Foundation works on two fronts. Music education is about people learning music. Preservation is about keeping music that already exists from being lost.

The two charities split the Academy's nonprofit work by purpose. MusiCares deals with the needs of individuals in the music business. The Grammy Foundation deals with teaching music and safeguarding what has already been made.

Neither organization picks Grammy winners or produces the Grammy telecast. Both operate as affiliated nonprofits alongside the Academy rather than as parts of its awards program.

The Recording Academy lobbies in Washington as well as running the Grammys. It supported the Music Modernization Act, which became law in 2018. The Academy's leadership has also turned over since 2019, and in 2021 it ended its anonymous nominations review committees.

Advocacy is a separate job from the awards. The Academy's work in Washington is meant to serve the people who make recordings, whether or not they ever receive a nomination. When it presses federal lawmakers, it acts for the profession as a whole, not as the producer of a televised awards show. Its support for the Music Modernization Act is one example of that work.

The Academy's top job has changed hands twice since 2019. Neil Portnow served as president and CEO from 2002 to 2019, a run of about 17 years. Deborah Dugan succeeded him as CEO. In January 2020, after about five months in the role, she was placed on leave.

Harvey Mason Jr. has led the Academy since 2020. Dugan's tenure, measured in months, was the shortest of the three. Portnow's, measured in years, was the longest. The two tenures sit at opposite ends of the Academy's recent history at the top.

The 2021 end of the anonymous review committees came during Mason's time in charge. It was a decision about how the membership body runs its own selection process.

In 2021, The Weeknd publicly boycotted the Grammys. His boycott was widely reported and forms part of the public backdrop to that year's committee decision. A boycott of that kind is aimed at the institution that runs the awards, not at a single category.

Lobbying, leadership and the rules for choosing nominees all concern the Academy as an institution. None of them turns on a specific award or winner. Together they show the organization at work outside the annual ceremony it is best known for.
