This year, SummerStage marked a monumental accomplishment — 40 years providing free concerts for New Yorkers — with a similarly monumental season.

“We were lucky enough to get some really amazing funders who gifted us with additional resources to celebrate the 40th,” executive artistic director Erika Elliott says. “It gave us a chance to look back and also look forward.”

That included shows with “important cultural icons — and distinctly New York people” including La India, Angélique Kidjo and Laurie Anderson, “bookings that felt really intentional.” This season’s programming spoke to the diversity of the five boroughs. “No other festival is doing a Taiwanese day, or a dedicated C-pop [Chinese pop] day, or a day fully focused on ­Morocco,” she says.

Another major moment came when the next-gen jam band Goose did a surprise pop-up show in June in Central Park the night after two Madison Square Garden gigs. Says Elliott: “For me, that was a big coup this year — to have the relationship and the reputation that a band at that level says, ‘Hey, could we do a show with you?’ ”

Elliott, a 22-year SummerStage veteran who has served as its executive artistic director since 2015, admits that because the organization doesn’t depend on ticket sales, she’s in an “enviable position” — but she points out the “competitive landscape” for nonprofits. “Our budget has always increased a little bit,” she says, “but I don’t know that it’s always able to keep up with the increase in artists’ fees.”

Additionally, climate change continues to throw curveballs at the outdoor festival. “We were not able to present Mavis Staples because of the smoke and the air quality. That’s a trend that never used to happen,” Elliott says of the R&B legend’s scuttled July show. “We are working on updating our process every year and always learning more. We look for guidance from the city when possible.”

Changes in the political climate — such as partial travel bans on countries like Nigeria — have also created challenges. “It changes our ability to book artists who deserve to play,” she says. “It’s really unfortunate … to not let an artist come and perform in this country, especially artists who have performed in this country before. I just don’t see how that builds bridges or does anything positive.”

Still, Elliott — a self-described “optimist” — remains hopeful for SummerStage’s future. “I’ve been here so long because it’s a dream job, to be able to bring music and artists and communities together, for free, across all five boroughs,” she says. “We have a legacy to build on for our 80th ­[anniversary].” —JOE LYNCH

Turning the extraordinary demand for live music in Mexico into unforgettable fan experiences has been OCESA’s greatest recent achievement, according to Guillermo Parra, international events director for the promoter, which finished at No. 3 on Billboard Boxscore’s 2026 midyear promoters chart with $559.5 million grossed, behind only Live Nation and AEG.

“Shakira’s 13 sold-out shows and Bad Bunny’s eight-date run at Estadio GNP Seguros are powerful examples, alongside major engagements by BTS, The ­Weeknd and AC/DC,” Parra says. “What makes this meaningful is the ability to deliver consistently at that scale. It reflects years of building trust with artists, developing specialized teams and investing in venues.”

On her Las Mujeres Ya No Lloran world tour, Shakira made history at the Mexico City stadium (formerly known as Foro Sol), becoming the artist with the most ­performances in a single tour at the iconic venue, where she sold 721,000 tickets across 13 shows, according to Billboard Boxscore. And only Madrid hosted more dates for Bad Bunny’s colossal DeBÍ TiRAR MáS FOToS world tour; the global star’s eight sold-out shows at the stadium in December 2025 brought in $88 million from 518,000 tickets sold.

“These results reinforce Mexico’s importance to global touring, while demonstrating that Latin artists can drive some of the world’s most significant live-music moments,” Parra adds.

His favorite musical moment of the past year-and-a-half: Oasis’ return to Mexico. “There was an extraordinary sense of anticipation around those two nights at Estadio GNP Seguros, and hearing the crowd sing those songs together was unforgettable,” he recalls. “For longtime fans, it was a reunion with music that had shaped their lives; for younger fans, it was their first opportunity to experience the band live.”

When asked about the biggest challenges facing the Latin music community, Parra says global touring infrastructure for live music must keep pace with the worldwide growth of Latin artists. “The audience is there,” he notes, “but reaching more fans requires suitable venues, experienced local teams and touring routes that make economic sense, particularly beyond the largest cities.”

For Parra, it is imperative to create more opportunities for emerging artists to develop sustainable live careers, and he believes that festivals, opening slots and venues of different sizes all play a role: “The opportunity is to translate Latin music’s global visibility into a stronger touring ecosystem, where more artists can grow and more communities can participate.” —SIGAL RATNER-ARIAS

With a roster that includes top-tier pop talent such as Ariana Grande, Harry Styles, Lady Gaga, The Weeknd, Dua Lipa and Charli xcx, CAA remains a juggernaut at the stadium and arena levels.

But that dominance comes with its own challenges: These days, CAA is in some cases booking four years out, says co-head of global touring Darryl Eaton, with agents at times looking as far ahead as 2030.

“We’re looking at everything holistically to figure out the entire global footprint in one fell swoop,” Eaton says.

And while this has been an exceptional year for CAA’s established clients — from Styles’ 30-date Madison Square Garden residency to The Weeknd’s After Hours Til Dawn outing becoming the third tour ever to surpass $1 billion grossed — the agency is also shepherding newer headliners like Sabrina Carpenter, Tate McRae, Doja Cat and Gracie Abrams to some of the world’s biggest stages.

Among CAA’s new stadium talents is client Rüfüs Du Sol (represented by the agency everywhere other than Europe). When the Australian dance trio put up the on-sale for its Inhale / Exhale tour in October 2024, the team worked to have tickets for the entire run — no matter where a date was booked — ready to go at once.

“We announced Australia, South America, North America and Europe all at the same time, and we put it on sale all at the same time,” Eaton says, “which was really hard across time zones. We were in war rooms, putting shows up in all these different territories over the course of 48 hours.”

The strategy worked: The tour, which ran from March 2025 through September 2026, became not only the first dance trek to gross more than $100 million, but also the first to surpass $150 million, ultimately becoming the genre’s highest-grossing and best-selling tour ever, with $158 million earned from 1.7 million tickets sold, according to Billboard Boxscore.

The tour is emblematic of the way that territories beyond North America — especially in Latin America, Asia and the Middle East — have become priorities for bookers. “The world’s just so much smaller and music is global,” Eaton says. “Our perspective in touring has gotten global as well.” —ARIEL KING

When TBA Agency took shape in 2020 amid the pandemic shutdown, there was no live music to speak of. But steered by veteran agents Avery McTaggart (managing partner), Marshall Betts (managing partner) and Amy Davidman (founding partner) — all furloughed from Paradigm that spring shortly after concerts were paused — the company hit the ground running when stages reopened. It hasn’t looked back since.

“The live show is paramount,” says director of A&R and agent Josh Mulder, who represents Remi Wolf, Faye Webster and Underscores. “It has to hit. It has to really be able to convert people. In such a crowded time and industry landscape, live is king — or queen.”

Fortunately, TBA has a plethora of artists renowned for their onstage prowess: Ethel Cain, whose just-wrapped headlining tour included two legs each in North America and Europe and a plum Coachella slot; Jungle, which the agency says sold 16,000 tickets across two nights at The Greek Theatre in Berkeley, Calif.; Big Thief, which played a two-hour-plus sold-out show at Red Rocks Amphitheatre in Morrison, Colo., in October; Webster, who pulled off an international tour with a 40-piece orchestra in 2025; and Wolf, who recently announced a 2027 headlining run including a sold-out date at New York’s Radio City Music Hall.

“I’m so curious what she’s going to do next,” says Mulder of Wolf, who improbably covered Tom Cochrane’s “Life Is a Highway” on her last tour. “I’ve been doing this for about 16 years, and every single show continues to blow me away.”

One rising TBA star is Underscores, the hyperpop artist who has broken out with her 2026 album, U, and landed the support slot on Charli xcx’s arena tour this fall. But don’t call it an overnight success — like many of TBA’s clients (and staff), Underscores has put in the time. “When I started working with Underscores six years ago, that was a very different sound from what was happening. I’m so thrilled she’s finally getting the shine she deserves,” Mulder says.

Of the challenges facing the touring industry, Mulder points not only to rising costs but staffing shortages: “With the amount of touring going out on the road, finding great-quality people is always a hurdle. We try to strike a balance of hitting as many markets as we can that are significant and important to the trajectory of our clients, without keeping them out too long.”

Even so, he sums up today’s touring industry as “exciting,” particularly for niche genres “that are finally getting a stage to grow and be seen” across the country. “People are responding. That gives everyone a massive amount of hope that fans are still interested in pioneering sounds that push boundaries, and that creates more space for other artists to shine.” -J.L.

Over the last year, marquee UTA clients such as Bad Bunny and Guns N’ Roses have staged major global tours as the agency continues to grow its international business.

“There are more places to play around the world, more markets are opening, cities and private corporations are opening more theaters around the world,” says David Zedeck, UTA partner and co-head of global music. “Music has never been more accessible.”

As the expansion continues, Zedeck says local promoters — who know how to best navigate their markets and assess regional demand — are essential.

“It’s making sure your ticket price is right, it’s making sure you’re on the right day of the week at the right venue,” he explains. “We forget that, while it’s a global business, tickets are sold locally.”

And as global genres including Latin and K-pop become some of the industry’s top earners, UTA — where Zedeck says many agents are “genre-agnostic” — is well-positioned. Bad Bunny’s DeBÍ TiRAR MáS FoTOS stadium tour grossed $472.2 million from 3.1 million tickets sold, according to Billboard Boxscore; he leads a Latin roster that also boasts ­Karol G, Rosalía, Rauw Alejandro, Romeo Santos and Rawayana.

UTA’s hip-hop and R&B business also had a big year, with Cardi B’s hotly anticipated Little Miss Drama Tour — the New York rapper’s first proper tour — grossing more than $70 million from 453,000 tickets sold, and Don Toliver’s 50-date North American arena tour grossing over $60 million from 670,000 tickets (the ascendant rapper embarked on a European leg in late October). The R&B Tour, Usher’s current joint outing with UTA client Chris Brown, has sold 1.5 million tickets and grossed $284.7 million across 36 shows.

For Zedeck, the most significant trend from the last year may be the growing intersection of sports and music — and not just because Bad Bunny headlined the 2026 Super Bowl halftime show. The FIFA World Cup and NHL have also started bringing in major talent — and Zedeck’s own client, the Jonas Brothers, headlined an NFL halftime show in September for the league’s first-ever regular season game in Australia, which took place at the Melbourne Cricket Ground.

Says Zedeck: “Those are big, seminal moments for artists.” —A.K.

Over the last several years, residency-style touring has become far more common — and as fuel prices rise and the economics become more difficult to manage, venues expect the trend to continue. Barclays Center, which hosted Ariana Grande for five nights in July, is no exception. The Brooklyn arena will host an even longer stay for a pop superstar next year, when Olivia Rodrigo will play 10 shows at the venue Feb. 11-28.

“We’ve been able to extend our hospitality and what we call ‘the Brooklyn way’ and stand out in a crowded marketplace not just for the artist, but for the ticket holders,” says Laurie Jacoby, chief entertainment officer and executive vp at Brooklyn Sports & Entertainment, the company that owns Barclays Center.

With Harry Styles camping out in Manhattan at nearby Madison Square Garden for 30 dates from late August through Halloween, Barclays shined with more tours coming through, hosting artists including Charli xcx, Phoebe Bridgers, Benson Boone, Shakira and Megan Moroney. Jacoby describes the summer and fall of 2026 as the venue’s best seasons yet.

“New York is amazing because it can support so much, but [Harry Styles] has been the best gift ever,” Jacoby says. “We were busy anyway, but it’s given us the opportunity to welcome sometimes people’s first big shows. It’s giving people an opportunity to be in Brooklyn and have a great experience.”

Barclays also tried to personalize shows for fans walking through its doors, including a campaign to create customized tarot cards for Bridgers’ three late-September dates at the venue. Grande’s five shows, meanwhile, grossed $13.5 million total from 75,400 tickets sold — the venue’s biggest Billboard Boxscore report of the year.

Those recent successes have extended a hot streak for the arena, which ranked at No. 6 on Billboard’s top venues (15,001-plus capacity, excluding stadiums) chart in 2025 with $125 million grossed and nearly 1 million tickets sold.

As the venue looks ahead to its upcoming bookings, it’ll need to continue to balance its tenant basketball teams (the NBA’s Brooklyn Nets and WNBA’s New York Liberty) with its concerts, work that Jacoby says comes together like a puzzle. With artists wanting to lean more into residencies, Jacoby notes that long lead times and communication are essential.

“People come together and try to make things work with their schedules and our venue,” she says. “Both leagues have been accommodating.” —A.K.

On her first day of work at New York’s Radio City Music Hall in 1998, Josephine Vaccarello — just a month out of college, starting a job as an administrative assistant — left the building in the evening and had to use the after-hours exit. That required walking through the opulent theater itself, and she wasn’t about to waste the moment.

“I was like, ‘I’m standing on the stage of Radio City Music Hall. I’m just going to do a thing!’ ” Vaccarello recalls today. So the self-professed “theater kid” from Long Island launched into “On My Own,” Éponine’s iconic lament from Les Misérables. “I had to go for it,” she says with a proud grin.

Nearly 30 years later, Vaccarello is sitting high above Midtown Manhattan on the roof of 2 Penn next to Madison Square Garden, which bought Radio City six months after she started that job. She’s in a very different position: In September, after three years as executive vp, live of Madison Square Garden Entertainment (MSGE) — where she’s spent her entire career — she was named its president.

“I’m going to be honest: I never wanted to leave,” Vaccarello, 50, says of her time with the company. “I love the company I work for. I love the buildings I get to work in. It still makes me get excited when I stand inside any of the venues and watch an entire room of people singing every word to every song, and I’m like, ‘I get to be part of that.’ It’s cool.”

During a mid-October Los Angeles Rams football game, Christy Castillo Butcher walked through SoFi Stadium, taking in the year so far. “It just blew me away that less than a week ago we had been hosting five nights of Bruno Mars,” she says.

Since SoFi and the Hollywood Park campus opened five years ago, the venue executive has fine-tuned that balance of high-profile sporting events — which this year included eight FIFA World Cup games, next year kicks off with Super Bowl LXI and in 2028 will feature Summer Olympics and Paralympics competitions — with sold-out superstar concerts.

“We’re a stadium, but we turn and burn almost like an arena,” she says of SoFi, where as senior vp of booking and programming she and her team manage the “robust calendar” of the 300-plus-acre mixed-use development, which also includes the 6,000-capacity YouTube Theater, shops and restaurants. The sprawling space, she says, has helped shape the programming by making it “a destination spot,” particularly for mini-residencies, like multiple nights with Mars, Beyoncé or Taylor Swift on their most recent tours. SoFi ranked at No. 4 on Billboard’s 2025 year-end touring chart with $236.4 million grossed and 1.3 million tickets sold, and this year, it has grossed $165.9 million across 15 reported shows, according to Billboard Boxscore; BTS’ four-night run alone brought in $57.7 million.

Castillo Butcher, who during nearly two decades at AEG Presents booked major L.A. venues Crypto.com Arena and Peacock Theater, touts her relationship-building — a skill she put to good use this year in particular. “Leading up to FIFA World Cup, the challenge on the programming side is getting ahead of it. Understanding what our blackout dates are going to be,” she says. “It really was an effort with everybody here, talking to our partners and reminding them so they understand what that timeframe is. People listened, and they were willing to route around our availability.”

Another high point: Mexican grupero band Los Bukis concluded its reunion tour this February with two sold-out nights at SoFi — after reuniting for the first time in 25 years at the stadium in August 2021, shortly after it opened. “That was a really special moment for all of us, especially for those of us that were here when we opened,” she says.

She’s looking forward to the Super Bowl, which SoFi last hosted in 2022 and in 2027 will include a halftime show from Jay-Z. “It’s going to be reimagined because [last Super Bowl] we were limited coming out of COVID; we were still in some of those restrictions,” she recalls. “In just five years, we get to have that opportunity again. I’m really excited to see our team collaborate with the NFL and reimagine the space — it will be a brand-new Super Bowl.” —LYNDSEY HAVENS

Michele Bernstein has long marketed some of the world’s biggest touring artists — work that over the last year and change has ranged from veterans including Dead & Company, Rush and Tool to younger acts such as Kendrick Lamar, Florence and the Machine, Machine Gun Kelly, Greta Van Fleet and Ivan Cornejo.

“We get to sell something really unique — it’s that in-real-life experience,” she says. “And that’s connective.”

But while the Dead’s community is the stuff of legends, Bernstein says she sees camaraderie across all of her clients’ fans. “Florence and the Machine is not dissimilar to the Dead; it’s an emotional experience,” she explains. “I work with podcasters too. I did Mel Robbins’ Let Them Tour [named after the author and podcaster’s popular “Let Them Theory”] … women are showing up with tattoos on their arms and they’re all wearing matching ‘Let Them’ shirts. It was like being at a Dead show!

“Music is subjective, but it’s not just music,” she continues. “It’s podcasters, it’s nontraditional touring, it’s comedy. It’s all these people being in the room with the people they really like — whatever their talent is.”

Still, Bernstein’s bread and butter is music touring, and for the marketing executive, who launched her own consultancy in 2020 following nearly two decades at WME, business is booming. Today, creative and intentional ideas are more important than ever to cut through the noise of social media, whether it’s partnering with a local doughnut shop to make custom doughnuts (“which we do a bunch”) or something more unexpected. Florence and the Machine’s most recent tour supported her album Everybody Scream, so “in Chicago, we found a scream club that screams on Sundays at Lake Michigan, and we did a promotion with them,” Bernstein says — just one of the tour’s market-specific, scream-­related events.

But it’s not just local businesses and organizations that are interested in collaboration — as major artists “have gotten more creative … companies are more open” to working together, Bernstein says. “Partners are more flexible to do things the artist’s way than to do it their way,” she notes. “Brands figured out that the magic is in the details, and that an artist on their socials can help drive their agenda far better than just an ad-buy. These brands also know that if they get an artist that’s on the rise, they’re going to have a huge win.” —ERIC RENNER BROWN

Since 2010, HansTolo Enterprises has been a live-music mainstay, helping to power tours for prominent hip-hop and R&B superstars including Brandy & Monica, Mary J. Blige, 21 Savage and A$AP Rocky. This year, co-founders Tone Lopez and Raphiki Rhodes tackled two colossal undertakings: Cardi B’s first-ever headlining tour and the historic joint stadium run by R&B kings Chris Brown and Usher.

“This is the culmination of where we were trying to be two to three years ago,” Rhodes says. “We had this Cardi B tour land in our lap, and for it to be as successful as it was, it was more than a concert — it was borderline theatrical.”

After delivering her first album in seven years with 2025’s Am I the Drama?, Cardi took the Billboard 200 No. 1 to 35 North American cities from February to April on the Little Miss Drama Tour. A mix of Cardi’s signature hits and high-wattage arena visuals, the must-see spectacle grossed over $70 million and sold 453,000 tickets, according to Billboard Boxscore.

“We thought it was just going to be her rapping, but it ended up being a well-­produced show,” Lopez says. “There were a lot of production hiccups that [Rhodes] had to put his foot in and solve, but he handled them really well. He grew as a production manager from that tour alone.”

Rhodes and Lopez went straight from Cardi to another seismic challenge. Kicking off in late June, Brown and Usher’s three-hour R&B master class brought two of the genre’s leading men together in stadiums from coast to coast — and despite a tight rehearsal window, HansTolo orchestrated the historic outing. According to Billboard Boxscore, the tour grossed $91.8 million from 465,000 tickets sold in August, marking the second consecutive month it surpassed $90 million. (Brown and Usher will remain on the road through mid-December.)

“Chris and Usher are iconic,” Rhodes says. “I don’t think there’s anything we can do that would be at this level of iconic material, based on our upbringing and what we grew up listening to. It doesn’t get any better than that.” —CARL LAMARRE

Independent live music has an affordability crisis: The cost of touring has gone up for artists and fans are being priced out of events thanks to rising ticket prices and predatory secondary market practices — and, even when venues get them in the door, those same fans are spending less on concessions.

“If we’re sending people out on 30-day tours and they’re saying they’re not ­making money and all the venues are saying they’re not making money, then no one’s doing well,” says Nathaniel Marro, executive director of the National Independent Talent Organization, which represents a growing number of indie agencies and managers.

Over the past year, NITO has helped its members — as well as venue and promoter partners — rethink how they do business. The proliferation of performing rights organizations (which collect royalties for songs performed at concerts) has driven the cost of touring up without songwriters seeing meaningful returns when royalties are paid out. NITO, along with royalty tracking platform Tour Tech, has begun researching the system in an attempt to modernize outdated practices and more accurately pay songwriters.

NITO has also been battling to keep concerts affordable for fans by working on fair ticketing legislation in New York, California, Vermont, Maine and Washington, D.C. In July, NITO collected signatures from more than 40 New England-based artists including Noah Kahan, Aimee Mann, Dropkick Murphys, MGMT and Grace Potter to support Massachusetts’ The Great Divide Act, which would cap resale prices, ban speculative ticketing practices (when resellers list tickets on the secondary market before possessing them), make fraudulent ticketing sites illegal and more. These legislative measures could help lower soaring ticket prices.

Marro and his peers in the live sector feel the lack of regulation around concert ticketing in the United States could drive the next generation of concertgoers away. “How many times do you need to be slapped across the face before saying, ‘OK, this doesn’t work for me anymore’? If you keep striking out on the concerts you want to see, you’re going to stop wanting to go to concerts,” he says. “We need to figure out ways that [concerts] aren’t once-every-six-months or once-a-year expenditures.” —TAYLOR MIMS
