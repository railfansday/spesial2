SPCX stock since its IPO: a $225.64 peak, a $104.83 low and a Dec. 8 unlock

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

SpaceX's SPCX stock closed day one at $160.95, hit $225.64, fell to $104.83 and faces a Dec. 8 lockup expiry. Here are 3 key numbers.

Few investors could buy into SpaceX when it began trading, because the company held back almost all of its shares from the market. That left a small pool of stock, and a modest number of buyers or sellers could move its price sharply. Investors have pushed SPCX through wide swings in its short public life for that reason. Those swings reveal as much about how many shares could trade as about what the rocket business is worth.

Different forces have set the price at different times. Buyers drove it up on the first day. A stock with so few shares in circulation can also move on bets against it and on changes in how many shares are free to trade. Those forces come from the mechanics of trading rather than from anyone's judgment of the company, so no price at any one moment settles what SpaceX is worth. A shareholder, or someone thinking of buying, has to decide which number to treat as the reference point: the offer price, the debut, the peak, the low or today's price. Each choice gives a different answer to whether SPCX has rewarded its investors.

Investing.com tracks SPCX's trading history, price extremes and share supply since its June 2026 listing. That record covers the stock's whole public life rather than a verdict from any single day, starting from a float of under 5% of shares when trading opened. It takes in the large blocks of stock still waiting for release, the sharp distance between the stock's best and worst days, and the debut that set the starting line for everything after it. Taken together, those numbers describe a stock whose price investors are still working out, not one they have agreed on.

SpaceX's first day of trading gave investors who bought at the offer price a clear win, and that gain became the reference point for every move since. Buyers who paid the $135 offer price ended the session holding shares worth $160.95, according to Investing.com. Buyers were even more eager during the day than that close suggests. Shares reached $176.52 at their high for the day, CNBC reports, and the stock finished the session below that level. The closing price marked where enthusiasm had cooled, not where it peaked. Any later gain or loss measured from it starts at a level traders had already retreated from once.

Not everyone agreed that SpaceX was worth even the offer price. Keith Snyder, senior analyst at CFRA Research, gave the stock a sell rating and a $115 12-month target, according to CNBC. In his view, the price was too high before buyers added any first-day premium. "It was a hard pill to swallow even there," Snyder said of the $135 pricing. The debut showed how much buyers wanted the shares. It did not settle whether SpaceX was worth what they paid.

Investors who bought SpaceX shares at the IPO price and held them are losing money, with the stock priced 11.47% below the offer in late November, according to Investing.com. That price was recorded in the middle of the Nov. 25 session, not at the close, so it is a snapshot of one afternoon, not a floor or a final verdict on SpaceX.

The distance between the stock's best and worst days is wide enough that no single price explains much on its own. Buyers pushed SPCX to its all-time high of $225.64 on June 16, in its first days of trading, so the top was set in the debut rush and not after a long run of gains. The low of $104.83 came on Aug. 3, early in a month when many investors were betting against the stock. Short interest peaked at 34% of public shares in the week before Aug. 12, according to S3 Partners figures reported by . A low set during a stretch of heavy short bets may reflect investor mood that summer more than what the company is worth.

The opening was also a managed event. CNBC reports that Nasdaq $NDAQ President Nelson Griggs saw strong backing among the orders for the opening, and that Morgan Stanley $MS led the effort to stabilize the stock's price. With a bank assigned to that role, the debut came out of an organized offering as well as open trading, and later sessions would show what price buyers set on their own.

The debut set a starting point, not a minimum price, and the months that followed would test how much of it held.

Short sellers pulled back sharply as the stock climbed off its bottom. By Aug. 12, short interest had fallen to about 11% of public shares, CNBC reports from S3 Partners figures, a drop that came partly from the larger pool of tradable shares after the first lockup expired the week before. "Shorts that wanted to short are out of bullets," said Ihor Dusaniwsky, managing director of predictive analytics at S3 Partners. With short interest down sharply, support for the price depends on investors who want to own SPCX.

SPCX's range records how investors were positioned as much as what SpaceX is worth.

The next big influence on SPCX's price may be a date on the calendar rather than news from a launch pad. On Dec. 8, the final IPO lockup tranche expires, and up to 797.6 million more shares become eligible for sale, according to Investing.com. That puts a large new group of potential sellers in the market on a single day. Some early holders have had the right to sell since the first lockup expired on Aug. 6, CNBC reports, and December adds to stock that has already been changing hands. To judge what December could mean for the price, investors first need to see how much new stock they have already had to take on.

The supply of tradable SPCX shares has grown in large steps more than once since the June debut. The August release alone made 911 million shares eligible for sale. CNBC reports that SpaceX sold 639 million shares when it listed, and further batches became eligible on Aug. 20, in September and in October. Investors have therefore already absorbed a single release larger than the entire IPO. December is best read as the last of the IPO-era unlocks rather than a single sudden event.

Shares that become eligible in December may push the price down, but nobody is required to sell them. "While the step-up in potential supply is meaningful, we think investors should understand that shares becoming eligible for sale does not mean the full tranche will be offered into the market," Mizuho analysts said in a report, as reported by CNBC. The largest block of stock is not part of the December release at all. Elon Musk holds more than 6 billion SpaceX shares and cannot sell them until his lockup ends in June 2027, according to CNBC. Through December, any selling from the lockup releases comes from early investors rather than the company's largest holder.

For anyone holding or eyeing SPCX, two questions remain open: how much early investors actually sell, and what Musk does once his own lockup ends.
