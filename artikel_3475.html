30 Years Later, One of the Greatest ‘X-Files’ Episodes is Still a Misunderstood Masterpiece

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

For the first three seasons of The X-Files, the writers kept you guessing, not only with regard to the show’s greater mysteries about aliens, but also about its most intriguing character. The Cigarette Smoking Man, played by William B. Davis, begins the series as a shadowy, silent character in the first episode. He looks on as Agent Dana Scully (Gillian Anderson) is given her not-too-subtle orders to undermine Agent Fox Mulder (David Duchovny) and his work on the X-Files. He goes on to appear sparingly, just a few times each season, to stop Mulder and Scully from getting too close to the secrets he’s spent decades covering up.

We’re led to assume that he’s some high-ranking G-man who’s privy to the government’s darkest secrets — and nothing else. We never get a definitive name or clarity about his position. His sporadic appearances always leave the viewers wanting to know more, but in season 4, the show finally provides perhaps too much information, while still managing to create a classic, memorable episode.

Debuting on Nov. 17, 1996, “Musings of a Cigarette Smoking Man” was the seventh episode of The X-Files' fourth season. It begins with a rather shocking setup, which shows the Cigarette Smoking Man pointing a rifle out of the window of a building. In his crosshairs is Melvin Frohike (Tom Braidwood), part of the trio of conspiracy theorists who run a tabloid called The Lone Gunmen. The Cigarette Smoking Man listens in on Frohike’s conversation with Mulder and Scully as he prepares to fire.

Frohike tells Mulder and Scully that he may have discovered the history of the Cigarette Smoking Man, and he begins by talking about the most notorious conspiracy in American history: the assassination of President John F. Kennedy.

From there, the episode flashes back to the early 1960s, showing a young Cigarette Smoking Man as an Army Captain stationed at Fort Bragg in North Carolina. He’s given a special assignment to kill Kennedy, which he then carries out. As the episode progresses, it jumps back and forth between the past and the present. In the second flashback, the Cigarette Smoking Man assassinates Martin Luther King Jr.

After that, it jumps forward to the early 1990s (before The X-Files begins) showing the Cigarette Smoking Man, and a committee of underlings, being behind such things as the Anita Hill controversy, the Rodney King verdict, and the 1992 Super Bowl where the Buffalo Bills lost to the Washington Redskins.

If the escalation there seems absurd, it's because it was kind of meant to be. Writer Glen Morgan, one of the show's early, formative writers, intended Frohike's report to be at least somewhat factually incorrect.

As producer Frank Spotnitz explained in a 1998 episode guide book, we should approach Frohike’s claims with a bit of skepticism. "In the closing scene, Frohike tells Mulder and Scully that the whole story was something he read in a crummy magazine,” Spotnitz said. “A lot of people didn't pick up on that subtlety. They thought that this was indeed the factual history of the CSM. As far as I'm concerned, it's not.” Though Spotnitz did add, “Some of it may indeed be true.”

The confusion, I think, comes from the fact that we don't just hear these stories from Frohike, we see them. A younger Cigarette Smoking Man, played by Chris Owens, is shown carrying out both assassinations, which I think convinces the audience that the stories are credible rather than dramatizations of Frohike's claims.

Still, there are some pretty great things about the episode, starting with Owens. Without doing an overt impression of William B. Davis, and without particularly resembling him, he gives a very convincing performance as the younger version of the character. In fact, he did such a good job that he was eventually brought back to reprise the role. He also went on to play the Cigarette Smoking Man's son, Jeffrey Spender, a recurring character beginning in Season 5.

The episode is also very well-written and deeply interesting. Some cool moments include the Cigarette Smoking Man crossing paths with Mulder’s father in the 1960s and, in the 1990s, he interacts with the beloved Deep Throat character, who dies in season 1. While the episode gets a little absurd in some of the things it attributes to the Cigarette Smoking Man, him having a link to the JFK assassination, at least, feels right given the show's obsession with conspiracies. And the scenes featuring the Cigarette Smoking Man and Deep Throat include a truly memorable conversation about the lives they’ve lived, followed by a heartbreaking moment that I won't spoil.

The episode was also successful in its intended goal of adding to the wider mythos of the character. Regardless of whether or not people interpreted it correctly, the episode enhanced the profile of the Cigarette Smoking Man, making him bigger than ever. It also left enough room in between the flashbacks to suggest that the Smoking Man may have been responsible for many more nefarious deeds in the latter 20th century. Even with the confusion over its canon, it certainly didn’t hurt his profile as the Cigarette Smoking Man would continue to be a mysterious and intimidating villain for the rest of The X-Files’ run. And, decades later, he’s still a beloved character by fans of the series.

Even I assume, buy X-Files fans who root for the Buffalo Bills.
