A diving pioneer found a Roman wreck off Portofino in 1959, then it vanished until police divers found it again 66 years later

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Carabinieri divers training off Punta Chiappa, Italy, found 9 Roman wine amphorae at a Roman wreck first reported in 1959 and lost since 1960.

On November 7, 1959, the Italian diving pioneer Duilio Marcante went down off Punta Chiappa, near Camogli in northwest Italy. At about 150 feet (45 meters) he found Roman amphorae jammed between the rocks, the first sign of a Roman wreck.

Six months later the pile he had described was gone. Nobody found the site again for 66 years.

During a recent training dive, Carabinieri divers came across an intact amphora at the spot. Italian heritage officials said on October 22, 2026, that it is Marcante’s lost wreck. The announcement mentions no remains of the hull.

The divers belong to the Nucleo Carabinieri Subacquei of Genoa, the diving unit of Italy’s military police. During the exercise they spotted a complete Dressel 1 amphora, a standard Roman wine-jar type, at more than 130 feet (40 meters).

The spot lies inside the Portofino Marine Protected Area, a very busy diving area. The divers reported it at once to the regional heritage office, the Soprintendenza for Liguria.

Follow-up dives were run with the office’s own diving unit, the Servizio Tecnico di Archeologia Subacquea (S.T.A.S.). The first map counted nine amphorae in a small area along cracks in the cliff.

The jars are hard to spot because they are fused to the rock under a thick coralligenous crust, a hard pink layer built mainly by calcareous algae.

According to the official press release, the team lifted the two most exposed jars of the nine, because large numbers of divers pass over the area.

Marcante (1914-1985) ran the Centro Subacqueo Mediterraneo and a spearfishing school in Genoa. In 1958 he had joined the first campaign on the Roman ship of Spargi, in Sardinia.

On his 1959 dive he saw two amphorae stuck to the rocks by stony coral and calcareous algae. He swam around the area and saw more jars nearby. At the foot of the cliff there seemed to be a mound, covered by muddy sediment.

On Monday, November 9, the team dove again and brought up two intact jars. They feared looters would take them. The next day Marcante reported the site to the heritage office and to the underwater archaeology center in Albenga.

The difficulties of the season prevented an immediate follow-up. Nino Lamboglia, who directed the Albenga center, later wrote that the depth and steep seabed also argued against sending the ship Daino, then in its first year of work.

On May 8 and 9, 1960, Gianni Roghi went back with Marcante and the Carabinieri. Roghi worked for the Albenga center, run by Lamboglia (1912-1977), a founder of Italian underwater archaeology.

The mound in the mud was gone. They found one amphora tied with a thin line, ready to be carried off by looters. Three broken jars were still locked in the crust.

Lamboglia described the case at an underwater archaeology congress in Barcelona in September 1961. He wrote that the site had probably not stayed “immune from clandestine hoarders.” After that, nobody found it.

Simon Luca Trigona, who heads S.T.A.S. Liguria, said the survey and a first look at the jars confirm that this is Marcante’s site.

The amphorae form a uniform cargo of Dressel 1A wine jars from the Tyrrhenian side of Italy. The type dates them to between the late second century BCE and the first decade of the first century BCE.

Trigona tied the cargo to the years just after Rome conquered southern Gaul. Genoa and the other Ligurian ports were then important for Roman trade.

A second wreck from the same period was found off Pegli, on the western side of Genoa, in the early 1950s. It has not been located since either.

Other cargoes from the Roman era have survived in very different places, from a ship off Adrasan in Turkey with its plates still stacked to intact goods in Lake Neuchâtel.

The heritage office says the site now needs regular checks and rules for divers. Colonel Samuele Sighinolfi, who commands the Carabinieri naval and diving group, said it is under constant surveillance.

Major Alessandro Caprio leads the Genoa unit of the Carabinieri heritage command. He said his officers watch auctions, websites and illegal markets for the work of “tombaroli del mare,” or sea tomb robbers.

Tips from divers have mattered elsewhere in Italy, as when off Sardinia a diver alerted authorities to a hoard of about 50,000 Roman coins. Busy dive sites carry risks too, and a study that tracked more than 700 divers found that most reef damage is accidental.

Superintendent Vincenzo Tiné said the find shows how much work remains, “even in areas that we consider well studied.” He noted that thousands of divers visit them every year.

Giovanni Anelli, the mayor of Camogli, hopes the jars can one day go on display. Guglielmo Caversazio, president of the marine protected area, offered to help protect the site.

So far the divers have recorded only jars. No timber from the ship has been reported. The release does not say how many amphorae the vessel carried.

“Difficult to imagine the size of the vessel,” Trigona said. Only the more detailed surveys planned for the near future can confirm other remains, he added.

The release also does not say where the two jars raised in 1959 are today. Nor does it explain what became of the mound at the base of the cliff.

Alessandra Cabella, an art historian on the S.T.A.S. Liguria diving team, put the story in three words. “Discovered, lost, rediscovered,” she said. Carabinieri divers were there in 1959, and again for the rediscovery.

The information was obtained from the Soprintendenza Archeologia, Belle Arti e Paesaggio per la Liguria.
