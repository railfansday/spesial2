30 Greatest Football Managers of All Time Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

Correspondent AUTHORITY Football journalist with experience at Reach PLC and PA Media; University of Central Lancashire graduate. FOCUS Fan-engagement content and match analysis across the Premier League and Championship. He provides the human stories behind the results to ensure fans get the full picture as it happens.

World-class players win matches, but legendary managers build dynasties that dominate football for decades and alter the whole sport's evolution.

Tactical innovators like Johan Cruyff, Arrigo Sacchi, Helenio Herrera and Rinus Michels transformed how the game is played, paving the way for modern masters, who have become celebrities in the same way as the players they oversee.

Serial winners such as Sir Alex Ferguson, Pep Guardiola, Carlo Ancelotti, and Jurgen Klopp have set extraordinary standards across Europe’s biggest clubs and premier tournaments.

Football Insider has now ranked the 30 greatest football managers of all time based on major silverware, tactical influence, longevity, and their ability to build iconic teams.

Scroll through our countdown from 30 to number one, and tell us in the comments which boss deserves to sit higher.

Pozzo was a visionary tactician who pioneered the 2-3-2-3 Metodo formation, which is still widely used in football today. He helped to establish the importance of flexibility within a team and was a leading figure in pushing proper fitness regimes among managers of his era.

He remains the only manager to win the men’s World Cup twice, with Italy in 1934 and 1938, while he also led his country to Olympic Gold in 1936. Pozzo’s controversial links to fascist leaders do contribute to his legacy being less celebrated than many on this list.

Zidane is perhaps the finest modern example of a world-renowned player being able to evolve into a top-class manager. The Frenchman has only been in the profession for 12 years, but has already shown just how much his elite knowledge of the game on the pitch has translated to the sideline. His biggest strength is an underrated skill of being able to unite some of the best players in the world to focus on one shared goal.

He is the only manager ever to win the Champions League three consecutive times, and numerous personal accolades were awarded to him for his impressive spell at Real Madrid. He can continue to further his legacy with more success on the international stage too.

Wenger is one of the most iconic managers to ever grace English football, not least due to his stubborn personality that often saw him clash with opposing bosses. There were few more memorable sights on a touchline than the Frenchman standing with his arms outstretched in a long Arsenal coat. He was also a fantastic coach who shaped the game on these shores into what it is today.

He revolutionised football nutrition in England by challenging bad habits of players and pushing for huge advancements in conditioning and recovery. These all contributed to him building an invincible Gunners team in 2004, while he racked up a total of 17 honours in 22 years with the club. Wenger still holds the record for most Premier League games managed (828) and most FA Cup wins as a boss (7).

Stein’s influence on the game and others lands him on this list, but his achievements as a manager were not too bad either. The Scot is credited with showing British clubs that they could play free-flowing, attacking football against other European sides and still dominate, while he also mentored Sir Alex Ferguson into becoming an all-time great.

He led Celtic’s Lisbon Lions to 1967 European Cup success, becoming the first British boss to win the competition, and established the club as the best in Scotland during that era following a tough mid-century period, with 25 honours won in 12 years to help build them into the dominant force they are seen as today.

It is best to try and forget his time in charge of England when analysing just how astute Capello was as a manager. The ruthless Italian icon could mould top teams into winning machines and his best sides were built on elite defensive solidity as well as the flexibility that allowed formation changes to beat opponents with ease.

His cabinets are brimming with trophies, including league titles with AC Milan, Real Madrid and Roma, as well as numerous continental accolades with the former. He cannot rank higher because the game seemed to move on from his slightly one-dimensional tactics towards the end of his managerial career.

Lattek remains one of the most revered and successful managers in German football history. He was an elite motivator who utilised his charm and wit to get top players to buy into his thinking. Pragmatism and organisation were always important components of his sides, and he never swayed from what he felt was right for his squad.

He was the first manager to win three separate European honours across spells at Bayern Munich, Borussia Monchengladbach and Barcelona, while also picking up six league titles with the Bavarian giants and a further two with BMG.

Back to the modern day, and Klopp earns his place on this list for being one of the most highly regarded bosses of the 21st century so far. He has personality in bucketloads, an almost unmatched aura and a distinctive cap-and-glasses look that makes him one of the most recognisable managers ever. He pioneered the idea of Gegenpressing and has influenced a generation of current and future managers with his ‘heavy-metal’ style of play.

After reviving Borussia Dortmund from mid-table to consecutive Bundesliga titles, he made Liverpool believe again and guided them to a maiden Premier League title, Champions League success, a handful of cups and a decade-long ding-dong with Man City - who have probably stopped him being ranked even higher on this list - all while overseeing a swashbuckling style of play.

Too low? The fall-out from the Man City financial scandal means history may judge he was likely robbed of a couple of league titles and maybe a few cups as well. But the Klopp story is far from finished after he made a welcome return to the dugout by accepting the Germany national team job at a low ebb in his country's history. He could go shooting into the top 10 by the end of his career.

Heynckes was a master man-manager and tactically astute, with his humility allowing teams to brim with tenacity and quality in their quest for success. The German boss meticulously prepared for opponents and studied the work of past greats to shape his philosophy as a manager.

He led Real Madrid and Bayern to Champions League titles, with the latter trophy making him the first German manager ever to win a continental treble. His outstanding longevity also contributes to earning a place on this list, with 23 years between his second and third Bundesliga titles as he was able to adapt throughout four decades of management.

Guttmann was one of the outstanding bosses of the 20th century, and the story behind his success sets him apart from others on this list. He was captured and sent to a forced labour camp near Budapest towards the end of World War 2, but escaped and continued his career from 1945 onwards. The Hungarian coach was a pioneer of the 4-2-4 formation which massively influenced teams of the time to try and outscore their opponents.

He transformed Benfica into European champions in 1961 and defended the crown a year later, discovered and developed Eusebio, and won trophies in four different countries across the world as his impact spread far and wide.

One man who knows everything there is to know about success on the world stage is unassuming leader Del Bosque. The mastermind behind arguably the greatest international team of all time, his man-management and innate ability to experiment to his players’ strengths were the driving forces behind Spain’s success in the 2000s and 2010s.

Their three consecutive trophies in that era were all presided over by Del Bosque, while he also guided Real Madrid to several major trophies during their Galacticos era at the turn of the century, including two Champions League titles in three seasons. He was never the most tactical thinker, but deserves plaudits for the way he could galvanise a squad of stars.

Van Gaal’s influence on modern coaching as we know it can not be understated, with the likes of Pep Guardiola, Jose Mourinho and Luis Enrique all counting on him as a main mentor at Barcelona. He boasted a great record of identifying and developing future stars, and was an eccentric genius, with people hanging on every word in his endless humorous press conferences.

His teams dominated possession and always knew their roles, with Van Gaal able to revolutionise Ajax in the 1990s, as they won 11 trophies in four years, while he also led Barcelona, AZ and Bayern to league titles and enjoyed relative success at Man United and with the Netherlands.

Hitzfeld may not have been a tactical genius, but he was able to use practical innovation to get the most out of his sides. He furthered the idea of squad rotation in Germany towards the end of the 20th century, built exceptionally well-drilled and flexible teams and was never tied to one single formation, getting success from numerous different set-ups.

The German boss was successful in Switzerland before winning seven Bundesliga titles in 14 years across spells at Dortmund and Bayern, as well as the Champions League with the former in 1997 and with the latter in 2001. He is one of a select few to lift the trophy with two different clubs.

The modern-day ‘catenaccio’ idea of defensive solidity in Italy was first promoted by Rocco at Padova in the late 1950s. His central idea was to make his teams sit deep in a tight defensive structure, then counter-attack when they won the ball back, with his influence felt in generations that followed as the national team and successful Serie A clubs usually prided themselves on a strong backline before anything else.

Rocco was successful at Triestina and Padova, then took his ideas to AC Milan in the 1960s and won two European Cups and 10 overall major trophies, meaning he remains their most decorated manager ever. He has also managed (459) and won (243) more Milan games than anyone else in history.

Lippi conquered football in every way possible. The elegant silver-haired Italian combined unescapable confidence in his abilities and tactical intelligence to build some of the greatest club and international sides of all-time, with a particular focus on high pressing and quick transitions.

He turned Juventus into a powerhouse in the 90s, presiding over five Serie A titles and their 1996 Champions League victory, as well as being on the losing side of three finals. Lippi then led Italy to World Cup glory in 2006, becoming the first manager ever to win both trophies. He even had time to become the first, and only, boss to lift the AFC Champions League as well as the UEFA version in a successful spell with Guangzhou Evergrande.

If you want to know what managerial charisma looks like, watch the YouTube videos of Peak Clough, whose razor-sharp wit, chip on both shoulders and love of the limelight made him media gold. English football owes a huge amount to Clough, who understood the value of the best in his profession - and in his eyes he was always top dog - in an era when boards of directors certainly did not.

The Middlesbrough native, whose career has inspired numerous books and films, was an unashamed eccentric who knew his players inside out and would conjure the most inventive methods to build them up or bring them back down to earth. His teams were easy on the eye, too, and he made no apologies for favouring entertaining, technical football in an era when many opponents' Plan A, B and C was route one. Clough was also smart enough to understand his own weaknesses, and was at his best when he had ace talent-spotter Peter Taylor at his side.

He was the ultimate overachiever as a manager, leading Derby County from the second tier to top flight champions, then doing the same at Nottingham Forest as they also clinched back-to-back European Cups in 1979 and 1980. Clough was an extraordinarily astute boss and larger-than-life personality who became a household name across the footballing world and paved the way for manager to become celebrities in their own right.

Paisley was initially reluctant to succeed Bill Shankly at Liverpool in 1974, but his decision to do so would ultimately change English football forever. The quiet, flat-capped boss was a genius on the training pitch who provided attention to detail and an emphasis on keeping the ball that had rarely been seen before on these shores. His expert recruitment also helped the Reds to keep on winning, with club legends Kenny Dalglish, Ian Rush, Graeme Souness and Alan Hansen all signed under his watch.

The Durham-born manager became the first to win three European Cups in his time at Anfield, while Liverpool also won an extraordinary six league titles in nine years and hoovered up domestic cup triumphs. He could be ranked higher on this list, but many feel like his tenure was a continuation of Shankly’s success rather than completely his own doing.

Trapattoni’s incredible longevity means he is one of only five managers in history to have presided over more than 1,000 club games and 100 national team games. He helped to define Italian football’s tactical identity in the 1970s and 80s, furthering Rocco’s catenaccio philosophy into a system that could better incorporate attacking stars like Michel Platini and Paolo Rossi at Juventus.

He won titles in four different European countries, and is one of three coaches to have triumphed in all three major European club competitions, with his best spell coming at Juve as he won 14 trophies in 13 seasons. Trapattoni also led the Republic of Ireland to their first European Championships in 24 years in 2012 before retirement.

Munoz’s achievements may be somewhat forgotten by the wider footballing world, but he was one of the sport’s first managerial greats in Europe and utilised his superior tactical intelligence to build a Real Madrid superteam in the 1960s and 70s which blew sides away with dominant attacking prowess and positional fluidity.

Madrid’s front three of Alfredo Di Stefano, Ferenc Puskas and Francisco Gento were allowed to run riot under Munoz as they won two European Cups and nine La Liga titles in 12 years under his guidance. He set the lofty standards and expectations at the Bernabeu that are still prevalent today.

An abrasive and blunt Austrian, Happel was another to champion the idea of ‘Total Football’ in Europe during a time of huge tactical change in the 1960s and 70s. He was the first manager to pioneer the 4-3-3 in Dutch football, which then became a huge part of other clubs’ successful systems on the continent.

His own career did not come without huge glory too, as he won league and cup titles in four different countries, lifted the European Cup with Feyenoord and Hamburg and took the Netherlands to their second-ever World Cup final in 1978.

The sleeping giant that we know Man United to be today only grew to become such a huge club because of Busby. Fans still sing about his ‘Busby Babes’ each week, with his entertaining mid-20th century sides becoming some of the first in England to prioritise quick forward play and individual freedom.

Even though the 1958 Munich air disaster decimated his team and changed his life dramatically, Busby recovered to build an iconic United side that became the first English club to win the European Cup in 1968 and won trophies galore. His story is one of tragedy, perseverance and ultimate success, as he paved the way for future Old Trafford sides to achieve even more continued glory.

Shankly played a similar role at Liverpool to Busby at rivals United, as he rebuilt the Anfield club with charisma and a fiercely competitive spirit around the same era of football. He promoted a pass-and-move style that succeeded through in-depth scouting for the time and unique motivational techniques.

His warrior spirit saw the Reds rise from the second division into a global football powerhouse in a matter of seasons, as they won three top-flight titles and a first-ever European crown in 1973. Shankly arguably had the most influence on a single club than anyone ever in British football history, as he introduced ‘You’ll Never Walk Alone’ as their anthem and laid the foundation for Paisley’s later success.

Lobanovskyi is revered as one of the greatest Ukrainian sportspeople of all time, having played his part in revolutionising football in the 1970s and 80s. He pioneered the idea of pressing as a collective, using computer analysis and statistics to gain the edge on opponents, and meticulous planning of certain match situations in training.

The in-depth, sometimes confusing, ideas in football as we know it today were effectively kickstarted by Lobanovskyi at Dynamo Kyiv. He is one of the most successful managers of all time, with 30 trophies to his name across a 32-year career that also saw him take charge of the USSR, UAE and Ukraine.

‘The Special One’ has been a divisive character in the modern era of football, but no one can deny his outstanding talent as a manager, which goes hand-in-hand with his brash, outspoken nature. Mourinho is one of the most high-profile coaches of all time, across all sports, and he remains box-office to this day after over 20 years in the dugout.

He took Europe by storm by winning the Champions League with Porto in 2004, then succeeded at Chelsea, Inter and Real Madrid, with seven league titles across four countries between 2002 and 2012. He completed a hat-trick of continental crowns at Roma in 2022 and could still add more to his cabinet in the coming years.

Ancelotti remains one of the most intriguing characters to ever take to the dugout, but maybe for what he is not, rather than what he is, as a manager. He has an innate knack for consistently building winning sides with little to no flaws, despite never being regarded as a master tactician in an era full of such intricacies.

The aura-laden Italian is one of football’s greatest adaptors, who can gain the trust of stars and manage numerous egos at once without a flaw. He has succeeded in England, France, Germany, Italy and Spain, with no other boss able to match his record five Champions League wins with Milan and Real Madrid.

Argentine-born boss Herrera took the idea of catenaccio to another level in the mid-20th century with numerous teams across Europe, as the idea of defensive solidity being a team’s main focus was heralded globally due to his success with the idea. He was famously self-confident, sometimes bordering on arrogance, and one of the first big personalities to grace the touchline, as he also pioneered the use of psychological motivating skills in football.

He won seven league titles across Spain and Italy and helped Inter become a dominant force with European Cup triumphs in 1964 and 1965, with the development of his ideas creating the iconic ‘Grande Inter’ side of that era.

Cruyff was a genius as a player and a manager. He became one of the most influential bosses in the history of the sport by developing ‘Total Football’ theories and having ultimate influence over the iconic bosses that followed him at Ajax, Barcelona and beyond. He is still heralded as one of the icons of the sport to this day, who exuded confidence and was a maverick in his own right.

He was obsessed with keeping possession and effectively invented the idea of a false nine at Barcelona. Cruyff impressed at Ajax but his biggest achievement was the complete transformation of Barca into the club it is today, as he led them to four titles in the 90s and several continental trophies after a lengthy period of struggle. His vision for ‘La Masia’ also came true and helps the club dominate world football into the present day.

The Dutchman’s predecessor and mentor ranks slightly ahead of him on this list for being the main architect behind ‘Total Football’. Michels was an absolute visionary and saw the game like no one else before or after him. He transformed the way managers approached football by giving players individual roles and making several ideas towards pressing and positional rotation work together for one whole approach that was way ahead of its time.

He turned Ajax into the definitive biggest and most successful club in the Netherlands, winning their first European Cup, while he won trophies in Spain and Germany too, and led the Netherlands to their sole major trophy at the 1988 European Championships. Michels was named the FIFA Coach of the Century in 1999.

Sacchi begins the top three after a 28-year managerial career that, unlike so many on this all-time list, did not follow any kind of professional playing spell. He went from being a shoe salesman to pioneering high pressing, zonal defending and collective attacking movement. He put Italian football back on the map by building arguably the greatest club side ever at AC Milan between 1987 and 1991.

The Italian boss won the Serie A title in his debut season at the club, then led the club to successive European Cups in 1989 and 1990 as he discarded the ideas of those that came before him and was an obsessive, uncompromising character with huge influence on several big names who followed him, such as Ancelotti, Klopp and Guardiola.

Pep Guardiola has reshaped modern soccer more profoundly than any manager of his generation. Fusing the positional play of Johan Cruyff and Rinus Michels into an obsessive, high-pressing blueprint, the Catalan tactician turned Barcelona, Bayern Munich, and Manchester City into relentless winning machines before stepping down at the Etihad in 2026. Few coaches in history have matched his ability to innovate tactically - witness the English League One and League Two sides copying his sweeper-keeper and passing out from the six-yard boss innovations - while dominating possession and tempo at the highest level. Oh, and he cuts a stylish and hyper-trendy figure in the dugout, every bit as fashion-conscious as the players he oversees.

His trophy cabinet makes him the strongest challenger to the number-one spot, boasting an astonishing 40 major honors across just 17 seasons. Guardiola has captured 12 top-flight league titles across Spain, Germany, and England—including an unprecedented English four-peat—alongside three Champions League crowns and two continental trebles. Currently on sabbatical and heavily linked with the United States national team, he sits second only because he has yet to rebuild a dynasty across multiple decades.

No manager in football history has sustained elite success across as many eras as Sir Alex Ferguson. Combining ruthless authority, masterclass man-management, and an unmatched hunger to rebuild, the Scot repeatedly tore down and reinvented title-winning squads over nearly four decades on the touchline. Even when rivals spent heavier or introduced new tactical systems, Ferguson adapted faster and willed his teams over the line.

He could get angry - pity the poor player or referee or journalist who got on his wrong side - but he had empathy as well and was a psychological master who knew there was more than one way to build a winning team. One of Ferguson's more overlooked skills was his ability to adapt and refresh, both his backroom staff and his own management style. He knew when to turn on the 'hairdryer' and when to switch it off.

After breaking the dominance of Celtic and Rangers in Scotland at Aberdeen with scarcely believable achievements, he became the father figure of Man United for a record-breaking 26-year tenure, winning 49 major trophies and dominating the Premier League throughout generations as many tried and failed to dislodge them at the top. He was a genius whose players may not all have adored him, but they all respected him and look back knowing he was almost always right. It will take an extraordinary figure to dislodge him from top spot.
