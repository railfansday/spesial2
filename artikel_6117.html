The Main Obstacles Boeing Faces If It Wanted To Build An Ultra-Long-Range 787

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

The Airbus A350 is already an exceptionally fuel-efficient aircraft with more range than almost any other airliner ever built, but it wasn't enough for some airlines still. In 2018, Airbus began delivering the appropriately named A350-900ULR, which currently operates the world's two longest flights, from Singapore to Newark and JFK for Singapore Airlines.

Meanwhile, Airbus will begin delivering the A350-1000ULR to Qantas in 2027, which will use the aircraft for nonstop flights from Sydney to New York JFK and London. The closest competitor to the A350 in terms of range and ultra-long-haul economics is the Boeing 787-9.

While it's slightly smaller than the A350-900, it operates four out of the world's ten longest nonstop services, and the Dreamliner has been renowned by airlines for its fuel efficiency. However, Boeing has never built a 'ULR' or extended-range version of the 787-9, thereby ensuring that the A350 will remain the default choice for carriers operating at the very limits of what's possible.

The real reason why Boeing hasn't developed a 'ULR' version of the 787-9 is that its customers haven't asked for one. The A350-900ULR was developed specifically to give Singapore Airlines an aircraft that could fly between Singapore and New York nonstop reliably, rather than to be a mainstream product in the lineup.

The A350-900ULR incorporates aerodynamic improvements, increased usable fuel, and a higher gross weight, but nearly all of these improvements were later implemented on the standard A350-900. It's a similar situation with the A350-1000ULR, an aircraft that Airbus developed in response to a Qantas RFP for an aircraft capable of flying nonstop from Sydney to London with a reasonable payload.

It will not be a mainstream product for Airbus, and Qantas reportedly holds exclusivity rights that prevent other airlines from taking A350-1000ULRs until all 12 of its examples have arrived. Overall, the A350-900ULR and A350-1000ULR are both niche aircraft designed to meet extreme requirements set by one customer each. If an airline desired a ULR version of the 787-9 and was willing to pay for it, Boeing would have certainly developed the aircraft.

For pretty much all airlines, however, the standard 787-9 has more than enough range, which makes a ULR unnecessary. For all intents and purposes, Airbus isn't selling the ULR variants of its A350s since they were unique solutions for a single customer, while Boeing lacks these types of variants due to market demand.

The A350-900ULR and A350-1000ULR were developed from customer requests, but Airbus won these orders in large part because the A350 was very well-suited to be adapted to these variants. The 787-9, while generally an excellent aircraft for ultra-long-haul flights, is already being pushed close to its limits on routes like Perth-London.

The A350 has more fuel capacity and is able to carry more payload across longer distances, making it a more capable aircraft at the expense of slightly greater fuel burn. At the very edge of its range, the A350 is largely limited by its fuel capacity, and so the main differentiating factor of both ULR variants is that they offer significantly greater fuel capacity.

The 787-9, however, is limited by its Maximum Takeoff Weight in particular, restricting how much fuel it may carry and how much payload may be loaded. On its longest scheduled flight, from Perth to London, Qantas regularly has to block seats because the plane reaches its MTOW before all seats can be filled. Of course, MTOW isn't something that remains static.

Indeed, Boeing has recently upped the 787-9's MTOW from 254.7 tons to 259.2 tons, adding about 310 NM (570 km) of range. However, the 787-9 is also approaching its limits in terms of how much weight the structure and landing gear can support. Any further increases in MTOW would likely require significant structural strengthening and a landing-gear redesign, which would be an expensive project that might not be necessary today.

An increased maximum takeoff weight allowance is key for Boeing.

The 787-9 is a more limited aircraft than either A350 variant on ultra-long-haul routes, yet four of the world's ten longest routes use this aircraft, including number four. The A350 is also used on four of the ten longest routes, including the A350-900ULR on numbers one and two. While the A350 can carry more payload further, the appeal of the 787-9 is its low operating cost.

This is because the aircraft simply burns less fuel than the A350-900 in almost all circumstances. The appeal of these widebodies is their small size and low operating cost, which opens up routes that weren't previously economical. With its lower fuel burn and smaller size, the 787-9 takes this to a further extent than even the A350-900, let alone the A350-1000.

The A350-900 can carry more passengers and cargo, but the added capability comes at a premium, both in terms of operating cost and purchase price. If an airline doesn't need the capability, then the 787-9 is usually the most lucrative option. With the latest MTOW bump, the 787-9 can fly as far as 7,875 NM (14,585 km) with a good payload, and this is enough for almost every airline.

The A350 really appeals to airlines that need the extra seats or to carry substantial cargo. The market certainly exists, which is why the A350 has been as successful as it has been, but the 787-9 does about 95% of what the A350-900 does while being less expensive to acquire and operate. As such, it has become the most successful widebody aircraft variant of all time.

The 787-10 is a simple stretch of the 787-9, with essentially no changes apart from the longer fuselage. This significantly increases the aircraft's revenue potential, and it doesn't weigh dramatically more than its larger sibling, which means that fuel burn is only slightly higher. It also has largely the same MTOW and fuel capacity as the 787-9.

This means that it could hypothetically operate most of the same routes as the 787-9 if carrying the same amount of payload. However, the point of the 787-10 is to carry more passengers and cargo than the 787-9. As it has the same gross weight, this means that there's less weight available to carry fuel, which means that the aircraft can't fly as far.

The 787-10 remains quite capable, and it can fly as far as 6,760 NM (12,519 km) with a substantial payload. However, its design directly trades range for payload, which means that it won't be regularly operating ultra-long-haul routes, which is the job of its smaller sibling. The 787-8 was the original 787 variant, but its range is actually shorter than the 787-9.

This is because its MTOW is actually lower than that of the 787-9, at only 227.9 tons, which means that the aircraft cannot carry as much fuel when fully loaded with a typical payload. While Boeing could likely increase the aircraft's MTOW, there hasn't been demand for an upgraded version of the 787-8 as sales have dried up, as airlines instead prefer the more economical 787-9 and 787-10.

While the 787-9 can do almost everything that the A350 can, the Airbus is the final answer for the world's longest flights. Not only does the A350 fly further than the 787-9, but it carries more payload while doing so, which means that airlines can typically earn more revenue with the aircraft.

Meanwhile, to operate the Singapore-New York routes, Airbus upped the A350-900's MTOW to 280 tons, updated its aerodynamics, blocked the forward cargo hold, and added usable fuel volume through software changes to create the A350-900ULR. It's really the fuel volume that is valuable for Singapore Airlines, as the airline doesn't even max out the aircraft's MTOW on its New York services.

In addition, the A350-900ULR's new wing twist and 280-ton MTOW were later ported over to the standard A350-900 model, which is now available with a 283-ton MTOW. But with the ULR's added fuel volume (achieved by allowing the aircraft to use more of its existing fuel tank volume), it can operate between New York and Singapore reliably. Meanwhile, Qantas has selected the A350-1000ULR for its Project Sunrise routes connecting Sydney to New York and London.

The A350-1000ULR incorporates an MTOW bump from 322 tons to 324 tons, but the biggest new feature for the aircraft is a new integrated rear centre tank, the engineering of which is similar to the RCT on the A321XLR. The RCT adds over 20,000 liters (5,280 gallons) of fuel capacity, adding over 1,000 NM (1,850 km) of range and allowing it to operate the world's longest flights.
