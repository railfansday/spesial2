The 4 Best Wearable Displays Project a Virtual Big-Screen Anywhere You Need

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

This heavy-duty AR eyewear is designed to use with your phone, laptop, or gaming handheld.

We’re still a long way away from proper augmented reality (AR) glasses that blur the lines between the internet and reality. Right now, there are two types of tech eyewear: Smart glasses, like Meta’s camera-clad Ray-Bans, do half the job, offloading features like cameras, speakers, and an AI assistant from your phone to your glasses. The other half, which has arguably been around longer but fewer people talk about, is wearable displays, which use AR technology to project a virtual screen in front of your eyes. (If you’ve ever tried watching a movie on a VR headset like the Meta Quest or playing a non-VR game on a PlayStation VR headset, it’s very similar.)

Plugged into a phone or a laptop, they can replace its small, portable screen with a massive projection that nearly engulfs your field of view so you can watch movies, play games, or work on a virtual monitor without taking up an inch of desk space or monopolizing your living room TV. With built-in speakers and a setup that can be as simple as plugging into a compatible device, they’re a handy companion for travelers, handheld gaming PC owners, or anyone who wants a more private way to binge-watch their favorite shows. Think of them as a personal theater you can set up anywhere.

Though they’ve been around for a while now, wearable displays are still fairly niche. There are some well-known computing brands in the space, but many of the biggest ones may be new to you. And with some confusing specs like resolution per eye and degrees of freedom, it’s good to do your homework before committing to a new pair. These are the best wearable displays that we think are worth a closer look.

I evaluate wearable displays based on the same criteria as other displays: image quality, sound, ease of setup, and overall usability. More specifically, I judged the clarity and contrast of images and video to gauge sharpness and legibility, as well as color depth and saturation. Most importantly, I wanted to see whether the screen remained clear and comfortable to look at over long viewing sessions. Since most wearable displays feature integrated speakers of some kind, I listened closely as well to gauge audio clarity.

Beyond the specs, I paid attention to what each model was actually like to use. Features such as spatial tracking and screen position controls sound impressive, but their value really depends on how well they work in practice and whether or not they meaningfully improve the viewing experience. I also weighed portability, connectivity requirements, and any additional accessories needed to get the most out of each pair.

Of my four initial recommendations, I’ve tested three of them to date, with more coming soon. I tested those glasses in my home and at our Easton, Pennsylvania, offices, watching YouTube podcasts, action/sci-fi movies, sitcoms (I’ve been in a New Girl phase for … a long time), and using them as a second monitor. I recommended the fourth model based on its slightly improved resolution per eye, wider field of view, and electrochromic dimming system.

RayNeo’s GT Max works well as a conventional display, but its primary appeal is its ability to function as a fully autonomous video streaming device. When paired with the Pocket TV Pro, an optional processor box that augments the glasses with additional built-in storage, battery, and processing power, you can stream video from Google TV, Netflix, YouTube, and many other popular streaming services without taxing your phone or laptop.

The GT Max has dual 1080p Micro-OLED displays with a 120-Hz refresh rate and a 59-degree field of view. The virtual screen expands up to 307 inches, which is the largest virtual screen I’ve tried to date. It also features 3DoF head tracking that allows you to anchor the screen or have it “follow” your eyes. When watching Royal Court (my favorite YouTube podcast set in medieval times), I could make out details and features just as well as I could if I was watching on my television, which sits about eight feet in front of my couch.

A little more on the Pocket TV Pro: It adds 64GB of onboard storage, and a 6,500mAh battery rated up to 5.5 hours of viewing. It’s also the key to getting Dolby Vision. When I used the accessory with the GT Max, I felt like I was getting a more traditional streaming device experience without needing to keep my phone connected.

On the audio side, The GT Max features a quad-speaker system, which sounded solid, though their open-ear design lets in some background noise (while that’s expected, you can always pair these with your favorite headphones or earbuds if it’s really a bother). In quieter settings, I had no issues hearing my YouTube videos, but if you were using these on a commute or an airplane, ambient noise might overpower what you’re listening to.

While the Pocket TV Pro gives the advantage for standalone streaming, the glasses still function just as well when connected to a phone or laptop. Video stayed sharp across my devices, and the fixed-screen mode was useful when I was working on my computer, though I occasionally had to zoom in on documents to make text easier to read at the display’s viewing distance. Whether you want a private theater experience or a more flexible external display, the GT Max does both.

The XReal xbx a01+ is a more affordable entry-level option if you’re just dipping your toe in the wearable display pool. With a lightweight frame and a plug-and-play USB-C connection, these are easy to use and good for casual gaming, streaming, and basic productivity work like email.

I also like that the xbx a01+ looks more like a pair of sunglasses than a set of generic black tech goggles. Blue polarized lenses give the design a little more personality, and the flexible temples and nose pad options help accommodate different sizes and fits. And with that lighter weight, you might actually be able to wear these around on your head regularly instead of keeping them tucked away in your bag.

The directional speakers handle audio well, and the USB-C cable supplies both the video signal and power. These also don’t skimp on features in the way you might expect for a more affordable model. You still get dual 1080p Micro-OLED panels, 120Hz refresh rate for gaming, a 50-degree field of view, and a plug-and-play setup.

One important caveat: the a01+ uses 0DoF stabilization instead of full 3DoF spatial tracking. The image is stabilized in front of you, but you don’t get the same anchored virtual monitor experience like you do with more expensive displays like the RayNeo GT Max or Viture Beast. For the price, though, it delivers the essentials without piling on a ton of extra features.

The RayNeo Air 4 Pro has strong display specs, with 1080p-per-eye Micro-OLED panels with a 120Hz refresh rate, 47-degree field of view, and HDR10 support. Adding HDR, which not all of these glasses have, improves image quality in a lot of streaming content, enabling bigger pops of color and more dynamic lighting. Podcasts and movies were vibrant and had impressive contrast without being overly saturated.

The 201-inch virtual screen, bigger than the xbx a01+, but smaller than the RayNeo GT Max, hits a sweet spot that feels a lot like a movie theater. Adding to the theater experience, these glasses come with a lens shield that blocks out ambient light, and “ear tube” attachments pump audio directly into your ears for fuller, louder audio playback from the Air 4 Pro’s 4-speaker sound system. The lens shields are incredibly helpful if you’re in a brightly lit space. The ear tubes, less so, but they are worth using if you don’t have headphones to bring to the party.

The glasses connect easily via USB-C, but like most other AR glasses that pair directly to your device, you can expect your battery level to take a hit. With the Air 4 Pro, streaming drained my phone’s battery by around 20 percent per hour—the others I tested lost battery at around the same rate. That’s fine for shorter watching sessions, but you’ll likely need to plug your device into power for movie marathons.

Comfort was another sticking point. Despite a light overall weight of just 2.68 ounces, the Air 4 Pro feels front-heavy, so they slid forward a bit when I was looking down. I struggled to find a secure fit. Finally, the screen display is center-fixed in your field of view, so it follows your head rather than staying anchored in space, making these better for stationary viewing than virtual-monitor multitasking.

Despite some fit and tracking quirks, the RayNeo Air 4 Pro delivers a solid personal theater experience. HDR support, punchy colors, and good contrast make movies and shows look great, while the light shield helps to stay immersed in what you’re watching. If your main goal is turning your devices into big-screen entertainment, these glasses are still a respectable option.

The Viture Beast is a bit pricey, but moves the needle a bit on both images and field of view. Its Sony Micro-OLED displays output at 1920 by 1200 pixels per eye (slightly higher than standard 1080p), and stretch across a wider 58-degree field of view. That’s great for gaming, sports, and other viewing experiences where you really want to see everything. The 174-inch virtual screen isn’t the biggest I’ve seen, which is a shame given these specs, but the viewing distance is four meters, which is closer than average, so the screen feels bigger because you’re up close.

The glasses use 3DoF tracking, with options to anchor the screen, follow your head movements, or adjust the viewing layout. The Beast also has an electrochromic dimming system built directly into the lenses with nine adjustable levels to go from mostly transparent to completely dark, and you can manage these settings on the glasses themselves. No lens shields to deal with here.

My one gripe here is the weight: At a little over three ounces, they can start to tire you out in a long session. You may also balk at the price, which is about the same as the RayNeo GT Max and its optional processor box combined, but they’re definitely an appealing pick if you want to maximize for a wide, immersive screen.

The main selling point of wearable display glasses is to make a relatively small set of lenses feel like a much larger screen, so image quality is the name of the game here. Most models in this category put a Micro-OLED display in each lens, which offers the benefits of self-emissive backlight, including deep black and sharp contrast for better clarity on a small display, and vibrant colors.

Frankly, from what I’ve seen to date there isn’t all that much variation in the core specs of these screens. Most of the good ones will hit 1920 x 1080 pixels per eye, which is the equivalent of 1080p or “Full HD” on a conventional TV or monitor. Most can also hit a 120Hz refresh rate, which is perfect for console gaming.

We start to see differences among the displays when we start to talk about field of view, which refers to how far the screens stretch out to the periphery of your vision. A wider field of view is generally a good thing, as it means you’ll be able to see more of the screen without actively turning your head. This makes the experience of looking at a giant virtual screen more immersive.

The other place we see more differentiation is the size of the virtual screen itself. Different glasses create different screen sizes, but also set a standard viewing distance, so some screens will be bigger, but farther away. In the end, the quality of the “big screen experience” is a balance of all three of these specs, so you kind of have to see it to get a feel for what you like and what you don’t. That said, I would point to FOV as the most revealing spec: It requires better displays and technology to expand the field of view, so a larger one is generally going to make you “feel” the screen size more than a larger window.

Then there are the very important, but highly technical nuances of any display—brightness, contrast, color depth, and accuracy, among other considerations. I have not been using calibrating tools on these displays (conventional ones can’t work on a screen this small) so it really just comes down to what looks good. Higher NIT count (brightness) is always a good thing, but in general the best way to find the best screen is simply to try them and see.

Spatial tracking relates to how well the virtual display responds to your head movements and stays positioned in your field of view, while screen placement affects where the display actually appears and how comfortable it is to watch. Both can make the difference between a screen that feels like it’s floating in front of you and one that moves with you or stays anchored in place. Degrees of freedom determine how the display can move in relation to you.

More affordable wearable displays with zero degrees of freedom (0DoF) always keep the virtual screen image stabilized in front of your eyes, so the display moves with your head. It’s sufficient for watching movies since you’re looking at a personal screen, but, per the name, you can’t control the position of the screen or change your perspective.

Premium headsets should have at least partial head tracking, and specifically offer three degrees of freedom (3DoF). That gives you the option to “anchor” a virtual screen in space so you can turn your head to focus on different parts of the screen or even look away. This can make the experience feel more like sitting in front of a monitor or TV, particularly for working or gaming.

The best option, six degrees of freedom (6DoF), uses full 3D head tracking to allow you to adjust your distance from the screen, in addition to moving your view vertically and horizontally. Having full control allows you to increase or decrease your field of view and immersion for different tasks, so you get close up for a big-screen movie experience, or push the screen away for scrolling.

Based on my testing so far, 3DoF has been the sweet spot. It doesn’t track your movement through physical space the way 6DoF does, but I’d still hold off on buying a more advanced model unless you’re ready to spend $500 or more on a pair of AR glasses.

Just like TVs and smart glasses, most wearable displays feature speakers in the stems so you can listen without connecting your own headphones. While this is definitely convenient if you don’t carry a pair of headphones around, the speakers generally don’t perform as well as a good pair of headphones.

Most wearable displays feature open-ear speakers, which provide no noise cancellation, which can be jarring when you have a giant screen in front of you and have forgotten about the outside world. (My advice? You’re usually better off connecting a good set of active noise-canceling headphones to complete the immersive isolation you get inside these glasses.)

To further that “personal theater” feel, some wearable displays take extra steps to prevent light from squeezing in front of your eyes when you have the glasses on. Light control matters more than you think: Ambient lighting can affect contrast and make darker scenes harder to see.

Different companies take different approaches to this: The Viture Beast uses electrochromic dimming. Internal sensors detect ambient light and use a small voltage to change the lens from transparent to tinted. The RayNeo Air 4 Pro comes with a physical lens shield that you pop on over the lenses to block light from coming through.

One thing to note: Blocking light and sound is great when you want to feel like you’re in a comfortable, relatively private space, but losing situational awareness isn’t ideal when you’re in public. Finding a balance between immersion and awareness is a personal choice, but one that you should consider as you tinker with your setup.

Most wearable displays draw power from your phone or laptop, which means you don’t need to worry about battery life on the glasses themselves. That said, you do need to watch your device’s battery, because these glasses will drain it quickly. Some are more power-efficient than others, but in general I’d recommend plugging your device into an outlet (or at least a battery pack) if you’re planning to use it for an extended period.

With many phones, in particular, you may actually need to consider temperature as well. Displays that seriously tax a phone’s battery may cause the phone to overheat (you will feel it), which could impact performance.
