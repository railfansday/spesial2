Australian wages grow at fastest pace since 2023 – but prices are climbing even more quickly

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Wage rates climb about 1 percentage point faster than before pandemic, triggering warnings of a sixth RBA rate rise

Wages grew at their fastest pace since late 2023 over the three months to September, buoyed up by this year’s boost to minimum pay rates for millions of lower paid workers – but still not fast enough to keep up with the rising cost of living.

The Australian Bureau of Statistics’ wage price index climbed by 1.1% in the most recent quarter, lifting the annual increase to 3.5% – a pace that economists said would keep the Reserve Bank on edge about the need for further interest rate hikes.

The treasurer, Jim Chalmers, said the pickup in wages growth was thanks to the government’s support for the minimum pay rise.

“We’re helping to deliver higher wages for workers because we know this is one of the best ways to help with the cost of living,” he said.

While wage rates are climbing about 1 percentage point faster per year on average than before the pandemic, prices are climbing even more quickly – a reality that Chalmers recognised.

“Real wages are declining not because wages have been too low but because inflation is still too high, which is why helping in the fight against inflation and getting the budget in better nick are such high priorities for the government.”

The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, said the latest figures confirmed that “Australians are working harder for less”.

“Australians are poorer under Labor. That is the reality of the economy that has been built by a Labor government that simply does not get it,” he said at a press conference.

The Fair Work Commission in early June implemented a 6% pay rise to about 100,000 of the country’s lowest paid employees to help compensate for cost of living pressures made worse by surging fuel prices.

The annual review also gave a 4.75% pay rise to 2.7 million workers on award wages.

Rachael McCririck, head of prices statistics at the ABS, said these decisions “were a key driver of much of the growth in private sector wages this quarter”.

Analysts said wages were growing faster than the Reserve Bank had been expecting, and that this added to the case, if only at the margin, for a potential sixth interest rate hike.

Cherelle Murphy, EY’s chief economist, said she did not expect a move after the RBA’s final board meeting for the year on 7-8 December, even though the wage growth figure was “uncomfortably high for the central bank”.

“[It’s] unlikely to prompt any immediate reaction, given the economy is now slowing in response to the five interest rate hikes delivered between February and November this year,” Murphy said.

“We expect the central bank will be in wait-and-see mode,” she said.

The ABS figures showed pay rates are rising fastest in the public sector, up by an annual 3.9%, while private sector wages increased by 3.4%.

“The main driver of public sector wage growth was pay rises for state government frontline workers covered by enterprise agreements. This included groups like nurses, midwives and police officers,” McCririck said.

Jeff Borland, an economics professor at the University of Melbourne, said while the wage price index was useful to assess inflationary pressures in the labour market, it was less useful as a way to assess workers’ actual incomes.

“The WPI is the labour cost to an employer of having a particular job done. If you want to know what is happening to the average worker, then the weekly income from the [ABS’s] characteristics of employment survey is a better measure,” Borland said.

By this broader measure of worker pay, the story is not as dire as that told by the after-inflation change in the WPI, he said.

Real average hourly labour income received per employed person was artificially boosted through the pandemic but has since come back to be just 3.4% higher this decade.

This extends the overall weak trend through the 2010s, Borland said.

In contrast, the wage price index is down 4.4% since the start of 2020.

“None of the real wage measures are telling you things are going gangbusters,” Borland said.

“But there’s a difference in the degree to which they tell you how badly things are going.”
