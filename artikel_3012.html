​The Escalation Trap: AI Can Free You Or Finish You

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A Futurist and AI expert, he leads KŌSE Advisory, advises ODI Global and GLOBSEC, and is a key analyst on tech, geopolitics and value chains.

In 1519, conquistador Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico with 500 men, hell-bent on conquering the Aztec empire. Wary of his crew’s commitment, Cortés ordered his ships scuttled, foreclosing any retreat to Cuba. His men had two choices: surge forward or die where they stood. They chose the former. Within two years, they had conquered the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlán.

But human psychology can just as easily flip toward panic and paralysis. Take the fall of Singapore in 1942. Knowing the island was indefensible, London still rushed in reinforcements to force a prolonged siege. But it proved a futile effort. When Japanese forces crossed over quickly, 80,000 British and Commonwealth troops surrendered.

Escalating geopolitical tensions often appear binary: Leaders must choose between doubling down or giving up. But this is a false paradigm. (Even Cortés eventually ended up mired in legal battles, stripped of both political influence and fortune.) Ultimately, both are losing options.

There is now a third choice: AI. But it’s a double-edged sword. Applied with discipline, AI gives you control over the ambiguity driving every crisis. Applied carelessly, it accelerates the escalation.

In my previous article, I discussed the Casino Doctrine: the idea that the world is already at war without declaring it, and power belongs to whoever controls the uncertainty. A prime example of controlled ambiguity is the “Is it open or is it closed?” tension in the Strait of Hormuz, where one side holds the cards, forcing everyone else to guess what’s happening.

In failed ambiguity, like the Russia-Ukraine war, the bluff has been called, the cards are face up and uncertainty is no longer a weapon.

The escalation trap is the slide from that calculated tension into open conflict. Every player bets to keep leverage, and each move that fails to deter invites a bigger raise until the table flips. Navigating that spiral requires mastering two forces: disruptive power (fast, asymmetric strikes like drones hitting Russian oil infrastructure) and structural power (the slow-built control over global thoroughfares like the Panama Canal or Peru’s Chancay port, and Beijing’s near-monopoly over the world’s rare-earth supply).

When disruptive power attacks structural chokepoints, ambiguity collapses fast. Because geopolitics, supply chains and economics now sit at the exact same table, an escalation in one shakes them all—and there is no house enforcing the rules.

Why do capable leaders walk into the escalation trap? Because most never align the two kinds of power they need to survive. Ideology builds structural power: alliances, values, long-term commitments. Pragmatism yields disruptive power: fast, cheap, tactical moves that win the round. Oscillating between the two gets you the worst of both. Every pragmatic bet wins a hand but erodes the structural position; every ideological stance without pragmatism hardens into rigidity. True control means using tactical disruption to build, rather than hollow out, lasting structure, but few leaders manage this tightrope walk.

In a previous article, I wrote about Machiavelli’s advice that neutrality is a liability and choosing a side beats hesitating. For business leaders, hesitation breeds internal friction and confusion over priorities. That hesitation is the exact gap through which operational control slips, one undeclared escalation at a time.

For company leaders, what makes the geopolitical escalation ladder a trap isn’t the measurable risk you can price. Rather, it is uncontrolled ambiguity about what is actually happening on the ground. Is a strait officially closed or just too dangerous to cross? Is a new toll a routine fee or a covert blockade? Is a military drill a bluff or a rehearsal? Meanwhile, insurance premiums creep up, tankers go dark, ports are struck ... and yet no one declares war.

At each rung of that ladder, corporate leaders traditionally face two losing moves: surrendering by pulling up stakes and moving supply chains into the next region’s crisis, or scrambling to react after the market has already priced in the shock.

True control requires a different approach—not eliminating global uncertainty, but outthinking it before the rest of the market sees the cards. That is where trusted, sovereign, domain-specific AI changes the paradigm, using foresight analytics to drive the optimal decision before your competitors do.

AI began as a disruptive power, making work faster and cheaper. Today, it is evolving into a structural power, reshaping economies, national GDPs and societal norms simultaneously. I call this omnichange: the moment one technology transforms every layer of the system, accelerated by superintelligence and operational Darwinism.

To leverage it, leaders must answer two questions: What values guide your AI, and how fast can you deploy it? Answer both, and you turn disruption into lasting structure; answer only one, and you cede control.

Making that shift requires building domain-specific intelligence in disciplined stages, anchored by clear human accountability. To paraphrase Machiavelli, fortune is a river in flood that prudent leaders channel with dykes built in calm weather. Superintelligence will be either the dyke or the flood, and governance is what determines which.

Stockpiles decay; intelligence compounds. Winning in an era of gray-zone conflict requires every leader at the table to act right now:

• CEOs: Settle the ideology question today, for good. Define what you stand for, which allies you build with and what your AI is for.

• Chief Procurement Officers: No supplier, chip or cloud is neutral. Choose your intelligence stack as you would a geopolitical ally.

• Chief Supply Chain Officers: Pre-price alternative routes, and give AI agents audited, bounded authority to reroute before shocks hit.

• Boards: Govern AI as structural power, not an IT project. Keep humans accountable, and set clear stop-losses on every market and pilot.

Systems built for already-declared wars lose control first—and when they fail, developing economies pay the steepest price. The real question isn’t who stockpiles the most resources but who governs omnichange.

We are in a dangerous window. But a window is also an opening. Leaders who act decisively—and keep human accountability at the center of AI—will turn omnichange into lasting structural power. Those who wait for calm will find that the seats at the table are already taken.

As we say in Turkish, “Kurt dumanlı havayı sever“—the wolf loves foggy weather. How you govern AI will decide whether you are the wolf or the prey.​
