IREN, CoreWeave and Nebius Stock Prices Have Plunged More Than 60%. Here's What It Means for AI Investors

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

The neoclouds' revenue keeps climbing, but delays and heavy borrowing have cut their stocks by more than half.

By Jonathan Ponciano – Nov 18, 2026 at 3:05PM EST

Some of the hottest AI stocks of the past year have been crashing hard after reaching meteoric highs.

Through Tuesday's close, IREN (IREN -3.13%) was down 79% from its 2025 high of $76.41, closing at $15.81. CoreWeave (CRWV -4.30%) was 74% below its high from last year, and Nebius Group (NBIS +2.19%) was 63% below its June high. All three rent out Nvidia-powered computing to AI companies, and all three are part of a group investors call the "neoclouds."

The puzzling part is that their businesses are still growing fast.

IREN's revenue rose 66% from the prior quarter to $228 million in the three months ended Sept. 30, and its AI cloud revenue nearly tripled to $200 million. Its backlog of contracted revenue grew 22% to $20.3 billion, and the company says about two-thirds of that is with investment-grade customers, including Microsoft and Nvidia.

Meanwhile, CoreWeave's third-quarter revenue jumped to $3.66 billion from $1.37 billion a year earlier, and it raised its full-year outlook. Its backlog stands at a massive roughly $132.5 billion.

Finally, Nebius told investors that customer prepayments on new contracts now cover more than 100% of the cost of bringing that capacity online, up from 50% to 60% in the second quarter.

Start with timing. IREN had targeted more than $4 billion in annualized revenue by the end of 2026. It now expects to hit that in January, possibly not until the end of February, after a customer requested a design change and suppliers ran late. Nebius also pushed back the timing of its targets, though management said the changes were "just about timing, not demand." Nebius stock fell 17% the day it reported.

Then there's the cost of growth. CoreWeave earned $207 million in operating income last quarter but paid $846 million in net interest expense, significantly more than $310 million one year earlier and resulting in a net loss of $709 million. IREN still expects about $15 billion in spending for the rest of its fiscal year, and $6 billion of that isn't lined up yet. Nebius plans to spend $40 billion to $45 billion next year.

That's a lot of money riding on a small number of very large customers continuing to pay. And with investors starting to doubt how long the AI spending boom will last, it's no surprise companies that borrow heavily to build for it have been the first to get hit.

Nevertheless, analysts haven't given up. JPMorgan cut its IREN price target to $55 from $65 on Nov. 9 but kept an overweight rating, citing rising contract prices. Jones upgraded IREN to buy on Nov. 6 with a $40 target, saying demand is still outrunning supply. That's still more than double where IREN is priced at today.

Meanwhile, Goldman Sachs trimmed its CoreWeave target to $101 from $106 but kept a buy rating, also more than double current prices, and Citi named the firm one of its top software picks, pointing to "signs of strong AI demand and nicer valuations."

The bull case here is that the market is punishing delays that will soon pass and that these stocks now trade far below their contract values. The bear case is that this is what the early stage of a tech bust looks like. In 2000, companies with real revenue kept posting strong results for months even as their stocks fell because orders often take time to unwind.

The neoclouds aren't the only AI stocks down sharply, either. Oracle (ORCL -0.52%), which also borrowed heavily to build data centers, is down over 60% from its June high.

For investors, the takeaway is to watch debt and deadlines more than revenue growth. If IREN hits its $4 billion target by February and CoreWeave keeps raising money on good terms, today's prices could look like a bargain. If the delays keep stacking up, the sell-off may not be finished.
