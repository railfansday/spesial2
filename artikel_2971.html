Anthropic Was Worth $111.50 on Its First Day of Trading. Is That a Problem for Investors?

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Anthropic opened trading today at 34% above its IPO price. A great company can still be a bad buy if you pay too much, and Warren Buffett warned about exactly that.

By Patrick Sanders – Nov 19, 2026 at 2:39PM EST

10 stocks we like better than Anthropic ›

Investors who bought Anthropic (ANT +15.40%) stock when it began trading just before noon on Nov. 19 ended up paying much more than investors who got shares in the record-setting initial public offering (IPO).

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 per share, but the stock opened at $111.50 -- a whopping 34.3% premium. Let's put that into real dollar terms -- someone who bought 100 shares at the IPO price would have paid $8,300. But if you didn’t qualify to be one of those early investors and had placed an order to buy shares as soon as the company hit the open market, if you got that order filled, your shares would have cost $11,150.

That was an extra $2,850 for the same ownership stake.

Anthropic didn't suddenly become a better company overnight. Its financial results didn't change, and neither did its business prospects. The only thing that changed was the price that investors were willing to pay.

So did investors get carried away and buy a great company at a bad price? Already, it looks like some did. Shares began to retreat almost immediately, and by 2:15 p.m. ET, the stock was trading below $95 a share. That’s still comfortably above the IPO price, but more than 17% below the peak of near $115 it reached in its first few minutes.

Warren Buffett addressed the mis-valuation issue in Berkshire Hathaway's (BRKA -0.85%) (BRKB -0.69%) 1982 annual letter to shareholders.

"For the investor, a too-high purchase price for the stock of an excellent company can undo the effects of a subsequent decade of favorable business developments," Buffett wrote.

That's a particularly relevant observation for Anthropic.

There's plenty to like about the company behind the Claude artificial intelligence (AI) models. Anthropic reported $17 billion in revenue during the third quarter, representing extraordinary growth from a year earlier.

The company also has the financial backing of some of the biggest names in technology. Amazon (AMZN -3.92%) and Alphabet (GOOG -2.23%) (GOOGL -2.66%) are major shareholders, while Nvidia (NVDA -2.38%), Advanced Micro Devices (AMD -9.13%), and several other technology companies committed billions to its shares through private placement deals accompanying the IPO.

Those investments reflect substantial interest in Anthropic's future.

But here's something investors should understand. A company can deliver years of outstanding revenue growth without necessarily producing outstanding stock returns.

When investors pay a premium for a stock, they're effectively paying in advance for some of the company's hoped-for future success. And the higher that premium climbs, the harder it becomes for the business to deliver results that justify the price.

Warren Buffett's investing lessons from more than 40 years ago still hold true today. Image source: Getty Images.

That's the problem with buying into the excitement surrounding an IPO. Investors can end up paying tomorrow's price for today's business.

Anthropic's IPO prospectus disclosed that it had taken on spending commitments for compute infrastructure and other needs to the tune of approximately $900.9 billion over a period that extends through 2045.

That's an extraordinary amount of money, even for a company competing in the rapidly growing AI market.

Some of its biggest commitments involve its deals to lease cloud computing capacity from Amazon, Alphabet, and Microsoft. Anthropic also has arrangements involving equipment supplied by Broadcom (AVGO -2.44%), AMD, and Nvidia.

These investments could help Anthropic expand its computing capacity and serve more customers. But the scale of its expenses can't be ignored. Anthropic will need to generate enough revenue — and, eventually, cash flow — to support its spending.

Anthropic's third-quarter revenue figure signals that the company can make money, but it is still in the growth stage. And investors are assigning considerable value to growth that hasn't happened yet.

Anthropic could grow rapidly enough to justify that premium, or even more. But the early jump in stock price made the investment proposition less attractive than it was at $83. And I think the market realizes that, as shares slid to less than $100 after the first hour of trading, and less than $95 by mid-afternoon.

The lesson that Buffett imparted more than four decades ago holds true today. Anthropic might turn out to be an exceptional company, and it could dominate important parts of the AI market and generate enormous revenue for years.

But today, the market has been charging a premium for its stock — one that may take a meaningful toll on long-term investors’ returns. And that's what investors should be thinking about before chasing Anthropic shares in these early days of trading.
