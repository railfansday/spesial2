FAA ground stops explained: who orders them, why, and how rarely the whole country stops

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

An FAA ground stop holds flights at their departure airports, and these 10 points explain who orders one, why it happens and how rarely the whole country stops

Most ground stops never make the news. A line of thunderstorms parks over one hub, or fog settles over a runway, and departures bound for that airport wait at their gates until conditions improve. A halt that reaches the whole country is a different matter. Two cases are widely cited. One is the airspace shutdown after the September 11, 2001 attacks. The other is the nationwide ground stop during the January 2023 outage of an FAA notice system.

An FAA ground stop is a traffic management order from the Federal Aviation Administration. It keeps flights headed for a specific airport or area on the ground at their departure points. Planes already in the air keep flying toward their destinations. The order is usually brief. It is one of several tools the agency uses to keep arrivals in line with what an airport can handle.

For travelers, the term tends to show up in an airline app notification or on a departure board without much explanation. The term has a specific meaning. A ground stop is not a cancellation. It comes with an expected end time, and the FAA can extend it or lift it early. Delays can still ripple outward after a stop ends, because aircraft and crews end up out of position.

This guide covers 10 points. It explains how a ground stop differs from a ground delay program, which spaces out departures instead of halting them. It identifies the FAA facility in Warrenton, Virginia, that coordinates these orders nationwide. It walks through the common triggers, from weather and runway closures to equipment failures and security events. It also covers stops that airlines request for their own flights, such as those during the July 19, 2024 CrowdStrike $CRWD-related disruption.

The final slides turn to the national scale. On September 11, 2001, the FAA ordered every civil aircraft in U.S. airspace to land and barred new takeoffs. That was a full airspace closure, which went further than a ground stop. On January 11, 2023, an outage of the Notice to Air Missions system led the FAA to halt domestic departures nationwide. Flights resumed later that morning. It was widely reported as the first nationwide ground stop of its kind since 2001. Both events stand apart from the local stops that make up nearly all ground stops.

An FAA ground stop is a traffic management order that keeps flights bound for a particular airport, or a particular area, on the ground at their departure airports. Planes already in the air are not part of the hold.

The name describes where the waiting happens. The hold applies at departure points, not at the destination. New flights toward the affected airport stay at the gate until the order ends.

That is why the effect often shows up far from the problem. A traveler in Denver can wait at the gate because of a hold on flights bound for Atlanta. Nothing needs to be wrong in Denver. The restriction follows where the flight is going, not where it starts.

A large hub draws flights from many cities. A hold on that one destination can therefore reach departure gates at airports across the country at the same time. Each of those airports may have clear skies and open runways.

The order can be narrow or wide. A hold on one airport affects only flights headed there. A hold on an area reaches flights bound for that whole region. Flights from the same origin to other destinations can still leave.

A ground stop halts departures to a constrained airport entirely, usually for a short period. A ground delay program is the milder tool. It keeps flights moving but spaces out their departure times. Fewer planes then reach the strained airport at once.

Both measures belong to the Federal Aviation Administration's traffic management toolkit. Both work by keeping delay on the ground at the departure airport. The difference lies in how much traffic each one lets through.

Under a ground delay program, flights still leave. Each affected flight receives an assigned departure slot, known as an expect departure clearance time, or EDCT. Airlines and pilots plan around that time rather than the original schedule. The result is a steady, slower flow of arrivals instead of a sudden pile-up.

Thunderstorms, low visibility, runway closures, equipment failures, security events and sudden traffic surges are the usual reasons the FAA orders a ground stop. Each one leaves an airport able to accept fewer flights than are headed its way. Most affect a single airport or region.

Thunderstorms are a familiar trigger during the U.S. summer travel season. Pilots route around storm cells rather than fly through them. A line of storms can block the arrival paths into a major hub even when the skies over its runways are clear. Lightning close to an airport can also force ground crews off the ramp. That halts fueling, loading and towing until the threat passes.

Low visibility works differently. Fog and low clouds push pilots onto instrument approaches. Controllers then have to keep more distance between arriving aircraft. That lowers the number of landings an airport can handle each hour. San Francisco International Airport is a well-known example. Its closely spaced parallel runways cannot support side-by-side visual approaches when clouds roll in. Arrival capacity drops as a result.

An airline can ask the FAA for a ground stop that covers only its own flights. Carriers make this request when an internal problem, such as an IT failure, keeps them from running their operation normally. The FAA issues the stop, but the request comes from the airline.

The trigger in these cases sits inside one company. It does not come from the weather, the runway or the air traffic control system. Skies can be clear and the airport fully open while a single carrier's planes stay parked.

That makes the reach of the stop narrow. The requesting airline's departures are held at their gates. Other airlines at the same airports can keep flying, because nothing about the airspace itself has changed. One carrier's departure board can sit frozen while a rival's flights leave on schedule.

On September 11, 2001, the FAA directed every civil aircraft flying in U.S. airspace to land and prohibited new takeoffs. The order closed the national airspace outright. It cleared planes from the sky instead of only holding departures at their gates.

That is the key difference from an ordinary ground stop. Under a routine stop, flights already in the air continue to their destinations. The September 11 order made no such exception. Aircraft aloft had to put down at the nearest suitable airport, regardless of where they had been scheduled to go.

The order applied to airliners, cargo carriers and private planes alike. Air traffic controllers across the country guided each flight to a runway. Many crews landed at airports they had never planned to use.

Nationwide ground stops are rare because the tool is normally aimed at one airport or region, not the whole country. Only two national cases are widely cited: the shutdown after the September 11, 2001 attacks and the January 2023 NOTAM outage.

The typical ground stop answers a local problem. Thunderstorms over one hub, fog at a coastal airport or a closed runway cut capacity in a single place. Flights headed anywhere else have no reason to wait.

That local scope is the point of the tool. Holding departures bound for one constrained airport eases pressure there and leaves the rest of the system running. A stop covering every departure in the country would also hold flights between airports with no problems at all.

For a national stop to make sense, the problem has to reach every flight at once. Weather rarely does that. Even a large storm system affects some regions and leaves others clear.

An aircraft that took off before the order continues its trip. It still lands at the affected airport or area. The ground stop limits new arrivals. It does not recall the ones already on the way.

Keeping the wait on the ground is the purpose of the tool. A plane parked at a gate burns no fuel circling in a holding pattern. It also adds no traffic to crowded airspace near the destination.

The program is built around an airport's arrival capacity. When demand for landings is expected to exceed what the airport can handle, the FAA lowers the number of arrivals it will accept per hour. Departure times at other airports are then adjusted to match that lower rate.

A ground stop is blunter. Flights bound for the affected airport or area do not leave at all until the stop is lifted or modified. Nothing is metered. The flow simply pauses.

That bluntness is why the ground stop tends to be the short-term option. It suits situations where conditions at the destination change faster than a metering plan can be set up. A ground delay program fits problems that can be forecast and managed over a longer stretch.

The two tools can work in sequence. A ground stop can buy time while traffic managers assess a problem. Once they know how much capacity remains, they can replace the stop with a ground delay program. That program then releases flights at a pace the airport can absorb.

Keeping delay at the gate rather than in the air serves a practical purpose. An aircraft waiting at its departure airport burns far less fuel than one circling in a holding pattern. It also keeps the airspace near the destination from becoming crowded.

For passengers, the distinction shows up on the departure board. A ground delay program usually gives a flight a specific new departure time tied to its assigned slot. A ground stop holds the flight with no assigned slot until the stop lifts or is converted to a slower release of traffic.

Ground stops are issued through the FAA's traffic flow management system. The agency's Air Traffic Control System Command Center in Warrenton, Virginia, coordinates each one with air traffic facilities across the country. Airlines are then notified and hold their flights.

The Command Center sits within the FAA's Air Traffic Organization, the branch that runs day-to-day air traffic control. Its job is national rather than local. Controllers at a single tower or radar facility manage the traffic in their own airspace. The Command Center looks at demand and capacity across the whole National Airspace System at once. It operates around the clock.

That wider view is what a ground stop requires. A hold on one destination affects flights waiting at many other airports, some of them hundreds of miles away. Those departure airports fall under different FAA facilities. One office has to make sure each of them applies the same order at the same time.

The process usually starts close to the problem. Traffic management units work inside the FAA's larger facilities, including the air route traffic control centers that handle high-altitude traffic. When a facility expects more arrivals than an airport can accept, its traffic managers raise the issue with the Command Center. National specialists then weigh the request against conditions elsewhere in the system.

Setting the scope is part of that decision. A ground stop can cover only departures from nearby airports, or it can reach airports much farther away. The choice determines how many flights stay at the gate.

Once a stop is set, it goes out as a formal advisory. The advisory names the affected destination and the departure airports covered. These advisories are public. Dispatchers, pilots and travelers can all read them.

Airlines take part in the process. The FAA and carriers share data and hold regular planning calls under a framework known as collaborative decision making. Airline operations centers learn of a stop quickly and keep the affected flights from leaving the gate.

Runway closures shrink capacity in a more direct way. A runway may close for snow removal, pavement repairs or a disabled aircraft. An airport that loses one of its runways can land fewer planes. A ground stop keeps inbound flights from arriving faster than the remaining runways can take them.

Equipment failures cover problems with the tools air traffic control depends on. These include radar, radio frequencies and the computer systems inside a control facility. Controllers who lose a tool they need to keep aircraft safely separated reduce the traffic they handle. That reduced flow lasts until the tool is restored.

Security events include threats against an airport and breaches of a secure area. A ground stop holds inbound flights while authorities respond. It avoids sending more aircraft and passengers into an airport where operations are on hold.

Traffic surges happen when more flights head toward an airport than it can absorb. This can follow an earlier disruption, when a backlog of delayed departures is released toward the same hub at once.

A ground stop for storms in one city holds only the flights headed there. Flights between airports outside the affected area keep operating on their normal schedules.

Every FAA ground stop is issued with an expected end time, and many last under a few hours. The FAA can extend a stop or lift it early.

The disruption often outlasts the stop itself. Aircraft and crews end up out of position, and putting them back takes time.

The expected end time is a planning estimate, not a promise. It reflects how long traffic managers expect the problem at the destination to last. If a line of thunderstorms clears sooner than forecast, held flights can be released ahead of schedule. If conditions stay poor, the stop can be extended, sometimes more than once.

A posted end time is therefore not the same as a departure time. Flights do not all leave the moment a stop lifts. Held aircraft still need to taxi, line up for takeoff and fit into the arrival flow at their destination.

A ground stop does not always end cleanly, either. The FAA sometimes follows one with a ground delay program. That lets departures resume but keeps them spaced out while the affected airport works through its backlog.

The longer tail comes from the way airlines build their schedules. In hub-and-spoke networks, a single aircraft typically flies several legs in one day. When a morning flight sits at the gate, the same plane arrives late for its next trip. The trip after that slips too. Delay spreads across the network, often to airports far from the one that triggered the stop.

Crews add a second constraint. Federal rules for passenger airline pilots, known as Part 117, cap how long pilots can remain on duty. A crew held during a ground stop can run out of legal duty time before the flight is cleared. If no replacement crew is nearby, the airline may cancel the flight rather than operate it.

Cancellations made during a stop leave aircraft and crews in the wrong cities. Airlines then have to reposition them. That work can push the disruption into the following day.

The length of a ground stop and the length of the disruption it causes are two different measures. The second depends on how fast an airline can get its planes and people back where its schedule needs them.

The July 19, 2024 disruption linked to CrowdStrike $CRWD is a widely covered example. A faulty software update from the cybersecurity company crashed computers running Microsoft $MSFT Windows around the world. Several U.S. carriers ended up under ground stops that day.

Each of those stops applied to a single carrier. None was a nationwide FAA ground stop. That distinction is easy to miss when several large airlines halt departures on the same day. The combined effect can look like a national shutdown. In practice, each hold was tied to one airline's own systems rather than to the national airspace.

The two kinds of stop also differ in what determines when they end. A carrier-specific hold lasts until that airline can restore its own operation. An FAA-ordered nationwide stop lasts until the agency resolves a problem that affects every airline, such as the January 2023 failure of its Notice to Air Missions system.

For travelers, the practical question is which kind of stop is in place. If only one airline is held, flights on other carriers may still be operating from the same airport.

Check two places first: your airline's app for rebooking options, and the FAA's airport status page for the ground stop's expected end time. Together they help a traveler decide whether to wait for the original flight or switch to another one.

A ground stop holds departing flights at their origin airports. Passengers wait on the ground, at the gate or in the terminal, rather than in the air.

The cause of a hold is often far from where the traveler is sitting. Ground stops apply to flights bound for a specific airport or area. A passenger departing in clear weather can still be held because of conditions at the destination.

The FAA's airport status page lists active ground stops alongside the time each is expected to end. That estimate can move in either direction while passengers wait. Rechecking the page shows whether a stop has been extended or lifted early.

Travelers $TRV who stay near the gate are ready if a stop lifts early. A held flight can return to the departure line with little warning.

The airline app can show changes to a flight's status and the rebooking options the carrier makes available. Those options come from the airline, not the FAA.

What a traveler is owed depends on the carrier. Refund and rebooking rules vary from airline to airline. The carrier's own policy shows which options apply to a specific ticket.

Disruption often outlasts the stop itself. When departures resume, aircraft and crews may be out of position. Delays and cancellations can continue after the hold ends.

Travelers with connections should track every leg of the trip. A late first flight can affect the next one even after the original hold is lifted.

Flights already in the air are not covered by a ground stop. Someone collecting an arriving passenger can check whether that flight departed before the hold began.

Ben Sliney, the FAA's national operations manager, is widely credited with giving the order to clear the airspace. He was working at the agency's Air Traffic Control System Command Center, which was then located in Herndon, Virginia. September 11 was his first day in that job.

The closure came in two stages. The 9/11 Commission Report describes the Command Center first halting civilian takeoffs nationwide, then ordering aircraft already aloft to land. In that sequence, a national ground stop was the opening move toward a complete shutdown.

Flights bound for the U.S. from overseas were turned away too. Many transatlantic flights diverted to airports in Canada. Gander, Newfoundland, took in a large share of them. Residents there housed and fed stranded passengers until their flights could continue.

The shutdown covered civil flying only. Military, law enforcement and emergency aircraft kept operating while commercial traffic stayed on the ground.

Limited commercial service resumed on September 13, 2001. The restart was gradual rather than a single reopening of the whole system. Some categories of flying, including much of private general aviation, remained restricted for longer than scheduled airline service.

On January 11, 2023, the FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop of domestic departures after its Notice to Air Missions system failed. News coverage at the time widely called it the first nationwide ground stop of its kind since 2001.

The Notice to Air Missions system, known as NOTAM, distributes official notices about conditions that can affect a flight. Common examples include a closed runway, an out-of-service navigation aid and obstruction lights that are not working. The notices were called Notices to Airmen until the FAA renamed them in 2021.

Pilots and airline dispatchers review the notices that apply to a flight's route, departure airport and destination before takeoff. They are a standard part of preflight planning. When the system stopped working, that planning step lost its official source of current hazard information.

The FAA responded by halting domestic departures across the country. The order applied to flights waiting to take off. Aircraft already in the air continued to their destinations.

That made the 2023 order narrower than the September 11, 2001 shutdown, which closed U.S. airspace to civil flights entirely. In 2023, the airspace stayed open and only new departures waited.

The FAA later explained what went wrong. It said a contractor had damaged a database file while working to synchronize the primary and backup databases. The agency said it found no evidence of a cyberattack.

That explanation traced the outage to an error during database work rather than to an outside intrusion. A single damaged file in one national system was enough to hold domestic departures nationwide.

Flights resumed the same morning. Most ground stops respond to weather, runway problems or congestion at one airport. This one began with a failure in a national information system that domestic flights depend on. Its outage affected departures at airports across the country at the same time.

The two widely cited cases met that bar in different ways. One was a national security emergency. The other was the failure of a single system that pilots across the country depend on.

They also differed in scope. In 2001, the order reached aircraft already in the air, directing every civil flight in U.S. airspace to land. A ground stop, by contrast, applies only to flights still at their departure airports. The 2023 action halted domestic departures and did not order airborne planes down.

More than two decades passed between the two events. Local ground stops kept occurring throughout that span, at individual airports and in specific regions.

Large disruptions do not automatically become national stops either. The July 19, 2024 CrowdStrike $CRWD-related outage produced ground stops at several U.S. carriers. Each one covered a single airline's flights, not all departures.

Accounts of nationwide stops consistently point to the 2001 and 2023 events. That consistency, rather than a precise historical tally, is the basis for calling them rare.
