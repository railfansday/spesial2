10 natural hot springs in the U.S. to visit this winter

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 natural hot springs in the U.S. to visit in winter range from aurora viewing in Alaska to historic bathhouses in Arkansas and snowbound pools in Wyoming

Geothermal water keeps flowing when everything around it freezes. Heat from deep underground still reaches the surface in the coldest months of the year. That contrast is what draws many travelers to winter hot springs. Steam lifts off the water and snow collects on the rocks along the edge. The air can sit well below freezing while soakers stay warm from the shoulders down.

Hot springs form when groundwater circulates deep enough to be heated, then rises back to the surface through faults and fractures. The heaviest concentration in the U.S. is in the West, where volcanic and tectonic activity brings that heat closer to the surface. Thermal springs also appear far from the Rockies. Central Arkansas has enough of them to support a national park.

This list covers 10 natural hot springs in the U.S. to visit in winter. It is a selection, not a ranking. The entries show how much a winter soak can vary from one place to the next. Some are geothermal pools at a hot springs resort, with lodging and meals close by. At the other end are free, primitive pools on public land, with few or no facilities and no fee.

The range stretches from Interior Alaska to central Arkansas and Northern California. Colorado contributes a public pool in a mountain town and rock-lined soaks in the woods. California's Eastern Sierra has primitive pools on public land. Wyoming has a pool reached in winter by snowmobile or dog sled, plus a free state park bathhouse. Montana adds a historic resort, and Idaho adds a town built around its mineral pools. Napa Valley closes the list with mud baths. Travelers $TRV with time can link several of the Rocky Mountain entries into one hot springs road trip.

Winter adds work to any visit. Snow and ice can slow travel on mountain roads. Remote soaking pools may take extra time and gear to reach. Access, hours and rules also change from season to season. Confirm current details directly with each site before traveling.

Chena Hot Springs is a geothermal resort about 60 miles northeast of Fairbanks, Alaska. Winter visitors can soak in naturally heated water there and watch for the aurora borealis overhead.

The resort sits at the end of Chena Hot Springs Road, which runs east from the Fairbanks area. Much of the route passes through the Chena River State Recreation Area, a large stretch of state-managed forest and river valley. Fairbanks International Airport is the usual gateway for travelers flying in from elsewhere in the U.S.

People have soaked at the springs for more than a century. Gold miners Robert and Thomas Swan found them in 1905. The site later developed into a soaking destination for Fairbanks residents.

Winter is aurora season at Chena. Fairbanks lies beneath the auroral oval, the ring-shaped zone around Earth's geomagnetic pole where auroras appear most often. Long winter nights in Interior Alaska leave more hours of darkness to watch the sky. Near the December solstice, Fairbanks gets less than four hours of daylight. The resort's distance from city lights also means a darker sky. Displays still depend on solar activity and clear weather. No single night is guaranteed.

Winter air in Interior Alaska can drop to minus 40 degrees Fahrenheit, the point where the Fahrenheit and Celsius scales meet. In air that cold, thick steam rises off the outdoor pool. Frost can form on wet hair and eyebrows while the rest of the body stays submerged in warm water.

The hot water also generates electricity. Chena runs a geothermal power plant fed by the springs. That water is cooler than the water most geothermal plants rely on. The plant uses a binary-cycle system. It passes the water's heat to a second fluid with a lower boiling point, and that fluid's vapor spins a turbine.

Geothermal energy also keeps the Aurora Ice Museum frozen year-round. The building is made from ice and snow. Geothermal heat warms the resort's greenhouses as well. Those greenhouses grow produce in a region with a short outdoor growing season.

Hot Springs National Park in central Arkansas is a national park built around thermal springs. Visitors soak along Bathhouse Row, a line of historic bathhouses in downtown Hot Springs. Some of those buildings still operate commercially and sell thermal baths inside a federal park.

The site's federal protection predates the national park system itself. Congress set the springs aside as a federal reservation 40 years before Yellowstone became the first national park in 1872. The reservation was later redesignated a national park.

The springs emerge along the lower slope of Hot Springs Mountain, part of the Ouachita Mountains. The water starts as rainfall that seeps deep underground. There it is heated before rising back to the surface through faults in the rock.

Visitors do not soak where the water surfaces. The water is collected and piped to the bathhouses instead. A soak here takes place indoors, in a tub or a pool, inside a historic building.

Bathhouse Row holds eight bathhouses built between the 1890s and the 1920s. The row was designated a National Historic Landmark District in 1987. Buckstaff Bathhouse has operated since 1912 and offers a traditional bathing routine. Quapaw Baths & Spa offers communal thermal pools as well as private baths.

Other buildings on the row serve different purposes. Fordyce Bathhouse, the largest bathhouse on the row, is the park's visitor center and museum. Its restored bathing rooms show how the spa era worked. Superior Bathhouse now houses a brewery that uses thermal spring water in its beer.

Behind the bathhouses runs the Grand Promenade, a brick walkway along the hillside. Public fountains around the park dispense thermal water at no charge. Residents regularly fill containers there.

Winter suits this park for a practical reason. The bathing takes place indoors, and cold weather or rain does not interrupt a soak. Central Arkansas also has a milder winter climate than the mountain hot springs of the Rockies and Alaska.

Ouray Hot Springs is a public pool fed by natural hot springs in Ouray, Colorado, a mountain town nicknamed the Switzerland of America. The town sits in the San Juan Mountains of southwestern Colorado. In winter, snow-covered peaks rise on nearly every side of it.

The nickname refers to the alpine scenery. Ouray fills a narrow valley along the Uncompahgre River, with steep rock walls and high summits rising directly above its streets. Those slopes hold snow through the cold months, and the pool sits on the valley floor below them.

The pool is run as a public facility rather than a private resort. Day visitors can soak without booking a room in town.

The San Juans are a largely volcanic range. Hot springs surface in several towns across the region, including Pagosa Springs to the southeast. Ouray's pool puts that underground heat in the middle of a town.

The town takes its name from Ouray, a 19th-century leader of the Ute people, whose homeland included this stretch of the Rockies. The settlement grew in the 1870s as a mining camp, after prospectors arrived in search of silver and gold. Brick and stone buildings from the mining era still stand along its main street. Ouray is also the seat of Ouray County.

Winter brings a second draw. The Ouray Ice Park, in the Uncompahgre Gorge at the edge of town, is built by spraying water onto the canyon walls until it freezes into climbing routes. The park hosts the Ouray Ice Festival each winter. A cold-weather trip can pair the two activities: a day on frozen gorge walls, followed by an evening in geothermal water.

Box Canyon Falls, a waterfall that drops through a narrow slot canyon on the south side of town, is a short trip from the pool.

Ouray sits on U.S. Route 550. Travelers $TRV arriving from the north follow the Uncompahgre Valley through Montrose and Ridgway. The nearest airport with regular airline service is Montrose Regional Airport. South of town, the same highway becomes the Million Dollar Highway, a mountain road that climbs over Red Mountain Pass toward Silverton.

Strawberry Park Hot Springs is a set of rock-lined natural pools in a wooded setting outside Steamboat Springs, Colorado. In winter, snow collects on the surrounding trees and boulders while steam rises off the water.

The springs sit in Strawberry Park, a valley north of downtown Steamboat Springs. Geothermal water rises from the ground and feeds a series of stone-walled soaking pools beside Hot Springs Creek. Cold creek water mixes with the hot flow. As a result, the temperature differs from one pool to the next.

The layout is closer to a natural spring than a municipal pool. Rock rather than poured concrete shapes the basins, and forest surrounds the soaking area.

Overnight stays are possible on the property. Along with a small number of rustic cabins, the springs rent out covered wagons and a former railroad caboose as lodging.

Steamboat Springs is a ski town in the Yampa Valley of northwest Colorado and the seat of Routt County. Steamboat Resort runs its lifts on Mount Werner at the edge of town. The area markets its light, dry snow under the trademarked name Champagne Powder.

Skiing in town predates the resort by decades. Howelsen Hill, a ski area near downtown, opened in 1915 and is named for Carl Howelsen, a Norwegian ski jumper who helped popularize the sport in the area. Steamboat Springs' Winter Carnival dates to 1914.

The town's name traces to trappers who heard a mineral spring's chugging sound along the Yampa River and likened it to a steamboat engine. Old Town Hot Springs, a separate soaking facility, operates in downtown Steamboat Springs.

Yampa Valley Regional Airport in Hayden, west of Steamboat Springs, handles commercial flights into the area. Strawberry Park is reached by road from town. Winter travel to the springs takes more planning than a trip to a downtown pool.

Travertine Hot Springs is a set of free, primitive soaking pools on public land just outside Bridgeport, in California's Eastern Sierra. There is no resort, admission desk or attendant. The site has only shallow pools of geothermal water set below the eastern wall of the Sierra Nevada.

The springs are named for the rock they have built. Travertine is a form of limestone made of calcium carbonate. Hot groundwater dissolves minerals as it moves underground. When that water reaches the surface and cools, carbon dioxide escapes. The dissolved calcium carbonate then settles out as solid rock. Over long periods, those deposits pile up into the pale mounds and ridges that surround the pools here.

The same material has a long history as a building stone. Much of the Colosseum in Rome is built from travertine. The Getty Center in Los Angeles is clad in travertine quarried in Italy. At Bridgeport, the stone is still forming in place, layer by layer, around the water that visitors soak in.

Bridgeport is a small town on U.S. Route 395 and the county seat of Mono County. The highway runs along the eastern side of the Sierra Nevada. The town sits in a broad, high valley below the range. Winter brings snow to the surrounding peaks, and the pools look out on those white ridgelines.

Travelers $TRV continuing south on 395 can see related geology at Mono Lake. The tufa towers that rise from the lake and its shoreline are also calcium carbonate. They formed where calcium-rich spring water mixed with the lake's alkaline water.

The pools sit on undeveloped public land. Their condition depends on the people who use them. The Leave No Trace Center for Outdoor Ethics sets out principles that apply directly here. They include disposing of waste properly, leaving what you find and being considerate of other visitors.

At a hot spring, those principles mean keeping soap and shampoo out of the water and keeping glass containers away from the pools. They also mean not moving rocks to dam or enlarge a soaking basin. The pools are small. Sharing space and keeping noise down leaves room for more people on busy days.

Granite Hot Springs is a developed soaking pool fed by natural springs in the Bridger-Teton National Forest near Jackson, Wyoming. In winter, visitors cross the snow-covered road to it by snowmobile, dog sled or another form of over-snow travel instead of by car.

The pool sits at the end of that road. A winter soak here is the final stop on a backcountry outing. Some visitors make the run on their own snowmobiles. Others book a guided snowmobile or dog sled trip with an outfitter based in the Jackson area.

The pool is a built structure, not a rock-ringed natural basin. Civilian Conservation Corps crews constructed it in 1933. That New Deal program put young men to work on forests, parks and other public lands across the U.S. during the Great Depression. Spring water feeds the concrete pool. Its walls give Granite a more defined shape than the primitive soaking spots found elsewhere in the West.

The spring lies along Granite Creek, which drains the Gros Ventre Range. The turnoff to the creek road is in Hoback Canyon, on U.S. Highway 191 southeast of Jackson. In summer, the same road is open to cars, and the pool serves campers and hikers as well as soakers.

Bridger-Teton borders much of the Jackson Hole valley, and Grand Teton National Park lies to the north of town. The U.S. Forest Service manages the national forest for several uses, including recreation, grazing and timber. Granite Hot Springs is a developed recreation site on that federal land. It is not a full-service resort with a hotel attached.

Jackson is known in winter as a ski town. Its ski areas include Jackson Hole Mountain Resort in Teton Village and Snow King Mountain on the edge of town. A trip over snow to Granite's spring-fed pool puts the same season to a different use. It centers on travel through a national forest rather than on lift-served runs.

Chico Hot Springs is a resort in Montana's Paradise Valley, north of Yellowstone National Park, with outdoor pools fed by natural hot springs. It opened to guests in 1900.

Paradise Valley is the stretch of the Yellowstone River valley between Livingston and Gardiner. The Absaroka Range rises along its eastern edge, and the Gallatin Range lines the west. U.S. Route 89 runs the length of the valley and links the two towns with the small communities in between.

The river shapes the valley. The Yellowstone is the longest undammed river in the contiguous U.S. It flows north out of the national park and through Paradise Valley. Near Livingston, it turns east and heads across the plains.

Chico's pools sit outdoors. In winter, soakers sit in spring-fed water while the valley air is cold and snow covers the Absaroka peaks above. The resort also has lodging and a restaurant. A visit can be an overnight stay rather than a quick stop on a drive.

Chico predates the federal agency that now manages Yellowstone. The resort was taking guests 16 years before Congress created the National Park Service in 1916.

Gardiner, at the valley's southern end, is home to Yellowstone's North Entrance. Theodore Roosevelt laid the cornerstone of the stone Roosevelt Arch there in 1903. In winter, the road from Gardiner through Mammoth Hot Springs to Cooke City is the only road in the park open to regular wheeled vehicles.

Inside the park, the thermal areas follow different rules. Yellowstone's hot springs and geysers can scald, and the ground around them can be a thin crust over hot water. Visitors must stay on boardwalks and designated trails in those areas. Developed spring-fed pools outside the park, such as Chico's, are the ones built for soaking.

Bozeman Yellowstone International Airport is the main commercial airport serving the region. From there, the drive to Paradise Valley crosses Bozeman Pass on Interstate 90 to Livingston, then heads south on U.S. Route 89 into the valley.

Lava Hot Springs is a small town in southeastern Idaho built around natural hot mineral pools. Its main soaking complex belongs to the state of Idaho rather than to a private resort company.

The town sits in Bannock County along the Portneuf River, southeast of Pocatello. U.S. Route 30 passes through it. Interstate 15, the highway linking Pocatello with Salt Lake City to the south and Idaho Falls to the north, is the nearest interstate.

State ownership of the springs dates to 1902, when the federal government transferred the hot springs land to Idaho. Today the pools are managed by the Lava Hot Springs Foundation, a state agency, rather than by a hotel group or spa chain. The main complex is open to the public and is not reserved for overnight guests.

Long before the town existed, the region was home to the Shoshone and Bannock peoples. Bannock County takes its name from the Bannock. The Fort Hall Indian Reservation, home of the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, lies to the northwest near Pocatello.

The town's name reflects its setting. Dark volcanic rock is exposed along the Portneuf canyon. The Portneuf River flows through the middle of town. The water that feeds the pools is geothermal, heated underground before it rises to the surface.

The soaking area is a group of outdoor pools kept at different temperatures rather than a single basin. Bathers can move from hotter water to cooler water at their own pace.

The springs shape the rest of the town's calendar too. In summer, visitors float the Portneuf River on inner tubes, and a separate swimming complex opens for the warm season. Some inns and lodges in town offer private soaking tubs filled with spring water.

For winter visitors, the hot pools remain the town's central attraction once the summer tubing and swimming season ends.

Hot Springs State Park in Thermopolis, Wyoming, operates a free public bathhouse fed by the park's mineral springs. Anyone can walk in and soak without paying. Each visit is subject to a time limit set by the park.

That free access is written into the park's history. In 1896, the Eastern Shoshone and Northern Arapaho tribes ceded the land around the springs to the federal government. The agreement required that part of the hot water remain free for public use.

The land later passed to Wyoming and became the state's first state park. The State Bath House is how that term of the agreement is honored today. Thermopolis commemorates the 1896 deal with an annual pageant called the Gift of the Waters.

Park materials describe the Big Spring, the main source of the park's water, as one of the largest mineral hot springs in the world. The water carries a heavy load of dissolved minerals. A sulfur smell hangs over parts of the grounds.

As the water flows downhill toward the Bighorn River, it leaves mineral deposits behind. Over time those deposits have built the Rainbow Terraces, a series of travertine steps streaked orange, yellow and green by heat-loving microorganisms. Boardwalks keep visitors off the fragile surface.

A suspension footbridge crosses the Bighorn River inside the park and offers a view of the terraces from the far bank. The park also keeps a small herd of bison, which visitors can see from a loop road on the grounds.

The free bathhouse is not the only place in the park to soak. Two privately run facilities on the grounds charge admission for larger pools and water slides.

In winter, cold air turns the heat coming off the terraces and pools into heavy plumes of steam. Thermopolis sits along U.S. Highway 20 at the northern end of the Wind River Canyon. The springs flow year-round.

The town is also home to the Wyoming Dinosaur Center, a museum with an active dig site in the surrounding hills.

Calistoga, a small town at the northern end of California's Napa Valley, is known for geothermal mud baths and mineral water soaks. Its spas draw on hot water that rises from underground. That makes it a hot springs stop built around treatments and pools rather than backcountry soaking.

The town's geothermal activity is visible above ground at Old Faithful Geyser of California. This privately owned geyser sits just outside town and erupts periodically. It is not the Yellowstone geyser of the same name, and it is a place for watching, not soaking.

Calistoga's spa history reaches back to the 19th century. Samuel Brannan, a California pioneer and businessman, built a resort around the local springs. Town lore holds that he meant to promote it as the "Saratoga of California," after the spa town in New York. The phrase reportedly came out as "Calistoga of Sarafornia," and the first word became the town's name.

The mud bath is the treatment most closely tied to Calistoga. The mud is typically a mix of volcanic ash and heated mineral water, and some spas add peat. Guests settle into a tub of the warm mixture for a set period.

A rinse usually follows. Spas then commonly offer a mineral water bath, a steam room or a blanket wrap. The exact sequence differs from one spa to the next.

Some of the town's lodgings also run swimming pools filled with the same geothermal water. Several of the spas sit along Lincoln Avenue, the town's main street. The Calistoga name also appears on a sparkling mineral water brand that began with the local springs.

Winter is Napa Valley's rainy season. Conditions on the valley floor stay far milder than at mountain springs in the Rockies or Alaska, and snow rarely falls there. The grape harvest wraps up in the fall, and growers prune the dormant vines over the winter months.

State Route 29 and the Silverado Trail both run up the valley to Calistoga. The drive from the San Francisco Bay Area reaches the town without any snow-country travel.
