What the year-end board meeting can do for security before the next crisis hits

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A high-profile incident can put security at the top of a board’s agenda. Year-end meetings are a chance to keep it there.

This article has been reviewed by the Quartz editorial team. The content may be licensed for reuse.

In November, Nvidia $NVDA opened an independent investigation after reports that its CEO, Jensen Huang, may have been targeted by a foreign intelligence effort. At the time, the company told Reuters that its board had no reason to believe company secrets or U.S. national security had been compromised. The inquiry drew attention to executive security. For other boards, it offers a reason to look back at how security concerns have surfaced at other companies over the year and consider where their own businesses might be exposed.

A fraud case in Hong Kong showed how the apparent involvement of a senior executive can influence an employee’s decision. In 2024, Hong Kong authorities described how an employee approved transfers that cost a company about HK$200 million after a fabricated video meeting appeared to feature its chief financial officer. The case gives directors a concrete issue to consider at year-end. How much authority do employees assume a senior executive has to approve an unusual transaction?

Looking across cases can help directors decide which risks deserve a closer look. A board might review how the company’s public profile changed over the year, then examine which decisions still depend on one executive. It can use that review to decide which procedures staff should test before they have to use them under pressure.

Tim Crockett, co-founder of security advisory firm DGBI-USA, has seen how a crisis exercise can reveal where authority sits in practice. In a crisis exercise he helped run, participants acted on an urgent instruction they believed came from the founder and CEO. The simulation used AI to impersonate the executive, whose reputation for expecting quick action made the request seem plausible. The exercise raises a question for leadership. Would an employee feel comfortable asking for a second check if the request appeared to come from the CEO?

Crockett sees payment approvals as a practical place to start. “A large transfer can require a second sign-off, and several smaller payments may deserve the same scrutiny,” he says. “The same principle applies when an executive makes an exception to the rules for accessing sensitive information. Someone else should review the decision, and there should be a record of it.”

A year-end review can also bring communications, security, and operations teams together. Crockett suggests looking at what the company disclosed through fundraising announcements, executive appearances, and other publicity over the previous year. “Communications staff can identify what became public,” he says. “Security and operations teams can then assess whether those disclosures warrant changes to travel procedures or access to sensitive information. The review can also account for changes abroad.” Crockett sees value in asking how geopolitical developments might affect travel, suppliers, or business continuity in the markets the company relies on.

Before the year-end meeting closes, directors can ask management to identify which lessons from the company’s own experience and from outside incidents apply to the business. Each agreed action should have a person responsible, a deadline, and a way to show whether the change works in practice. Directors can then put those actions on the agenda for the next regular meeting and ask what changed, what an exercise revealed, and which decisions still need their attention.

Crockett says, “Before the next security incident makes headlines, boards should know which safeguards they’ve tested, what those tests revealed, and who’s responsible for closing the gaps.”
