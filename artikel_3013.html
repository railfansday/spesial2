Making VR Learning Reliable Enough To Scale

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Dzmitry Lubneuski is the CIO at a1qa, a leading pure-play software testing company. He’s a tech expert with a solid 20-year background in QA

​Imagine a class halfway through a virtual reality (VR) lesson when the learning application on a student’s headset stops working. While the instructor tries to fix it, the rest of the class waits. The student reconnects, but their progress is gone. The instructor now has to decide whether to repeat the activity or move on with an incomplete record.

VR lets learners repeat tasks and explore different choices in situations that would be difficult to re-create in person. But when a provider offers a course to schools or workplaces, the lessons need to run reliably on the devices and networks those customers use.

Quality assurance (QA) checks whether a course meets its functional, compatibility, performance and usability requirements. Testers identify faults under realistic conditions, developers resolve them and testers verify the fixes. Starting this work during course design gives teams time to address problems before customers encounter them.​

​QA covers the learner’s journey from device setup and sign-in through joining a session, using lesson controls, completing activities, saving progress and recording assessment results. Testers examine these functions across a representative range of supported headsets, controllers and hardware configurations, including demanding activities and recovery after interruptions. Compatibility with equipment customers already use makes adoption easier and expands the range of institutions the provider can serve.

Developers and testers use agreed rendering and response requirements to evaluate each supported model. Meta’s guidance for Quest developers explains that sustained missed frames produce judder and increased latency. The displayed content lags behind the user’s head movement, and that latency can contribute to discomfort.

Load tests increase the number of simultaneous learners, classes and instructors to establish how many the software supports before responses become too slow or sessions too unstable to meet agreed targets. A simulated connection loss shows what happens to the group while one learner reconnects and whether saved progress is restored. The test records an instructor’s time and support effort needed to restart sessions or recover missing work, helping providers identify delivery costs they can reduce.

An activity that begins in a browser may finish in a headset, with results viewed on a phone. Integration tests check that learner identity, progress and assessment results remain consistent across these handovers and reach the learning management system correctly. Errors in these transfers can leave staff correcting records and learners repeating completed work.​

Representative learners complete a full activity while observers note confusing controls and requests for help. When several participants ask the same question, observers flag the relevant instructions or controls for revision before they add to instructors’ workloads. Removing those obstacles can improve engagement and activity completion while reducing the time instructors spend answering questions. The review covers how easily participants find pause and exit controls and whether physical actions can be performed safely in the intended space.

When someone reports discomfort, testers review the application’s behavior and headset setup, taking account of session length. Meta’s locomotion guidance recommends customizable movement options and testing with diverse users because physical needs and comfort triggers differ.

Where a headset prevents someone from participating, a desktop simulation or instructor-supported activity may offer another route. Trials with learners show whether that route lets them practice and demonstrate the intended skill.

Some learners can’t follow audio instructions and may need captions or another format. Testers examine whether essential information reaches them and whether controls remain usable. For translated content, reviewers check meaning and layout, including whether longer text obscures controls. Correcting these problems makes the course accessible to more people. Providers can then offer it to institutions serving additional language groups, expanding their potential user base and opportunities for sales.​

Before rolling out a new activity or device software update, teams run regression tests on lesson controls, saved progress, assessment results, integrations and accessibility features. Testers also verify head and hand tracking, permissions and Wi-Fi or Bluetooth connections that the update could affect. These checks help catch faults before an update disrupts courses already in use.

During live sessions, monitoring captures frame rates and application response times. Crash reports and failed connections reveal patterns across sessions. A designated reviewer compares alerts and technical data with accounts from educators or trainers.

Support staff rehearse restoring service after a failed update. Testers verify that automatic saves and recovery steps let learners resume training with their progress and assessment records intact. Teams set response targets according to fault severity, and testers verify fixes before release.

Providers can compare releases by tracking the number of sessions abandoned because of technical faults per 100 sessions started, with similar devices, activities and class sizes. Support hours per session and expected session volumes help them estimate staffing needs and delivery costs. Repeat support requests show which problems persist. Educators or workplace trainers contribute skill assessment results alongside learner feedback, so expansion decisions also reflect learning outcomes. Together, these findings help providers decide whether to expand delivery and what to fix first.​

QA gives edtech providers evidence for deciding whether a VR course is ready to scale. Testing shows whether learners can complete activities reliably across supported devices, whether systems remain stable as participation grows and whether progress and assessment records survive interruptions and updates.

After launch, monitoring, regression and recovery testing help teams identify problems before they undermine delivery. Combined with support costs and learning outcomes, these findings give providers a stronger basis for deciding when to expand a course, what to fix first and where technical problems are creating avoidable operational costs.
