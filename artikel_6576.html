The Truth About ‘Natural Flavors’ On Food Labels Might Surprise You

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

The word “natural” comes with a few caveats.

I consider myself a pretty diligent ingredient-label reader. But there’s one phrase I see constantly that, until recently, I hadn’t given much thought to: “natural flavors.”

It’s printed on everything from flavored drinks to packaged snacks, and it sounds pretty straightforward. You’d probably assume that if something contains natural flavors, those flavors come directly from the foods they’re supposed to taste like. After all, that’s what natural suggests—except the actual definition is much broader than you might expect.

According to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), natural flavors can come from a surprisingly long list of sources, including fruits, vegetables, spices, herbs, dairy products, meat, seafood, and even certain fermentation products. And while those ingredients must come from qualifying sources, turning them into flavorings can be far more involved than simply squeezing juice from a fruit.

So what exactly does “natural flavors” mean, and how much does that label actually tell us about what’s in our food? Here’s what you should know.

The FDA’s definition of natural flavors focuses on where the flavoring comes from, not necessarily the food it’s supposed to taste like. Under federal regulations, natural flavors can include essential oils, extracts, distillates, and substances produced through roasting, heating, or enzymatic processing. They can originate from everything from fruit and vegetable juices to herbs, roots, bark, and fermentation products.

That means seeing “natural flavors” on a strawberry-flavored product doesn’t automatically mean the added flavoring came from strawberries.

Specific FDA rules govern how manufacturers describe flavors on the front of packages, particularly when a product’s recognizable flavor comes from sources other than the named food. But the generic ingredient-list declaration alone doesn’t identify the original source. In other words, the word “natural” doesn’t necessarily tell you what you’re tasting.

The FDA generally requires food manufacturers to list ingredients in descending order by weight. But flavorings are one category that can be declared collectively rather than listing every individual flavoring substance.

Under federal regulations, qualifying ingredients can simply appear as “natural flavor” or “natural flavors” on a product’s ingredient list. So manufacturers can use any combination of flavorings without disclosing the specific ones in your food.

There are exceptions, particularly for major food allergens, but the rule helps explain why the ingredient list doesn’t always provide a complete breakdown of a product’s flavor formulation.

The word “natural” tends to sound reassuring, especially when it’s printed on a product alongside claims about ingredients or quality. But it doesn’t automatically tell you anything about that food’s nutritional value.

According to the FDA, food ingredients must meet the same safety standard whether they’re naturally or artificially derived. The agency has also explained that its longstanding policy concerning the broader term “natural” on food labels was not intended to establish any particular nutritional or health benefit.

So while natural and artificial flavors differ in how they’re sourced, that distinction alone doesn’t establish that one is healthier, more nutritious, or safer than the other. And that feels like a pretty important distinction when you’re trying to make sense of the grocery aisle.

Another distinction worth knowing is that “natural” and “organic” don’t mean the same thing. The USDA has additional requirements for flavorings used in certified organic foods. Under federal organic regulations, manufacturers can use nonsynthetic flavors when organic flavors aren’t commercially available.

Those flavorings must come from organic or nonsynthetic sources and cannot be produced using synthetic solvents, synthetic carrier systems, or artificial preservatives. Those are additional restrictions beyond simply meeting the FDA’s definition of natural flavoring.

So while a conventional food and an organic food might both list natural flavors, that doesn’t necessarily mean the flavorings were produced under identical requirements.

The biggest takeaway is that natural flavors aren’t necessarily something to avoid. They’re just a lot less specific than many may assume. The label tells you that the flavoring meets the FDA’s definition of natural, but it doesn’t necessarily identify the ingredient it came from, explain how it was processed, or reveal every flavoring component. It also doesn’t automatically make the product healthier than something containing artificial flavors.

If you’re curious about what you’re eating, it helps to look beyond those two words. Check the full ingredient list, pay attention to how the product describes its flavor on the front of the package, and contact the manufacturer if you need more specific information about a flavoring’s source.

Ultimately, the most surprising thing about natural flavors is just how little those two words actually reveal.
