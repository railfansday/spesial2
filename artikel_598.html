‘Gugu’s World’ Team on the Diversity of Brazilian Cinema and Championing Films That ‘Respect the Storyteller’

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

The creative team behind the coming-of-age drama celebrates a long journey that has culminated in making a star out of newcomer Yuri Gomes and connecting with audiences across Brazil and the world

In the nine months since “Gugu’s World” premiered in Berlin, director Allan Deberton has had only the most “joyful experiences.” By the time the filmmaker sits down with Variety to discuss the whirlwind, the film has just opened in its home country of Brazil, turning its young star into a phenomenon.

That star is Yuri Gomes, who plays the titular Gugu. The boy is the epitome of freedom, zigzagging across a dirt field with a football, then breaking into a dance sequence the moment he hears the throbbing beats of Brazilian brega music. Yes, those around him judge him, including stern dad Batista (Lázaro Ramos), but Gugu is sheltered by the love of grandmother Dilma (Teca Pereira). The two live together in a small town in Ceará, their loving home and safe cocoon until the day Dilma’s health deteriorates due to a rapidly developing case of Alzheimer’s. “Gugu’s World” follows Deberton’s “Pacarrete” and reunites the director with screenwriter André Araújo, who originated the story. The tale of this energetic boy resonated deeply with the filmmaker, who knew he needed a “great cast” to do justice to the “responsibility” of tackling the issues in the script, from talking about young queerness to dealing with caring responsibilities and progressive disorders. Deberton landed two of Brazil’s great screen stars in Ramos and Pereira, but the process of finding Gugu was a much more complex, lengthy operation. The film team saw over 700 kids and eventually found Gomes, a star-in-the-making.

“We needed an actor who had the spirit of Gugu,” says producer Karen Castanho of Biônica Filmes. “But today, the great challenge of filming kids is that, because of social media, kids are not very spontaneous. Yuri is the opposite of that.”

Deberton worked closely with casting director and AD Luciana Vieira to ensure that not only Gomes but the wider cast of children were “very natural.” “We simply see children being children. We don’t see kids acting. I think that’s very strong in the film.”

The director said it is “transformative” to work with children. He enjoyed the experience so much that he decided to replicate it for his next project, “Adoption,” dealing with his personal experience adopting his son. “What they put on the screen is very truthful. We need to be open to that gaze, which is pure, intimate, truthful and that makes us feel alive.”

The film presented a great challenge to Ramos, an actor-director with decades of experience and one of the most recognizable names in Brazil thanks to his work on telenovelas and films such as “Madame Satã” and “Carandiru.” Ramos is more widely known for his loving, well-humored roles, so playing a stern father dealing with internalized homophobia and unable to accept his son was difficult.

“My first read of [Batista] was one that is very distant from the dad I aim to be,” he said. “I was looking to villainize that figure, to signal his flaws, like an alert call. But when we started rehearsing, he gained richness and a much more interesting personality, which is this man who does care about his child, who seeks to be closer to his child, but he doesn’t have the resources.”

Asked about the physicality of his character, who is often contained in contrast to Gugu’s penchant for movement, Ramos shared the complex process. During rehearsals, it felt “curious” to Ramos how he couldn’t mimic Gomes’ movements. The two often danced together or moved together between takes, but Ramos returned to introspection when cameras rolled. “It was good to encounter that vitality of his to understand the opposition we’d aim for in each scene,” he said.

Pereira, on the other hand, was able to imbue Dilma with a lot of herself, such as “her joy for life, that animated way of living and the way she cares for her grandson,” she noted. The role was still quite different from everything she had done previously, with the experience made even more special thanks to the relationship she developed with Gomes. “Luciana Vieira gave us exercises for us to develop that care, to develop a bond,” she recalled. “It was great. Yuri is an intelligent, clever child, as well as talented.”

Working on an indie project always brings a set of challenges, but shooting with a large ensemble cast mostly of children and setting the production in Quixadá, a small city in the inland of Ceará, added massively to Castanho’s plate.

Despite the city being a frequent production hub, the film team wanted to avoid “obvious locations,” turning the city into a base camp. “Most of the locations were within one hour of the city, with a big team, to sets without any infrastructure. We had to literally bring everything to film in these places, which involved traveling one hour every day.”

“At the same time, we needed a city that could host all these people,” added the producer. “The logistical challenge was huge. The background actors are all from the region, but the team is very diverse. Brazil is a country of continental dimensions; we had professionals from all over Brazil. The greatest challenge for us was logistical.”

Deberton noted that the local population completely embraced the project, and the film team worked closely with all background extras to ensure they were comfortable with the costume and production design and felt integrated into the production. Ramos said he “loves shooting on location” and ended up staying longer in Quixadá than he was originally supposed to. “I live in an urban center, I’m very agitated, very rushed,” he said. “And there’s a timing there of the effect of a smaller city, where people know each other, where people have time to chat and I think that ended up influencing me too.”

When asked what “Gugu’s World” communicates about Brazil to international audiences and what they want the film to be in dialogue with when it meets audiences all over the world, the team Castanho is quick to say she wants people to understand the diversity of Brazilian cinema. “This is a small film with a big heart and, especially in this moment in the world, I think we all need that love.”

Deberton echoes that thought, saying he often felt that Brazilian films were “put in a box” as a type of cinema that would connect with cinephiles but not broad audiences. “I think it’s the opposite: when we have access to powerful stories, to characters that transform, that connection happens naturally and genuinely with the audience,” he said.

“This progress that we’re seeing with national cinema shows a quality of directors, of teams and producers,” added Pereira, noting the great momentum of her national cinema, with standout international hits such as “I’m Still Here” and “The Secret Agent” projecting Brazilian films to the world.

“As Allan says, regionality is very important because we don’t want to stay just in the big urban centers,” added the actress. “The quality of the work depends also on the opportunities. Brazil is very diverse. We have a lot to show still, and a lot to do still … And for me it’s very important. I want and hope that all this results in more jobs for many people, because we need to talk about ourselves. And that should be seen by the whole world.”

Ramos wrapped the conversation by humorously stating he would bring a “contentious” subject to the table, which is the need for the regulation of streamers in Brazil so that independent productions can benefit from the market they have helped build, as well as revising how partnerships with major studios and production companies are forged.

“After the pandemic, we went through a period in which, due to market needs, we made some concessions and negotiations, and made many films without identity,” he said. “People watched a film and wouldn’t even know the name of the protagonist at the end of the movie. Or they would be faced with a storytelling model based on either an algorithm or on the desires of another country.”

“Gugu’s World” goes against the grain in this sense, according to the veteran performer. “This film struggled to find partners and financiers for a long time,” he went on. “So let’s take the opportunity to get people to consider the current state of filmmaking, watch this film and go back to respecting the storyteller, which I think is an important message, because otherwise we get a lot of tepid or similar movies.”
