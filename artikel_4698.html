Customer Service Is a Lesson in Learned Helplessness

When we contact customer service, no one actually helps us. We have come to expect this affront, even as it has reduced us to yelling uselessly into the digital void. While companies profit, we experience visceral harm.

These repeated psychological defeats are not trivial. We may find ourselves putting off the chore of getting incorrect charges removed from a bill or promised service upgrades. Following the path of least resistance, giving up, saves us time and surges of outrage, but a subtle hit to our self-esteem is taken with each surrender.

Repeated blows to our sense of personal agency may be more consequential than we realize. When our calls for help are delayed or go unheeded, we may lose confidence that respectful consideration is even attainable. Dialogue with a chatbot or exchanges with an AI “assistant" keep us at an impotent distance that diminishes us.

A few weeks ago, I dialed the customer service number because the service I’ve been paying for had stopped working. The customer service number on their website got me a mechanical voice at the outset: “If at any time you want to talk to a representative, say, representative." I said this word over and over, to no avail.

Not long ago, it was possible to speak to a person. These customer service people were effective, and the company made good on its promises. Now the obstructive methods tamper with our sense of control. No amount of persistence yields someone capable of correcting the problem and thus affirming our conviction of personal efficacy, our belief that we can get something done on our own behalf. The lesson of helplessness is learned again and again.

Research from the American Psychological Association shows that people care less about how long they wait and more about feeling like the line is moving. However, an amorphous phone system doesn't inform people of where they stand in line. The customer service hold is related to learned helplessness; both are uncontrollable and involve repeated frustration. Any action an individual takes has no impact. When people repeatedly endure unpleasant events they cannot escape or control, they stop trying to change their situation. This is learned helplessness.

A hopeful tale recently appeared in The New York Times. A man called for help about erroneous charges on his hotel bill; he spent time going through the motions. For a long while, he got nowhere, and his time was utterly wasted. Then, finally, he reached a human who instantly corrected the problem. Ah, but this unnamed hotel company had messed with the wrong man. It turned out to be the commissioner of the New York City Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, and he is not taking this lying down, as the saying goes.
