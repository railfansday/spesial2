I Rode My Road Bike Where the Pavement Ends to Find Out How Much Gravel It Could Handle

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Here’s what to swap—and what to avoid—before taking your road bike off pavement.

Just as the seasons change, so do riders’ preferences of which types of riding are preferred. For some, the season for taking the road bike on the local roads starts to fade as temperatures get colder and the gravel trail that parallels the road gets more appealing every degree cooler. It’s time to turn the road bike onto that gravel path.

Many of us have tried to take our road bikes on gravel, and there are steps you can take to get the most out of that underbiked gravel ride. After a recent lunch ride, the question was asked, “how much gravel can you take a road bike on?” Since gravel means different things to different people the easy answer is, it depends, but I wanted to stress test my road bike to find out.

I remember an old-school bike technician I used to work with told me, “you can ride any bike on anything if you are light on the bike and find the right line.” While that may be true, I wanted to find out how I could most comfortably take my road bike on everything that is off-road.

After taking my road bike where it was not designed to go, I learned some limitations as well as some simple part swaps to get the most versatility out of a road bike. Below I’ll touch on those limitations that I found and give you the most impactful changes you can make to your road bike before taking it on gravel. As Doc says to Marty McFly in Back to the Future, “Where we’re going, we don’t need roads.”

The bike I decided to do the testing on is my Trek Madone, and I’ve had 700x28mm Pirelli P Zero Race tires pretty much since I’ve built up the bike. Those are by far my favorite road tires for all-around road riding—they’re fast, grip well through turns, and are surprisingly durable for performing so well.

Even though I love those Pirellis for road riding most of the year, I knew I would want to have extra rubber when I’m chattering along through some of the chunkier gravel I’d be testing on. I decided to swap out my tires for a set of the Continental Grand Prix 5000 in 700x32 mm. The maximum tire width on the Gen 8 Trek Madone is 32mm, but these Continental tires only measure just over 30mm because of the internal rim width on the stock Bontrager wheels. I could probably bump up the width a bit more, but I decided to keep the 32mm just in case I knock the wheels out of true during the stress testing.

Going wider with your tires adds an extra bit of compliance and more contact patch—both will help your ride quality when you take your road bike out on gravel. Part of the trick with going wider and bumping down your tire pressure is to make sure your tires are set up as tubeless. Setting your tires up tubeless allows you to run a lower tire pressure without the risk of pinch flats.

When you take your road bike on gravel, new bar tape can help by decreasing a little bit of the vibration. As you ride, most bar tapes compress a bit from the constant pressure of your hands. Some tapes may even start to unravel in sections where you keep your hands a lot—we used to see this a lot when customers couldn’t comfortably reach the hoods. There would be visible wear marks where the rider was keeping their hands on the bars. So replacing your tape at least once a season is a good idea to keep comfort up.

Out of all the bar tape that I’ve tried my favorite for taking a road bike on gravel is the Lizard Skin 3.2 mm Bar Tape. The slightly thicker tape reduces a bit of the chatter in your hands and it’s tacky enough that your hands stay stuck to the bars when you need them to be. This is true on wider tire gravel bikes as well, but it’s especially noticeable when you don’t have the same vibration dampening from a wider tire.

Flat tires happen on any bike, but they can be even more common rolling down a chunky gravel road on your road bike. Correct tire choice, tubeless setups, and riding a bit more cautiously than normal will all help prevent a ride-ending flat tire.

If you do get a flat just make sure you are prepared. At a minimum I typically carry at least one tube, tire levers, at least one type of inflation, and a good multitool. If I’m going to be far from my car, home, or if I’ll be out of cell phone service I’ll bring more than one tube—especially on an underbiking ride. I’ve been stuck out on a ride after burning through my lone spare tube, so now I like having two on rides like this. A few pieces of extra additions to your flat kit are: zip ties, a tire boot or dollar bill, and a tubeless plug kit if you’re running tubeless tires.

Keep in mind it’s still possible to get flat tires with tubeless tires. A few years ago I took my road bike out on a local gravel ride and I bumped my tire pressure down to have a little more comfort and traction on my skinny road tires. I was rolling through some gravel and I hit a rock with my rear wheel hard enough for the tire to get pinched against the rim and put a good slice in my tire. Luckily I brought a tire boot (I used a dollar bill) and an extra tube so I was able to pedal back to the shop. In a pinch I’ve used things like candy wrappers or leaves as a very temporary tire boot. They don’t take up much room in your pack, so if you bring one in your pack there is no need to MacGyver anything on the side of the trail.

If you only have a road bike and want to occasionally ride gravel I would strongly consider getting a second wheelset to ride gravel. If you have one wheel set for gravel and one for pavement you can easily swap wheel sets when you want to ride one discipline versus the other. Set up the wheels with a cassette with the same gear range, same size rotors, and the widest tires your frame and fork can fit—suddenly you have two bikes in one.

As I was taking my road bike on exceedingly questionable terrain I thought, what would most people actually be okay with riding this bike on? As I rolled my Madone into some singletrack it was quickly apparent most people wouldn’t be stoked to follow behind me. Insufficient tire clearance, room for tread, and lack of suspension might be the first reasons you’d think of why a road bike isn’t going to perform well on singletrack (and you’re absolutely right). The geometry of the Madone didn’t give the handling needed to reasonably navigate through the tight trails in the woods.

The next category is where we start to delve into true gravel: doubletrack, jeep roads, and limited maintenance gravel. This is where I started to see the road bike actually perform reasonably well. The extra tire width combined with bumping down the tire pressure allowed the bike to feel reasonably compliant rolling through the varying surfaces. However, I really felt the times I caught a small rock that was hiding underneath the leaves.

Riding local gravel roads and canal trails may have been bumpy, but it was most certainly doable. I noticed I was bumping around a lot, and I felt like I was putting in more effort to match my typical effort on the same segments I usually ride with a gravel bike. Especially when I started to ride through gravel after a bit of rain or sections that were covered in wet leaves I wished for a wider tire with more tread. I had to keep some of the descents a little more tame compared to normal, but on my local gravel climbs I was rolling up faster and more efficiently than normal. That is, until my rear tire would lose traction and I would burn all my matches trying to keep the bike both upright and going up.

Even with that being the case, so many modern road bikes now offer tire clearance to get a cyclocross tire set up. If I were committing to my road bike as a gravel-to-road crossover bike for the winter I would set it up with some fast-rolling tubeless cyclocross tires, put on new bar tape, put an extra tube in my flat pack, and let it ride.
