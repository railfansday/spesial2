Educators who gathered on Nov. 5 say waiting until 3rd grade to fix reading costs children two years they can't get back.

OAKLAND, Calif., Nov. 18, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- For decades, schools have treated 3rd grade as the year that decides whether a student becomes a reader. District leaders from across the country came together last week and declared 3rd grade is two years too late.

On Nov. 5, 56 district leaders and literacy leaders from six states met in Boston for Convene 2026. The national gathering, hosted by Ignite Reading, centered on the First Grade Promise, a commitment launched in 2025 to get every child reading on grade level by the end of 1st grade.

Leaders spent the day-long event pinpointing where 1st graders slip through the cracks in a system and drafting their commitments to close reading gaps early, when intervention has the most lasting impact. Ignite Reading also announced the first national First Grade Promise Week, set for March 1â5, 2027.

The window to get a child on track closes long before the 3rd grade tests most states still use to measure reading success. A 2026 NWEA analysis tracked more than 400,000 children from the start of kindergarten. Kindergartners who started school reading below benchmark have a 1 in 4 chance of reading proficiently by the end of 3rd grade. For children still in the bottom 20% in the spring of 1st grade, those odds fell to 1 in 40.

Kareem Weaver, Co-Founder & Executive Director of FULCRUM, stated, "If we wait until 3rd grade to intervene, we are asking 8-to-9-year-olds to carry the burden of a 'wait-to-fail' model. Fixing reading gaps isn't about catching kids up; it's about giving every child the foundational tools needed to read on time, 1st grade. That shift in timing changes the trajectory of a child's life, and it's key to securing their basic civil rights."

District leaders from Georgia, California, and Massachusetts spoke throughout the day, sharing how they're keeping the promise in their own communities.

In Georgia, where state reading tests begin in 3rd grade, Savannah-Chatham County Public School System moved its reading intervention funding to 1st grade this year. The district has also set a public goal of 95 percent of 3rd graders reading on grade level within five years, up from 53 percent. District leaders joined Weaver to detail how they built the case for the shift.

"Foundational literacy unlocks a child's ability to achieve unlimited possibilities. We owe it to our students, families, and community to ensure that all students are reading on grade level, and that promise begins in kindergarten and 1st grade," said Shraddha Nunziata, Senior Director of Initiatives & Special Projects of Savannah-Chatham County Public School System. "Ignite Reading is a crucial partner in ensuring our youngest readers have the support they need to become strong readers, but we have also strengthened and streamlined structured literacy instruction in the classroom, so the progress our students are making is thanks to our dedicated and talented teachers. By concentrating efforts in the earliest grades, our intervention burden decreases in second grade and beyond, and students are set up to succeed in all subject areas."

Leaders from California's San Bruno Park School District and Massachusetts' Chelsea Public Schools took the stage to detail their district efforts to ensure families are active participants in a system that gets 1st graders reading. Attendees heard how Chelsea's family liaisons are trained in literacy initiatives so they can answer parents' questions about screening and report cards, and how San Bruno Park leverages students' own excitement about their early literacy intervention to gain buy-in from parents and guardians.

"Parent engagement has been recognized for years as a critical factor in student achievement. To build engagement, we must cultivate relationships with families that are built on trust by supporting them in learning to navigate the school system and helping them remove any barriers that may impede their ability to support their child," said Michelle Graham, Director of Teaching, Learning and Intervention at San Bruno Park School District.

"Learning to read is not easy for many children. These students need explicit, systematic instruction and additional support to become confident, successful readers. It is essential that we share clear, meaningful information with families so they understand how their child is progressing, where they may need additional support, and how we can work together to ensure every child has the opportunity to acquire the skills they need to become proficient readers," said Katelyn Webber-Schubmehl, PK-8 Literacy and Humanities Director at Chelsea Public Schools.

First Grade Promise Week will take the question district leaders worked on in Boston to communities across the country: what will it take for every child to read by the end of 1st grade? National speakers and the full program will be announced in January.

Anyone can take the First Grade Promise pledge now at www.firstgradepromise.org.
