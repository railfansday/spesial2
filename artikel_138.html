How to deal with a class difference in friendships? Make fun of your privileged friends

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Lasting cross-class friendships involve routinely ‘taking the piss’ out of the more privileged party, research by the LSE shows

Routinely “taking the piss” out of more privileged friends is one of the main ways people cope with a class difference in their friendships, research has found.

Cross-class friendships are widespread and mutually beneficial, a study by the London School of Economics showed, but a class gap can still pose an “omnipresent threat” to friendships.

More privileged friends often provide financial support or cultural guidance to less privileged friends navigating elite spaces, the research found, and in return get a better understanding of the world outside their immediate sphere.

But because the more tangible support tends to flow one way, it often brings about feelings of inequality or indebtedness, said the sociologist Prof Sam Friedman, who co-wrote the research.

In some cases, it becomes a source of tension or sends friendships into a downward spiral, said Friedman, the author of The Class Ceiling and Born To Rule, which explored how the British elite has changed over the last 120 years.

He and Prof Rose Butler, from Deakin University in Australia, looked at successful and failed cross-class friendships and found that those that lasted exhibited “class diplomacy”. This involved making jokes, prioritising friendship over class differences and building empathy through talking about class differences.

A working-class man referred to as Peter in the study described making jokes about his friend, Julian. “It would be your typical private school boy jokes … and stuff like, I don’t know, about mummy and daddy’s credit card on a night out,” he said.

A working-class friend in a similar friendship said his more privileged friend’s reaction to these kinds of jokes made him respect him. He said: “I think the humour’s really important because essentially … he was quite thick [skinned], he never like got pissy or anything. A lot of the time he would take it on the chin.”

The paper’s starting point was two studies by economists in the US and UK that analysed billions of friendship ties on Facebook and said that cross-class friendships were a good thing because they gave working-class people social capital that propelled them towards upward social mobility, Friedman said.

He and Butler tested this claim using 42 in-depth interviews and found that humour was often pivotal to defusing tension.

One friend from a background self-described as “upper middle class” and “elitey”, said: “The having a laugh thing is such a big deal … like the therapeutic way of managing [the class difference] … He’ll take the piss out of me but it’s fine.”

Other more privileged friends would make jokes at their own expense. One working-class person in the study said about her friend from a wealthy background: “Every time I discovered a new house [that her family owned] I’d be like ‘Jesus Christ’ and would just let it slip, but both Sophie and me take the piss out of it. Like she’ll rip herself apart … she always does it before I can.”

Friedman said the humour in successful friendships never resorted to “punching down”. “It’s punching up that works,” he said.

Some friends coped with the difference by not discussing class. Less privileged friends talked about not wanting to call out snobbery.

One middle-class person said about their less privileged friend: “I don’t think we really ever mention class. But I think we’re hyper aware of the difference. It feels like it’s conferred such real-life advantages and disadvantages that it almost is so difficult to speak about.”

Another friend from a less privileged background said: “I think he had a lot of learning to do and I think when both of us started to get to know one another, both his side realising how unbelievably lucky he’s had it, but on the flip me not being such a judgmental prick … it went both ways.”

Friedman also identified that “the emotional burden of confronting class difference often fell disproportionately on friends from less advantaged backgrounds”.

“This was partly due to the fact that most of these friendships had emerged in middle-class spaces where middle-class norms were dominant and often taken for granted,” according to the paper, published in the journal Social Forces on Wednesday.

“Such inequality was best encapsulated by the idiom ‘a chip on the shoulder’ – the idea that someone is habitually (and unreasonably) bitter because they feel they’ve been treated unfairly – and is normally deployed on the basis of social class in Britain.”

Friedman said working-class interviewees often described this phrase being used against them by their friends at some point, “or feared that it might be used against them”.
