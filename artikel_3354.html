7 Most Politically Subversive Movies of All Time, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

There are multiple ways in which a film can be subversive, whether it's by twisting the tropes of its genres on their head, deliberately undermining traditional societal norms, or bending genres to surprise audiences. One particular thing that makes several of the most subversive movies in history so great, on the other hand, is that they disrupt and aggressively question dominant political ideologies, the mainstream media, and topics that are usually considered taboo.

It's one thing for a film to be artistically subversive, but films that actively challenge dominant power structures and cultural norms instead of reinforcing them prove that politically subversive cinema can be particularly powerful. Over the years, the big screen has been treated to several such movies, made by artists of the caliber of Stanley Kubrick and Pier Paolo Pasolini.

Infamous for being one of the most heavily censored mainstream horror films in history, Ken Russell's The Devils is about as legendary as the director himself. Based on the fall of 17th-century Catholic priest Urbain Grandier and the possessions of Loudun, it's a film which weaponizes blasphemy and sacrilege to tell a story that is, at its core, far more political than it is religious.

There is certainly plenty of religious commentary in The Devils, but what makes it one of the best horror masterpieces of the '70s is how unapologetic it is in calling out procedural injustice, the centralization of power, and government brainwashing through religious hysteria. It's a must-see NC-17 cult classic that's made all the more politically potent by the fact that it's based on true events.

Charles Chaplin had long refused to get with the times and leave behind the silent cinema that he had completely revolutionized. However, going into the late '30s, he found that the subject matter of The Great Dictator deserved to be such a direct political statement that it couldn't possibly be anything but his first true talkie. Thus, the world was treated to one of the best dark comedy movies of all time.

By the time the film came out — and certainly when Chaplin began developing it in 1938 — many in the Western world still vastly underestimated the horrors that were being committed by Hitler and the Nazis. So, Chaplin did what he did best: he took these very serious topics and ridiculed dictators like Hitler and Mussolini, loudly calling for a stop to their atrocities well before the United States entered World War II.

Sergei Eisenstein is widely regarded as one of the most important and influential filmmakers in history, largely thanks to his contributions to the art of film editing. There is no better way to learn about and understand those contributions than watching Battleship Potemkin, one of the best war thrillers of all time, which dramatizes the 1905 mutiny aboard the eponymous Russian battleship.

Battleship Potemkin is proof that even an official piece of state propaganda can be politically subversive. Indeed, though the film was commissioned by the Soviet government to celebrate the 1905 revolution, it was an explosive piece of international political subversion seeking to undermine the capitalist social order the world over. Later on, even the USSR itself came to consider Eisenstein's avant-garde techniques a bit too rebellious for their taste.

Despite how quiet it seems, Kubrick's Paths of Glory is also one of the most outwardly anti-war movies in history, based on the Souain corporals affair that took place during World War I. Due to the film's critically negative depiction of the French military, the country refused to allow Kubrick to shoot there and even refused to allow the movie to screen there until 1972.

What arguably makes Paths of Glory even more radical and subversive, as far as Kubrick's anti-war movies go, is that instead of laughing at the matter, it's an entirely solemn and serious depiction of it. One of the most hopeless war movies of all time, Paths of Glory exposes the dark underbelly of military leadership in ways that no other film had really dared to depict before. In it, patriotism and military justice are dangerous myths used to manipulate working-class young men into allowing themselves to be used like cannon fodder.

What could possibly be more politically subversive than a movie that has been documented to be used as a training manual by both insurgent and counterinsurgency groups? We are, of course, talking about Gillo Pontecorvo's Italian-Algerian masterpiece The Battle of Algiers, a docudrama shot in a newsreel style about the rebellion against the French government during the Algerian War.

It's one of the best films of the 1960s, crafted with such an unparalleled sense of visceral realism that it really does allow the audience to feel as though they're flies on the wall, watching this conflict from the outside. Without ever sugarcoating the horrific violence of anti-colonial resistance or state counter-terrorism, Pontecorvo forces the viewer to witness all of this brutality with every moral nuance and political layer laid bare for them to closely analyze.

From beginning to end and from top to bottom, Pier Paolo Pasolini's Italian-French political art horror masterpiece Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom is a movie specifically designed to be as subversive, transgressive, and provocative as possible. Loosely adapting Marquis de Sade's The 120 Days of Sodom and setting the story in WWII Italy instead of 18th-century France, the film was released a mere three weeks after Pasolini was killed, making it his final masterpiece.

Saying that you need a strong stomach to get through Salò would be the understatement of the century. Pasolini, a lifelong Marxist and critic of Italian power structures, wanted to make a movie that criticized consumerism, fascism, and systematic abuse. Boy, did he succeed. Salò is quite infamously one of the heaviest movies of the 20th century, so politically aggressive and full of unrelenting, stomach-churning horror that it was outright banned across many countries. In many territories, it still faces bans and censorship to this day.

For precisely the same reasons that arguably make it the single most politically rebellious feature film in history, Herbert J. Biberman's Salt of the Earth was one of the first fully independent movies made outside of the Hollywood studio system. That's because Biberman, as well as screenwriter Michael Wilson and producer Paul Jarrico, were all blacklisted in Hollywood at the time due to their alleged involvement in communist politics.

Salt of the Earth was a direct act of working-class political subversion starring actual striking laborers and their families, created as an explicit celebration of worker solidarity and civil rights right at the peak of McCarthyism and the Red Scare in the United States. As if that weren't enough, it's also one of the greatest American films of the '50s, making it a must-see for all those interested in how complex politically radical cinema can be at its best.
