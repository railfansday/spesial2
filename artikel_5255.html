Jensen Huang is back at Nvidia. Here's why

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Figures like Huang, like Steve Jobs, come as a single package. We can't call on their genius without also living with their limits

Jensen Huang is back. One reading of why: He won the power struggle because he sat at the center of an ecosystem of buyers, suppliers, and financiers, all of whom depended on his word, his skill, and his vision.

Nvidia $NVDA doesn't just sell into the AI build-out. It finances it, and in doing it has taken on risks that could ripple well beyond the company. The AI chipmaker has invested $30 billion in OpenAI, which buys computing power from Nvidia's customers. It put $2 billion into CoreWeave, a customer that rents Nvidia-powered computing to AI developers. It agreed to buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity through 2032, a backstop initially valued at $6.3 billion. It gives some large customers 3-12 months to pay. It committed up to $10 billion to Anthropic, which in turn committed to buy $30 billion of computing from Microsoft $MSFT's Azure, which runs partly on Nvidia systems. Nvidia's money funds Anthropic, which pays Microsoft, which buys Nvidia hardware.

Nvidia also lends out its credit standing. It has taken on up to $118 billion in guarantees that lenders and landlords rely on, including up to $105 billion backing OpenAI's leases at SB Energy's Ohio data-center campus. And it is working with Blackstone, Apollo, KKR and others to raise more than $500 billion of outside capital, on which it may cover up to a quarter of a project's residual value.

This circular financing is risky. If demand for computing power falls behind supply, Nvidia would absorb losses as seller, investor, backstop buyer, and guarantor all at once. Its share price would fall sharply, dragging down the market and tightening credit elsewhere in the economy.

Nvidia's counterparties saw Huang as the personal guarantor of this fragile system. In March, speaking of TSMC $TSM, Huang said: "I don't know how many tens, hundreds of billions of dollars of business we've done through them, and we don't have a contract." Suppliers invest upstream, he added, "because I said to the CEOs, 'Let me tell you how big this industry is going to be.'"

Two days after Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO following revelations of multiple affairs, including with a suspected Chinese spy, Elon Musk said on social media that "many of those deals were a handshake with Jensen, not a written contract."

Finance, I'd argue, is 10% math and 90% psychology.

Here's a more Machiavellian reading of what happened Monday when Nvidia's board reinstated Huang less than two weeks after his ouster: Huang planned his resignation as a springboard back. Think game theory. Had he stayed, every new revelation would have weakened him further. By resigning first, he took the sting out of whatever came next, since he'd already fessed up. And he let everyone feel what life without him looked like.

Nvidia's stock was hammered in the days before and after his departure, and all three major market indexes ended last week in correction territory as the wider market slumped with Nvidia. Partners worried his handshake deals would fall apart. Within days, an unnamed ally told the Financial Times they were assembling a "war room" to lobby for his return.

The board chair said during the company's earnings call last week that Huang wasn't coming back. Six days later, he was back. Nvidia stock quickly soared 35%. The rest of the market followed.

The board's statement is revealing. It outsourced its judgment to Washington, saying that "senior levels of the United States government" had assured it Huang posed no national security risk. Trump later called his own intervention "very important." To him, Huang was an ace in the hole in the AI race with China. The board, which had cited Huang's lack of candor, now declared itself "satisfied" with it.

That was a climbdown. Nvidia reinstated him before its own investigation had concluded. Huang offered humility and thanks to his family, but no fresh apology upon his return. The board also announced a search for a chief compliance officer, reporting to a board committee. Whoever takes the job will be a watchdog for the board while depending on Huang's cooperation. Don't envy them. Score it Huang 2, Board 1.

Huang is larger than life. Few doubt his genius, even as many recoil at his disregard for protocol. We talk about economic forces, but they are embodied in the people who make decisions and take risks on our behalf. Almost 200,000 people signed a petition for his return, more than 10,000 of whom said they were Nvidia employees.

Figures like Huang, like Steve Jobs, come as a single package. We can't call on their genius without also living with their limits. They live in the world of ideas rather than people, which is why they feel free to break rules and norms. Institutions exist to check them.

Larry Hirschhorn is a management consultant and expert on the psychodynamics of organizations.
