Netflix’s ‘Secrets’ Takes Indonesian Auteur Ravi Bharwani Into Thriller Territory After Eight-Year Gap

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Indonesian filmmaker Ravi Bharwani‘s Netflix thriller “Secrets,” his first feature in eight years, will world premiere at the 21st Jogja-Netpac Asian Film Festival on Nov. 29, four days before it begins streaming on the platform on Dec. 3.

Sha Ine Febriyanti (“This Earth of Mankind”) plays Parni, a former sex worker who now runs a beauty salon while raising her daughter on her own. Her hard-won stability is upended when Jamal (Abimana Aryasatya, “Gundala”), the violent man she once escaped, gets out of prison and comes back determined to reclaim her. Shenina Cinnamon plays Parni’s daughter Riska, and Ruth Marini plays Darsi. The film is adapted from Dewi Kharisma Michellia’s short story “Rahasia dari Kramat Tunggak” (“Secrets from Kramat Tunggak”). Its early section unfolds in 1980s Jakarta, in a milieu drawn from the Kramat Tunggak district, before the story leaps ahead to the 2000s and Jamal’s return. Bharwani co-wrote the screenplay with Rayya Makarim, who has worked with him for over a quarter century, and Wilza Lubis produced through Buddy Buddy Pictures. Both were also on board for his previous feature, 2018’s “27 Steps of May.”

Bharwani, whose earlier features are “Impian Kemarau” (2004) and “Fishing Platform” (2008), tells Variety he spent the intervening years writing scripts and synopses, completing a couple of screenplays along the way. “But then my biggest problem is still funding, distribution, so that’s my biggest challenge,” he says. “And simply, this is the one that got financed, so I jumped in without having to wait for another few years anymore.”

He was first drawn to the short story by a scene in which a woman is won in a game of chess. “I can feel the mood of the scene, the setting, the color, and the stickiness, the humidity of that scene,” Bharwani says.

“Secrets” is his first thriller, and with it come erotic scenes, violence and action, none of which he had filmed before. He says exploring the darker side of human psychology, tackling domestic violence and working at the scale of a Netflix production made the shift difficult. That difficulty was also what drew him in.

“For me, it’s not just about the bombastic scenes or the sensationalism,” he says. “For me, it’s how to make the audience invest in the fear, in the anger, in the hate, in being a victim, being in power of this character.”

Bharwani set the opening in the 1980s because brothels in Kramat Tunggak operated legally then. The district was later shut down, though he notes the trade simply moved underground, and the area no longer exists. His research drew on a book about Kramat Tunggak and on Michael Glawogger’s 2011 documentary “Whores’ Glory,” which follows sex workers in several countries.

“And then I do not try to be consistent, to be true to the setting of the 1980s, but what is important for the film, and that’s the one that I go along with,” Bharwani says. He worked closely with cinematographer Arief Retno Pribadi on color, costume and lighting so that each scene’s look matched its emotional register.

Shaping Jamal meant ongoing conversations with Aryasatya through prep, the shoot and post-production. Bharwani says the actor has an uncle he recognized as a near-exact match for the character and grew up in a tough environment steeped in machismo, yet brings a real sensitivity to emotion and to women.

“There are moments that we see this is too human that we see, ‘Okay, the audience will get into giving more sympathy for Jamal,'” Bharwani says. “So we need to edit it out, but at the same time not wanting to make him a one-dimensional character.”

Indonesia is one of Asia’s largest and fastest-growing theatrical markets, but much of that growth has come from horror and other genre fare. Bharwani says his previous work relied on international festival funding that took years to secure, and the films sometimes ended up playing only at festivals.

“So hopefully with Netflix over here, they spearhead the whole industry, change the whole industry setting of not just going to safe areas, going to horror, going to drama, or going to easier comedy films,” he says.

At JAFF, “Secrets” plays in the Indonesian Screen Awards section. Editor Harin Paesongthai, production designer Menfo Tantono and composers Aghi Narottama and Tony Merle round out the key crew. Beyond promoting the film, Bharwani plans to work through the festival lineup and meet other filmmakers.

He already has projects in the writing stage. “Making a film for me is a great chance to be able to express what I feel about humans, about environment, about stuff that I experience myself,” he says.

By his count, he has enough material ready for two or three more films – if someone is willing to make them.
