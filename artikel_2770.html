Jalen Washington joins new lawsuit to win eligibility for Tennessee basketball

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Jalen Washington is continuing his pursuit of eligibility for the 2026-27 season by joining a new lawsuit against the NCAA in his home state of Indiana.

The Tennessee basketball center was named a plaintiff in a lawsuit filed on Nov. 13 against the NCAA and Purdue University in the Tippecanoe Superior Court, according to court documents obtained by Knox News.

A total 45 athletes, including UT track's Lauren Jarrett, are named in the lawsuit, citing a recent ruling from an Indiana court, and are requesting a temporary retraining order, preliminary injunction and permanent injunction that would allow them to compete in their sports' seasons during the 2026-27 academic year.

"Plaintiffs do not write on a blank slate in Indiana," the motion for a temporary restraining order says. "On September 23, 2026, after an evidentiary hearing with live testimony from more than a dozen players and coaches, the Monroe Circuit Court granted a preliminary injunction to more than ninety athletes from the high school Class of 2022 on the same claims Plaintiffs assert here."

A hearing for Washington's case has been scheduled for Nov. 23 at 9 a.m. ET.

Washington's latest request for a temporary restraining order expresses some urgency in his pursuit to become eligible with a Dec. 1 deadline for finalizing rosters and a marquee contest for the xx No. Vols (3-0) vs. No. 3 Michigan State on Nov. 17 (9 p.m. ET, ESPN2), the start of a difficult non-conference slate for the Vols, approaching.

"Jalen Washington, a native of Gary, Indiana is currently attending the University of Tennessee. Tennessee plays Michigan State University, which is ranked 6th in the country, on November 17, 2026. That nationally televised matchup between two top 10 teams represents a unique opportunity to showcase his skills for NBA scouts," the motion for a temporary retraining order states.

In addition to granting the plaintiffs immediate eligibility, blocking the NCAA and conferences from enforcing rules against their eligibility and awarding them the costs of legal fees, the complaint filed requests a transfer portal window be opened for suing athletes. Both Washington and Jarrett would compete at Tennessee if they are made eligible, the complaint says.

This lawsuit is similar to the one Washington filed in the Davidson County Chancery Court in Tennessee, but expands on some of the legal claims. It argues denying the plaintiffs eligibility constitutes a breach of contract by the NCAA, violates multiple state laws and interferes with opportunities for the plaintiffs to earn money for their skills and advance professional careers.

The lawsuit's claims challenge the NCAA's application of an age-based eligibility model that allows college athletes to play up to five seasons starting at their college enrollment or 19th birthday, whichever comes first. The rule does not apply to athletes from the 2022 signing class like Washington, who played four seasons at North Carolina and Vanderbilt before transferring to UT.

Washington's quest to win another year of eligibility has now hit roadblocks in two different states since the Court of Appeals in Tennessee ordered a stay of the temporary injunction, which initially made him eligible for the 2026-27 season on Aug. 27.

The Tennessee Supreme Court denied Washington's request for review of the Davidson County Chancery Court's order to stay a temporary injunction that allowed him to transfer to UT on on Oct. 16, saying the plaintiffs "have not shown a likelihood of success on their claim that the NCAA breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing."

He was also listed a plaintiff in a separate eligibility lawsuit in Texas that ultimately earned Lady Vols player Nya Robertson a temporary injunction, allowing her to play in games vs. Coppin State, New Haven and Middle Tennessee State. But Washington was not one of the three athletes granted an injunction by the District Court of McClennan County, Texas.

Tennessee coach Rick Barnes is frustrated about what he believes are inconsistent rulings in eligibility lawsuits around the country. Washington's lawsuit points out that trial courts in 28 states have granted injunctions to athletes suing for eligibility while he remains sidelined.

"If one's eligible, they should all be eligible and that's the way I see it," Barnes said on Nov. 16. "I think it's just a cloud hanging over college basketball and we've got too good of a game for something like this to be happening."

Barnes called it "embarrassing" the NCAA is spending precious legal resources and money fighting these lawsuits. He suggested the NCAA admit they made a mistake in excluding class of 2022 athletes from the age-based eligibility rule, create a blanket waiver for those athletes, and put whatever money that would have been spent on legal fees towards support for Olympic sports or historically Black colleges and universities.

"For them not to understand that the field needs to be leveled, and I don't know how you can take money out of a kid's pocket that's proven he's got a situation here that he can do that, I think it's a lot of pride on the part of the NCAA and it's somewhat some arrogance," Barnes said.

The 6-foot-10 Washington was a key addition for the Vols, who could use all the help they can get in the frontcourt. He averaged career-highs in points (8.9), rebounds (5.6) and blocks (1.3) per game while shooting 61.8% from 2-point range at Vanderbilt last season.

Especially after the Vols gave up 24 offensive rebounds in a narrow win against Tennessee State on Nov. 13, Barnes believes Washington would make a massive, immediate impact should he win his eligibility.

"I think physically, he's a difference-maker for us inside. I think he, based on where we are right now, would play significant minutes and he would make a difference," Barnes said. "There's no doubt he would make a difference."
