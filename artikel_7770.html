DOJ Scores Key Victory in Precedent-Setting Military Attorney Licensing Case

The Justice Department has defeated New Mexico’s attempt to dismiss a landmark case involving the licensing of servicemember and military spouse attorneys. On Nov. 19, Judge Kea Riggs of the U.S. District for the District of New Mexico filed by the New Mexico Supreme Court and the New Mexico Board of Bar Examiners in the Department’s first-ever lawsuit to enforce the professional license portability provisions of the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA).

“The Court’s decision is a significant victory for military families and the rule of law,” said Acting Assistant Attorney General Craig W. Trainor of the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division. “The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act is designed to ease the burdens of military service, including by helping military spouses continue their careers when their duty station changes. Federal law allows servicemembers and spouses to easily transfer a wide variety of professional licenses as they move across state lines, including attorney bar licenses. The Civil Rights Division will not allow state licensing authorities to deny these rights and jeopardize the readiness of our troops and the financial stability of military families.”

“Military families should be able to make New Mexico their home without facing unlawful barriers to employment,” said First Assistant U.S. Attorney Ryan Ellison for the District of New Mexico. “When servicemembers and their spouses are assigned here, they deserve the protections Congress guaranteed them, regardless of their professions. This ruling ensures that New Mexico will have to answer the government’s allegations, and we will continue pursuing this case on behalf of those who serve our country.”

On June 11, the Department filed a lawsuit against the New Mexico Supreme Court and Board of Bar Examiners, alleging that they violated the SCRA by refusing to recognize the out-of-state law licenses of qualifying servicemembers and their spouses. The suit alleges that New Mexico imposes requirements on military applicants that exceed what is allowed by the SCRA by requiring them to provide transcripts, test scores, and references and to submit to a full character and fitness evaluation.

In denying the motions to dismiss, the court made three primary findings. First, the court found that the New Mexico Supreme Court and Board of Bar Examiners are “licensing authorities” covered by the SCRA’s license portability provision. Second, the court held that while the law permits a licensing authority to conduct a criminal background check, it does not allow it to conduct the expansive character and fitness evaluation it normally requires for first-time applicants. Finally, the court determined that the Department had alleged facts suggesting that the Supreme Court and Board of Bar Examiners had engaged in a pattern or practice of violating the SCRA.

The Department’s enforcement of the SCRA is conducted by the Civil Rights Division’s Housing and Civil Enforcement Section in partnership with U.S. Attorneys’ Offices throughout the country. Since 2011, the Department has obtained over $490 million in monetary relief for over 153,000 servicemembers through its enforcement of the SCRA. For more information about the Department’s SCRA enforcement efforts, please visit www.servicemembers.gov.

Servicemembers and their dependents who believe that their rights under the SCRA may have been violated should contact the nearest Armed Forces Legal Assistance Program Office. Office locations can be found at legalassistance.law.af.mil.
