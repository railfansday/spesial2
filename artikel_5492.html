From Trump University to Yeezy, 5 celebrity brands that failed or faltered

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These celebrity businesses that flopped or stumbled, from Trump University to Planet Hollywood and Yeezy, ran into lawsuits, bankruptcies, settlements and broken partnerships

Fame can draw a crowd to a launch. It cannot settle a lawsuit, satisfy a creditor, answer a regulator or keep a corporate partner on board. The celebrity businesses that flopped or stumbled in this list show where that limit sits. Each one carried a famous name. Each one still faced the same pressures that test any other company.

A recognizable founder lowers the cost of getting attention. Fans already know the name on the label before the first product ships. That head start does not change how a business is judged once it is open. Customers still decide whether to come back. Courts still hear complaints. Regulators still review what a company says about its products. Partners still weigh whether an association helps or hurts them.

The five cases here took different turns. Trump University was a real estate seminar program. It drew a lawsuit from the New York attorney general and class actions from students. Donald Trump agreed in November 2016 to pay $25 million to settle those suits. His Trump Steaks and Trump Vodka products were also discontinued.

Planet Hollywood opened in New York in 1991. Its backers included Arnold Schwarzenegger, Bruce Willis, Sylvester Stallone and Demi Moore. The restaurant chain filed for bankruptcy protection in 1999 and again in 2001. Gwyneth Paltrow started Goop as a newsletter in 2008. In 2018, the lifestyle brand paid $145,000 to settle a civil case with California district attorneys over unsupported health claims. Adidas ended its Yeezy partnership with Kanye West in October 2022 after his antisemitic remarks. Kim Kardashian's KKW Beauty line took a new name, SKKN by Kim, in 2022.

Not every one of these ventures disappeared. Goop still operates. Planet Hollywood still runs at a much smaller scale. The lawsuits against Trump University ended in a settlement. Adidas cut its ties with West and sold off the remaining Yeezy inventory. The KKW Beauty name was retired.

This is a selection of celebrity business failures and setbacks, not a ranking or a complete list. Each slide covers one celebrity's venture or group of products and the event that marked its turn: a lawsuit, a bankruptcy filing, a regulatory settlement, a terminated partnership or a rebrand.

Trump University, a real estate seminar program carrying Donald Trump's name, ended in court. In November 2016, Trump agreed to pay $25 million to resolve the lawsuits the program drew.

The legal pressure came from two sides. One case was a government action brought by the New York attorney general. The others were class actions filed by former students of the seminars.

A single $25 million agreement covered both the state's case and the students' claims. The settlement closed the litigation without a trial verdict.

The Trump brand also reached beyond education. Trump Steaks carried the name into food. Trump Vodka carried it into liquor. Both product lines were later discontinued.

Taken together, the three ventures stretched one name across seminars, meat and spirits. All three have since ended.

Planet Hollywood, the celebrity-backed restaurant chain that opened in New York in 1991, filed for bankruptcy protection in 1999 and again in 2001. The brand outlasted both filings and still operates, though at a much smaller scale.

Film actors backed the chain. Its investors included Arnold Schwarzenegger, Bruce Willis, Sylvester Stallone and Demi Moore. Their names tied the restaurants to Hollywood from the start.

The first bankruptcy filing came eight years after the New York opening. The second came two years after the first. That timing put the company back under bankruptcy protection soon after its first case.

Neither filing closed the business. The Planet Hollywood name is still in use. It now sits on an operation far smaller than the chain that first sought protection in 1999.

Gwyneth Paltrow's Goop paid $145,000 in 2018 to settle a civil case with California district attorneys. The case involved health claims the brand made about its products, including the jade egg, that were not supported.

The settlement was a regulatory setback for Goop, not the end of the business. The company kept operating after the case and is still in business.

Paltrow founded Goop in 2008. It began as a newsletter before it grew into a lifestyle brand that sells products.

The jade egg was one of the products at the center of the dispute. The case turned on what Goop told customers those products could do for their health.

Goop's trouble came from law enforcement officials, not from creditors, a bankruptcy court or a partner ending a deal. The district attorneys challenged the brand's marketing claims, and the $145,000 payment resolved the civil case.

That places Goop among celebrity business setbacks rather than shutdowns.

Adidas ended its Yeezy partnership with Kanye West in October 2022 after he made antisemitic remarks. The sportswear company later sold off the Yeezy inventory that remained.

Yeezy was the sneaker line Adidas produced with West. The partnership tied a major manufacturer to a celebrity name.

The deal ended because of West's public statements. It did not end with a bankruptcy filing or a court settlement. That sets it apart from celebrity brands that failed under pressure from creditors or lawsuits.

The split left Adidas holding Yeezy products that had already been made. The company chose to sell off that stock after the partnership ended.

The case turned on one party's decision. Once Adidas ended the agreement, the Yeezy line under that partnership stopped.

Kim Kardashian's KKW Beauty name ended in 2022, when the line was rebranded as SKKN by Kim. The rebrand replaced the original label with a new one.

The change was a name switch. It was not a bankruptcy filing, a court-ordered shutdown or a legal settlement.

KKW Beauty is the one case here where the turning point was a change of identity rather than an outside party forcing an end. The KKW Beauty brand name stopped being used in 2022. SKKN by Kim took its place.
