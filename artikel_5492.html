Who was James Bond based on? 10 spies tied to Ian Fleming's wartime world

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 real spies linked to James Bond by memoirs, biographers or popular tradition range from a Yugoslav double agent to the gadget maker named as Q

James Bond came out of a war, not out of a single man. Before Ian Fleming wrote fiction, he spent World War II inside British naval intelligence. That job put him close to the agents, operations and planners of a secret war.

Fleming served in the Naval Intelligence Division (NID) of the British Admiralty. He worked as personal assistant to its director, John Godfrey, and held the rank of commander in the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve. The post gave him a view of how intelligence was gathered, protected and used. His first Bond novel, Casino Royale, appeared in 1953.

Fleming never named one model for 007. Bond is a fictional composite. The question of who he was based on has no single answer. What exists is a long list of candidates. Some names come from memoirs written by the spies themselves. Others come from biographers who have made a case in print. A few rest on long-standing popular claims.

Those sources carry different weight. A double agent's own account of crossing paths with Fleming is not the same as a documented encounter. A biographer's argument is not the same as a statement from Fleming. A superior Fleming served directly also has a different kind of claim from someone whose reputation simply matched the character.

The 10 figures below are a selection of real spies who inspired James Bond, according to the memoirists, biographers and traditions that make each link. They are not a ranking or a complete list. They include an agent born in the Russian Empire whose legend predates World War II, plus a Yugoslav double agent. An MI6 station chief in Paris and a Scottish diplomat who served in the SAS also appear. So do a Warsaw-born SOE agent, an American-born operative codenamed Cynthia and a Canadian spymaster in New York. The last three are an MI5 spymaster, a maker of concealed gadgets and the naval intelligence chief Fleming served.

Each slide sets out what the person did and who connects them to Bond. Where a link is unproven or contested, the slide says so. The aim is to separate documented careers from the legends that grew up around the character.

Sidney Reilly was a British agent born in the Russian Empire. His "Ace of Spies" reputation is often linked to James Bond. That connection rests on legend, not on any confirmed statement by Ian Fleming.

Reilly's best-known operation took place in Moscow in 1918. He took part in the so-called Lockhart plot, a scheme by Allied agents and diplomats against the new Bolshevik government. The plot took its name from Robert Bruce Lockhart, Britain's envoy to the Bolsheviks at the time. The plotters tried to win over the Latvian riflemen who guarded the Kremlin.

The scheme fell apart in late summer 1918. On Aug. 30 of that year, Lenin was shot and wounded in Moscow. The Cheka, the Bolshevik secret police, then launched the crackdown known as the Red Terror. Lockhart was arrested and later exchanged for the Soviet diplomat Maxim Litvinov. Reilly escaped Russia. A Soviet tribunal later sentenced him to death in absentia.

Reilly did not stay away for good. Soviet authorities say he was shot in 1925 after being lured back into the USSR. The lure is associated with Operation Trust, a deception run by the OGPU, the Cheka's successor agency. Trust posed as an anti-Bolshevik underground network. It drew émigrés and foreign intelligence contacts across the border. Reilly crossed from Finland in 1925 and never came back.

His fame grew after his disappearance. In 1967, Robin Bruce Lockhart, the son of the 1918 envoy, published a book titled Ace of Spies. In 1983, the British television series Reilly, Ace of Spies, starring Sam Neill, brought the nickname to a wider audience. Both works presented him as a daring operative working against a hostile regime.

Many accounts of Reilly's early life and career contradict one another. The portion that can be stated with confidence is narrower. It covers the 1918 plot, his escape from Russia and his disappearance in 1925.

Reilly's name appears often among possible models for Bond. The comparison rests on his record as a British agent who worked against the Soviet state. Bond's chief enemy in the early novels was SMERSH, a Soviet counterintelligence organization. The resemblance is one of reputation rather than documented influence.

Dusko Popov was a Yugoslav lawyer who worked as a double agent for the British during World War II, under the MI5 codename Tricycle. His own memoir places Ian Fleming at a casino in Estoril, Portugal, watching Popov gamble.

German military intelligence, the Abwehr, recruited Popov as an agent. He reported the approach to the British and agreed to work for them. MI5 then ran him as part of the Double Cross System. That operation turned German agents into channels for false information.

A body known as the Twenty Committee oversaw the system. Its chairman, J.C. Masterman, later described the program in The Double-Cross System in the War of 1939 to 1945, published in 1972. Popov's reports to his German handlers were shaped by his British controllers.

In 1941 the Germans sent Popov to the U.S. He carried a questionnaire listing the information his handlers wanted him to gather. Some of its questions concerned Pearl Harbor, the U.S. naval base in Hawaii.

Part of that questionnaire was hidden in microdots. These were photographs shrunk to roughly the size of a printed period. Popov passed the material to the FBI. Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on Dec. 7, 1941, months after his arrival.

Popov's memoir, Spy/Counterspy, appeared in 1974. Estoril sits on the coast west of Lisbon. Portugal was neutral during the war, and the Lisbon area drew agents from both sides.

No independent record confirms Popov's account of Fleming at the casino. The story comes from Popov alone. He published it decades after the war, by which time Bond was already a film franchise.

Casino Royale, Fleming's first Bond novel, builds its central set piece around a high-stakes game of baccarat. Popov's Estoril story also unfolds at a casino table. The link between Popov and Bond rests on that parallel and on Popov's own word.

His wartime record stands apart from the casino story. Popov's work for MI5 belongs to the documented history of the Double Cross System, whether or not Fleming ever saw him play.

Wilfred "Biffy" Dunderdale ran the MI6 station in Paris in the years before World War II and into its early stages. A long-standing claim links him to James Bond, but no document shows that Ian Fleming used him as a model.

His best-documented role involves the Enigma cipher machine. Germany's armed forces used Enigma to encrypt military messages. Reading that traffic became a central goal for Allied codebreakers.

Poland got there first. Mathematicians at the Polish cipher bureau, including Marian Rejewski, began reading Enigma messages in the early 1930s. Their work also let them build replica machines.

In July 1939, weeks before Germany invaded Poland, the Polish cipher bureau shared that work with its allies. The meeting took place at Pyry, in a forest south of Warsaw. Alastair Denniston and Dilly Knox attended for Britain, and Gustave Bertrand led the French side. The Poles agreed to supply each ally with a replica Enigma machine. Dunderdale is credited with helping get the British machine to London.

That delivery mattered for what followed. British codebreakers at Bletchley Park built on the Polish breakthrough throughout the war. Their decrypts, given the code name Ultra, went to Allied commanders and political leaders.

Dunderdale's post placed him where this exchange came together. The Paris station worked closely with French military intelligence. The French had their own long-running cooperation with the Polish codebreakers.

The German occupation of Paris in June 1940 ended that arrangement in its prewar form. British intelligence could no longer run a station in the French capital.

The Bond connection draws on a different side of his record. Dunderdale had a reputation for polished dress and a stylish manner. That image, rather than any statement by Fleming, is what the claim rests on.

The link remains tradition rather than documented fact. Fleming described Bond as a compound of the secret agents and commando types he met during the war. Dunderdale is one of several intelligence officers whose names have been attached to that description over the years.

Fitzroy Maclean was a Scottish diplomat who became a soldier, served with the SAS in North Africa and led a British mission to Tito's partisans in Yugoslavia. He is often named as an inspiration for James Bond, but no statement from Ian Fleming confirming that link has been verified.

Maclean was born in 1911 and joined the Foreign Office as a young man. A posting to the British embassy in Moscow in the late 1930s placed him in the Soviet capital during Stalin's purges. He attended the 1938 show trial of Nikolai Bukharin. He also made long journeys into Soviet Central Asia, including regions closed to foreigners.

When war came, the Foreign Office would not release him for military service. Maclean found a way around the rule by standing for Parliament. He won the seat of Lancaster as a Conservative in 1941. Leaving the diplomatic service freed him to enlist, and he joined the Cameron Highlanders as a private.

In North Africa he joined the early SAS under David Stirling. He took part in a 1942 raid on the port of Benghazi, deep behind enemy lines. He was later sent to Persia, where he seized General Fazlollah Zahedi, a senior officer suspected of working with the Germans.

In 1943, Winston Churchill sent Maclean to Yugoslavia to work with Josip Broz Tito's partisans. He parachuted in as head of the British liaison mission, holding the rank of brigadier. His reports fed into Britain's decision to back Tito rather than the rival Chetnik movement of Draža Mihailović.

Maclean told the story of these years in his memoir Eastern Approaches, published in 1949. The book moves in three parts from the Soviet Union to the desert war and then to Yugoslavia.

People who make the Bond comparison point to that mix of diplomatic polish, travel through closed territory and raids behind the lines. The idea has circulated for decades as a popular claim rather than a documented one.

After the war Maclean stayed in the House of Commons. He held Lancaster until 1959, then represented Bute and Northern Ayrshire until 1974. He died in 1996.

Christine Granville, born Krystyna Skarbek in Warsaw in 1908, worked for Britain's Special Operations Executive in World War II. She is often suggested as a model for Vesper Lynd in Ian Fleming's Casino Royale, but that link has never been proven.

The Special Operations Executive, usually shortened to SOE, was a British wartime organization formed in 1940. Its job was sabotage and support for resistance movements in occupied Europe. It ran separately from MI6, Britain's foreign intelligence service. Its agents worked behind enemy lines under false identities.

Skarbek served the British under the name Christine Granville, which is the name most English-language accounts use for her. Early in the war she worked as a courier into occupied Poland. Her routes included crossings of the Tatra Mountains on skis.

In 1944 she parachuted into southern France. The drop put her inside German-occupied territory, alongside SOE networks already working there.

Her best-known act came in August 1944 in Digne, in what is now the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence department. The Gestapo was holding three captured men there. Two were Francis Cammaerts, who led an SOE network in southeastern France, and Xan Fielding, a British officer who had earlier served with SOE in Crete.

Granville helped free all three. She negotiated with an official connected to their captors and offered a large bribe. She also warned that Allied forces landing in Provence would hold him responsible for the prisoners' fate.

Britain later awarded her the George Medal and appointed her an OBE. France gave her the Croix de Guerre.

Granville survived the war. In June 1952 she was stabbed to death in a London hotel. Her killer, Dennis Muldowney, had become fixated on her. He was hanged later that year.

In the novel, Vesper Lynd is assigned to help Bond in his mission to beat Le Chiffre, an agent working for the Soviets, at the baccarat table. Bond falls for her. She is later revealed to have been working for the Soviets herself, and she takes her own life.

No documented statement from Fleming confirms that Granville inspired Vesper. The link remains a popular suggestion rather than an established fact.

Betty Pack, born Amy Elizabeth Thorpe in Minneapolis, worked for British Security Coordination in the U.S. under the codename Cynthia. In 1941 she helped British intelligence obtain naval cipher material from the Vichy French embassy in Washington, D.C.

Pack was born in 1910. She was American by birth but entered the world of British diplomacy through marriage. Her husband, Arthur Pack, was a British diplomat. His postings took her abroad in the 1930s, including to Spain and Poland. They gave her access to embassy circles in several capitals.

British Security Coordination, known as BSC, was a British intelligence organization based in New York during World War II. It ran operations across the U.S. while the country was still neutral. Pack's work in Washington fell under that organization.

The Vichy French embassy was a wartime target. The Vichy government collaborated with Germany after the French defeat in 1940. Its diplomats in Washington held material used for French naval communications. Naval cipher material could give British codebreakers a way into those communications.

Pack's route into the embassy ran through Charles Brousse, a press attaché there. She built a close relationship with him. The cipher material was then copied by British intelligence. Pack and Brousse married after the war.

Her methods explain why her name comes up in discussions of Bond. Her approach to gathering information is often compared to the seduction that runs through Ian Fleming's novels. Bond regularly wins over women connected to his targets. Pack's career is sometimes cited as a real-world version of that pattern, with a woman as the agent.

That comparison rests on resemblance, not documented influence. No verified link between Pack and Fleming has been established. Fleming did not name her as a source for his fiction. There is no confirmed record of the two working together. She appears in discussions of Bond's origins because her story fits the pattern of his missions. Fleming never said he drew on it.

William Stephenson, a Canadian from Winnipeg, headed British Security Coordination in New York from 1940. He is often named among the real-life sources for James Bond. That link rests partly on Stephenson's own claims, and it remains contested.

British Security Coordination, usually shortened to BSC, was Britain's main intelligence organization in the U.S. during World War II. It worked to protect British interests in a country that had not yet entered the war. Stephenson ran it from offices in Rockefeller Center in Manhattan. Its work covered intelligence, security and propaganda across the Americas.

Stephenson came to the post from outside the usual civil service path. He flew as a fighter pilot in World War I. Between the wars he built a business career in Britain. That background gave him contacts in industry and government on both sides of the Atlantic.

His connection to the Bond story runs through Ian Fleming's 1941 visit to the U.S. On that trip Fleming met William Donovan, a New York lawyer who later led the Office of Strategic Services. The OSS was the American wartime intelligence agency and a forerunner of the CIA. Fleming wrote a memo for Donovan on how an intelligence service might be organized. Stephenson was a close British ally of Donovan in this period.

The popular image of Stephenson owes much to A Man Called Intrepid, a 1976 book by the journalist William Stevenson. The two men's similar names often cause confusion. The book cast Stephenson as a master of wartime intrigue. It also made "Intrepid" the name popularly attached to him. Many of the book's claims have since been challenged.

Part of the Bond story comes from Stephenson's own accounts of his career. Not all of those accounts have held up. His place among Bond's inspirations is a claim, not a settled fact.

The documented record is narrower. Fleming's 1941 trip came while BSC was operating in a still-neutral U.S. Stephenson was knighted for his wartime service. He died in Bermuda in 1989.

Maxwell Knight ran MI5's agent-running section and placed informants inside Britain's Fascist and Communist groups. Biographer Henry Hemming argues that Knight was a model for M, Bond's boss in Ian Fleming's novels.

Hemming makes that case in his 2017 biography, M: Maxwell Knight, MI5's Greatest Spymaster. Knight was born in 1900 and died in 1968. His section was known inside MI5 as B5(b).

B5(b) recruited people with no intelligence background and placed them inside political organizations. Many stayed in place for years. They reported on members, meetings and outside contacts.

Olga Gray was one of his best-known agents. On Knight's instructions, she joined the Communist Party of Great Britain and gained the trust of its officials. Her reporting helped expose a ring passing secrets from the Woolwich Arsenal. Percy Glading, a Communist Party organizer, was convicted in 1938 as part of that case.

Knight's section also watched the far right. Joan Miller, another of his agents, got inside the Right Club. That pro-Nazi group was founded by the Scottish Unionist MP Archibald Ramsay. Her work fed into the 1940 case against Tyler Kent and Anna Wolkoff. Kent was an American cipher clerk at the U.S. embassy in London. He had copied embassy documents, including correspondence between Winston Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt. Both he and Wolkoff were convicted.

Hemming's case for the M link draws partly on a name. Within MI5, Knight went by the initial M, according to Hemming. The argument also rests on his role as a quiet chief who directed agents from a distance.

Knight had a second public life. He broadcast on natural history for the BBC. He also wrote books about animals and published two crime novels in the 1930s. Those novels were Crime Cargo, published in 1934, and Gunmen's Holiday, published in 1935.

The Knight link to M remains Hemming's interpretation. No verified statement from Fleming confirms that he based M on Knight.

Charles Fraser-Smith made concealed escape and evasion gadgets for the British Ministry of Supply during World War II, including compasses hidden inside ordinary objects. He is commonly named as a real-world model for Q, the equipment specialist in the James Bond stories.

Escape and evasion equipment had a narrow purpose. It was meant to help people stranded behind enemy lines avoid capture or get out of captivity. A compass was a core item in that kit. It let someone on the run hold a direction across unfamiliar country without asking anyone for help.

Concealment was central to the work. A compass discovered during a search would simply be taken away. Fraser-Smith's job was to hide such tools inside items that a soldier, airman or agent might plausibly be carrying anyway.

He described his wartime work in a memoir, The Secret War of Charles Fraser-Smith, published in 1981. The book appeared decades after the war ended. By then, Bond had been a film franchise for nearly two decades.

The link to Q rests on resemblance rather than on any documented statement. Fleming is not on record confirming that Fraser-Smith was the model for the character. The comparison comes from the overlap between a man who built concealed devices and a fictional officer who supplies Bond with them.

Fleming's own novels handle the role differently from the films. In Dr. No, published in 1958, M calls in the Armourer, Major Boothroyd, to replace Bond's Beretta with a Walther PPK. Fleming took that name from Geoffrey Boothroyd, a firearms enthusiast who had written to him criticizing Bond's choice of pistol.

The gadget-heavy Q that most audiences know came largely from the screen. Desmond Llewelyn played the character in films from From Russia with Love in 1963 to The World Is Not Enough in 1999. That version, with its trick briefcases and modified cars, is the one usually set beside Fraser-Smith's wartime inventions.

John Godfrey served as Director of Naval Intelligence at the British Admiralty from 1939 to 1942, with Ian Fleming as his personal assistant. He is often cited as a model for M, the head of the Secret Service in the Bond novels. His claim rests on Fleming's direct service under him.

Godfrey was a career Royal Navy officer. Fleming came to the Admiralty from the City of London, where he had worked in finance. Godfrey recruited him in 1939. The assistant's post gave Fleming a wide view of the division's work, from deception planning to special operations.

One early product of that partnership was the Trout memo. It was a 1939 paper on deceiving the enemy that went out under Godfrey's name. It is widely attributed to Fleming. The memo compared deception to fly fishing and listed dozens of ideas for misleading German forces. One idea, planting false papers on a corpse, foreshadowed the 1943 deception known as Operation Mincemeat.

In 1941 Fleming worked on Operation Goldeneye. It was a contingency plan to keep British intelligence and sabotage work going in Spain. It would have been used if Germany invaded the Iberian Peninsula or Spain joined the Axis. The invasion never came, and the plan was not put into action. Fleming later gave the same name to his house in Jamaica, where he wrote the Bond novels.

Fleming also helped form 30 Assault Unit. It was a commando force whose job was to seize enemy documents, codes and equipment before they could be destroyed. Its teams moved with or ahead of advancing Allied troops. Fleming oversaw the unit largely from London rather than serving in the field.

Godfrey left the intelligence post in 1942 and was succeeded by Edmund Rushbrooke. Godfrey went on to command the Royal Indian Navy. Fleming stayed at the Naval Intelligence Division through the rest of the war.

The Godfrey link does not rest on a memoir or on one biographer's argument, as some other claimed models do. It rests on the years Fleming spent answering to him. Bond's M remains a fictional character, and Godfrey is one of several real figures tied to the role.
