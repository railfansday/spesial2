Tested: 1991 BMW 318i Has a Case of the Wiggles

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

When dressing well is everything, take the Bimmer.

From the August 1991 issue of Car and Driver.

There are two reasons you buy a BMW. One, for refined performance. Two, for grand looks and image and that little pro­peller badge on the edge of the hood. We like BMWs that have a lot of One. But the new-for-1991 318i convertible falls short in refinement, and we therefore think its mis­sion is reason number Two.

The 318i convertible is mechanically identical to BMW’s 318is two-door sedan. Both use a 134-hp twin-cam four-cylinder and a five-speed manual transmission (no automatic is available). The convertible is heavier by more than 250 pounds. The extra weight comes from additional chassis brac­ing and two very thick beams that run along the doorsills for the length of the passenger compartment.

The 318i convertible sells for $29,060, which is nearly $7000 more than the 318is two-door sedan. The soft-top is slower by a second to 60 mph, taking 9.7 seconds. The convertible is also slower in the quarter­-mile by most of a second. Still, the soft-top is not a turtle—it will accelerate about as quickly as a Honda Accord.

The 318’s engine performance and driv­ability are good. The 1.8-liter is enchant­ingly smooth, and it urges you to rev to the redline for every shift. BMW spaced the five gear ratios perfectly for this kind of maximum driving. The flow of power is uninterrupted from a stop to top speed.

Our test car gripped the road as well as the 318is hardtop we tested last September, but there is a big difference in feel between the hardtop and the convertible. Where the hardtop is tight and crisp turning into a cor­ner, the convertible wiggles and twists. Its rear end seems to send waves of jiggles for­ward whenever the pavement turns demanding. This trait pops our BMW refinement bubble—we miss the better ride of the hardtop. And this behavior is surpris­ing, because BMW’s new Wackersdorf assembly plant builds the convertibles as convertibles, not as sedans with their roofs hacked off. That approach usually results in a more rigid chassis.

Wind noise with the top down is accept­ably low, and the standard six-speaker stereo is one of the best. With the unpadded top up, though, normal conversation echoes around inside the car.

Like the 318is, the four-cylinder convertible is intended to be an entry-level BMW. As such, its folding roof is manually operated, and to raise and lower the tight­-fitting top you need to stand outside the car. It’s an easy task to lower the top, and when folded the top lies under a slick hard cover. Raising the top, however, is only gratifying if you bought this convertible for reason number Two. The top fabric is color-matched to the car’s paint and draws a lot of stares.

For about $7500 more, you can get the six-cylinder 325i convertible with a power top. In this price league, that extra conve­nience is worth it. BMW evidently believes the same, because it plans to sell twice as many power-top six-cylinder convertibles as it does four-cylinder 318i convertibles.
