The Weakest Storyline in the Red Rising Trilogy Is Even Worse 10 Years Later

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Pierce Brown's Red Rising trilogy is among the best works of science fiction produced in the 2010s. Starting as a classic take on the YA genre that dominated the early half of the decade, the series soon evolved into something more mature, a harsh but ultimately cathartic depiction of space war that effortlessly blended large-scale mayhem with a more coming-of-age arc — a galactic bildungsroman, if you will.

A huge portion of the story concerns the blossoming, complicated romance between protagonist Darrow and Virginia "Mustang" au Augustus. The two spend most of the trilogy trying to determine the nature of their feelings for each other, ultimately marrying and starting a family. Yet, the relationship has always felt incomplete at best and outright unconvincing at worst, especially when revisiting the trilogy. To make matters worse, there is another potential romantic partner for Darrow who would've made more sense and been more in line with the trilogy's larger themes about growing up and embracing change and opportunity.

The Red Rising trilogy is set in a futuristic version of the Solar System ruled by The Society, a government system that divides everyone into colors depending on their role. At the top are the Golds, the undisputed rulers of the pyramid; at the very bottom are the Reds, slaves who perform the lowest and most dangerous tasks. The main story centers on Darrow, a Red helldiver from Mars whose wife is killed by the archgovernor of Mars, Nero au Augustus. Presumed dead, Darrow is instead recruited into the Sons of Ares, a rebellious faction seeking to overthrow The Society. Genetically modified and turned into a Gold, Darrow is infiltrated into Gold society to sabotage them from within.

The first book, Red Rising, sees Darrow enrolling in the Institute, where teen Golds go to learn the art of war and the ruthlessness of The Society. There, Darrow meets a young girl whom he nicknames Mustang, developing deep feelings for her after she saves his life following an attack. Mustang turns out to be Nero's daughter, the first complication in their dynamic. To continue his plan, Darrow enters Nero's service after The Institute, eventually kickstarting a civil war on Mars in the second book, Golden Son. The book ends with Darrow revealing his Red identity to a shocked Mustang, who feels justifiably betrayed by his deception.

In the third book, Morning Star, Darrow escapes from captivity and takes leadership of the Sons of Ares. The Solar System is now in open war, as multiple factions are in rebellion against The Society and its Sovereign, Octavia au Lune. Once again, Darrow and Mustang ally to fight the common enemy, eventually (finally, more like it) getting together — for real, this time. Their status before this book is both complicated and, more frustratingly, unclear, which is the main reason why their relationship feels incomplete.

One of the biggest issues in the Red Rising trilogy is how granular Brown gets with some sequences — particularly the ambitious and admittedly immersive action set pieces — at the expense of some of the big-picture worldbuilding and character arcs. Nowhere is that flaw more apparent than in the Darrow-Mustang relationship. Red Rising does a solid job laying the foundations of their eventual romance, although it's far more concerned with The Institute's war game and Darrow's ascent to become Primus.

However, the real problem happens in Golden Son, which takes place two years after Red Rising. In that time, Darrow and Mustang's connection has deepened, and they have even been intimate — that is to say, they fall in love off the page. From there, Brown tells us about their relationship rather than showing us, a huge point against the believability of the romance. It doesn't help that Darrow is haunted by the memory of Eo, his dead wife and the main reason why he wants to overthrow the Golds. There's also the little detail that Darrow is lying to Mustang about his identity, always keeping her at arm's length out of fear of her finding out his Red nature. His fears eventually prove right, as Mustang reacts with justifiable shock, hurt, and apprehension.

It's precisely here that the core issue with Darrow and Mustang lies. Their relationship is based on a lie, with Darrow constantly guilty and afraid, and Mustang second-guessing everything because she can sense his reluctance to open up. There's also a huge trust chasm between the two that extends to both their role as leaders in the war and their more intimate dynamic with each other. The truth is, for most of the saga, Darrow and Mustang don't trust each other, and thus can't fully trust the validity or nature of their feelings. By the time they actually get together at the end of Morning Star, it doesn't feel earned or even triumphant; rather, it feels like the end of an unnecessarily long race where the contestant limped towards the finish line, crossing it out of duty rather than genuine desire.

Red Rising is hardly the only sci-fi epic with a weak central romance. After all, many authors struggle to balance the intricate worldbuilding needed to bring these tales to life with the more intimate quality that makes any successful romance work. For example, and for all its many, many strengths, Frank Herbert's Dune and the Harry Potter saga also arguably fail in the romance department. However, what makes the Red Rising central couple feel weaker is the fact that there is another, equally iconic character who would've made far more sense as Darrow's partner.

Victra au Julii is one of Darrow's fiercest and most staunchly loyal allies, harboring an open and unrequited crush on him. Because Darrow's love for Mustang is so unconvincing, his rejection of Victra feels puzzling and outright frustrating. See, Brown actually spends time developing Darrow and Victra's bond, largely because it comes as a result of their time in battle — because their connection is born in the heat of war, we actually get to see it blossoming.

There's also the fact that Victra's love for Darrow was never dependent on his status as a Gold, his deeds, or his status as the Reaper. She has no particular reaction to learning that he was a Red because she always saw the person, not the color or the title. Her loyalty is to him, born out of respect, admiration, love, and an appreciation for his tactical mind and resourceful nature. Moreover, while Darrow and Mustang's bond feels youthful and hopeful, Darrow and Victra's feels mature and solid: they've been through hell and back together, and not only survived but thrived. Sadly, Victra ends up with Sevro, one of Brown's most head-scratching developments.

In the end, the Darrow-Mustang romance feels more forced than anything, a remnant of the saga's origins as YA fiction, as if Brown left them together because he felt like he needed to, rather than because it made the most sense. Many stories do the same, especially if they think the fans will not accept the protagonist with anyone else — indeed, the concept of an "endgame" couple has caused much damage to far too many stories. Red Rising is one such example. Darrow and Mustang might be the safe, expected choice, but Darrow and Victra feel organic and are far more interesting. And in a story as ambitious as this one, "interesting" is always better than "safe."
