AI agents are hunting down forgotten subscriptions — and saving people money

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

AI agents are canceling all the subscriptions that shoppers forgot to end. Some merchants are taking action and blocking attempts to do so

Nashville resident Scott Burka had been paying for his kids' iPad subscriptions and chess accounts without noticing. Muse, an AI agent Meta $META released in September to handle digital chores, found the charges. Burka told the agent he wasn't using any of them, "and it actually helped me cancel approximately $100 worth of expenses a month that were completely just under the radar," he told NPR. That's about $1,200 a year Burka had been spending without realizing it, or nearly two-thirds of what an average American spent on their entire subscription bill last year, according to Mastercard $MA and FT Strategies.

Burka isn't the only one. Other Muse users have handed the agent their bank and credit card statements and used it to cancel subscriptions they no longer needed. Each of those cancellations cuts off money businesses had long collected from forgetful customers. For subscription sellers, the money adds up. Stanford economists Liran Einav, Ben Klopack, and Neale Mahoney estimated in a 2025 paper in the American Economic Review that sellers take in roughly twice as much money, on average, as they would if customers never put off canceling.

Whether a Muse user can stop those charges depends on the seller. Lawyers at Venable, a firm that advises subscription companies on cancellation rules, wrote last week that no federal or state law says whether a company has to accept a cancellation request an AI agent sends on behalf of a customer. Amazon $AMZN has already barred Muse from buying anything on its site, saying the agent broke Amazon's terms of service. A subscription seller could try to block Muse from canceling the same way, and no law clearly says it can't.

Once an AI agent can read someone's email or bank statements, it can find every subscription the person is paying for, lay them out in one list, and cancel whichever ones the person no longer wants. Jeetu Patel, Cisco $CSCO's president and chief product officer, told Business Insider last month that a personal AI agent, on its first pass through his inbox, found he was paying $9,000 a year in subscriptions. Patel asked it for a table showing what each one cost and how long he'd had it, chose two he didn't need, and told the agent to drop them. It canceled both and forwarded him the confirmations. "I'm like, OK, that was kind of creepy how easy it was to do that," Patel said. "And then it saved me 1,000, 1,200 bucks a year just over there." The agent costs him $20 a month, or $240 a year, so those two cancellations alone cover it four to five times over.

Agents also catch charges their owners meant to cancel and forgot about. A Silicon Valley correspondent for Maeil Business Newspaper, a South Korean daily, wrote in September about paying Otter, an AI service that writes up meeting notes, $99 a year for several years. In 2025, the correspondent decided to cancel before the next renewal date, then forgot, and Otter charged another $99 in June. Muse flagged that charge once the correspondent linked a Bank of America $BAC account. The correspondent asked it once to cancel, and Muse took care of everything else, without the correspondent ever visiting Otter's site or hunting for a cancel button.

Sellers of digital services stand to lose the most once agents start turning up forgotten charges. Mahoney, the Stanford economist, points out that people rarely forget they're paying for pet food, because a new bag shows up on the doorstep every month. A credit-monitoring service sends nothing to the door, and it can keep billing customers long after they've forgotten it. The quiet monthly bill from a digital service is exactly the kind of charge an agent turns up.

The agents, in other words, haven't set off a wave of cancellations, at least not yet. That leaves both sides where they started: Muse users keep what they save, and sellers like the Times can still decide whether to let the agents in at all.

Sellers already have the means to make that choice. Kopit Levien told analysts on the same call that the Times has "the ability to permit, to restrict, or to block specific tools." Sellers that shut agents out could lose new customers, though. Jordan Mackler, co-founder and CEO of ScribeUp, which builds subscription-management tools into banking apps, said, "If consumers trust that they can easily see, manage, and cancel recurring charges, our data shows they are likely to expand their overall recurring spend and are more willing to try new services in the first place." A seller that blocks agents protects the forgotten charges it already collects but gives up some of the sign-ups that easy canceling brings in.

No federal rule settles the question. In 2025, a federal appeals court threw out the Federal Trade Commission's (FTC) 2024 rule, which would have required sellers to offer a simple way to cancel and stop every recurring charge right away. In February, the FTC restored its 1973 rule, which covers only merchandise plans that mail members a notice of each upcoming shipment. When the FTC asked the public in March whether to bring back parts of the 2024 rule, lawyers at the firm Bryan Cave Leighton Paisner wrote in Legal 500 that the agency had taken only the first step of a process that takes years. In the meantime, the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act, a federal law, still makes online sellers give customers a "simple" way to end billing, but it never says what simple means, let alone whether a cancellation sent by an agent counts.
