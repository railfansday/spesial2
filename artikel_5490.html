James Webb detects 'forbidden' H₃⁺ light that physicists predicted 40 years ago

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Webb found H₃⁺'s forbidden rotational light, 40 years after it was predicted. Nine slides trace the proof and the open brightness puzzle.

Some of the hardest things in astronomy to see belong to objects that scientists already know well. The molecule H₃⁺ was never hidden from astronomers, and they had long observed the light it gives off as it vibrates, according to arXiv. One specific kind of its light was missing: the light it should give off as it rotates. This is a story about confirming how a familiar molecule behaves, not about finding a new one. Astronomers already knew H₃⁺ was out there. What they lacked was proof that it does something physicists had calculated it should do.

A prediction can be physically sound and still be impossible to test until instruments improve. The missing light lies in the mid-infrared, according to Neufeld et al. Astronomers had no instrument sensitive enough to catch such faint light there until the James Webb Space Telescope detected it. The long wait came from the lack of a tool able to do the job. That makes the detection a test of something larger than one molecule. It tests whether physicists can trust calculations made with no observation behind them, decades after they did the work.

Clarín traced how Webb's discovery unfolded, from the molecule itself to the proof. The account covers the molecule's odd triangular build, a second object in space that showed the same light, and the calculation that first showed a way around the rule keeping that light hidden. Every step had to work for the proof to arrive. Scientists needed a real physical way around the rule, a telescope sensitive enough to register the light, and a place in space with conditions violent enough for the light to be seen. Even now, the proof settles only part of the question. Astronomers know the light exists, but they cannot yet explain why it is as strong as they measured it.

The molecule at the center of this discovery contains nothing but hydrogen. Its formal name is the trihydrogen cation, according to the Department of Energy. Its three hydrogen atoms sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle, according to Clarín. A shape that even and that small suggests a molecule with equally simple habits.

H₃⁺ has a reputation far larger than its three atoms. It is often called "the molecule that made the universe," but that phrase is popular shorthand, not a scientific label. The nickname does point to a real role. H₃⁺ helps form water and organic molecules in space, according to the Department of Energy. A part in building molecules that matter this much makes any light from the ion worth chasing.

Chemists are still working out how even this three-atom ion is put together. In the lab, the triangle can form when a roaming neutral hydrogen molecule takes one more hydrogen atom, the Department of Energy reports, in research whose contact researcher is Marcos Dantus of Michigan State University. The researchers studied that route in laser-excited organic molecules on a lab bench, not in space. The work shows one indirect way to build H₃⁺, but it does not show how the ion forms between the stars. H₃⁺ is simple to draw, but it is not simple in how it behaves.

H₃⁺ matters to astronomers because it changes the clouds it sits in, not simply because it is present there. Clarín reports that the ion works as a coolant in interstellar clouds, the gas that fills the space between stars. In that role H₃⁺ takes an active part in cooling those clouds instead of simply drifting through them. That makes the ion a working piece of the clouds rather than a passive trace of something else. Astronomers who study H₃⁺ therefore also learn about the gas around it, not only about the ion itself.

Cooling is not the job that made H₃⁺ famous. The ion is often called "the molecule that made the universe," and that reputation comes from its part in forming organic molecules, not from its role in carrying away heat. Those are two separate kinds of work. One concerns which molecules can form in a cloud, and the other concerns how that cloud cools. A single ion made of three hydrogen atoms takes part in both, even though only one of them gave it a famous name.

A molecule that does this much in the clouds between the stars is worth observing in every kind of light it gives off, and each kind that remains unseen leaves a gap in what astronomers know about those clouds.

"Forbidden" does not mean impossible here. It means the light H₃⁺ gives off as it spins is so weak that it almost never shows up. The cause lies in how evenly the molecule is built. Its three hydrogen atoms sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle, and it has no permanent dipole moment, according to Clarín. A molecule needs that kind of built-in electrical imbalance to give off light freely while it rotates, and H₃⁺ has none. According to arXiv, its forbidden lines come from centrifugal distortion, in which the molecule bends slightly out of its even shape as it spins. The light is real, but it comes from the motion rather than from the molecule at rest, and light produced only as a side effect of spinning is weak.

Scientists working in laboratories failed to record this light, just as astronomers did. arXiv reports that before Webb, no laboratory experiment and no astronomical source had ever recorded the rotational spectrum of H₃⁺. The gap extended to experiments on Earth as well as observations of the sky. That suggests the faintness of the signal, and not only the reach of telescopes, stood in the way. Better instruments were part of the answer, but astronomers also needed to know exactly what light a warped, spinning H₃⁺ should produce, so that they would recognize it in the data.

The search could begin only after someone worked out on paper the precise light that the spinning molecule should give off.

The way around the rule that kept H₃⁺'s rotation dark came from calculation, years before any instrument could test it. In 1986, University of Chicago astrophysicists Fu-Shih Pan and Takeshi Oka worked out that loophole on paper, Clarín reports, and the physics behind it is a real effect of spin. A fast-spinning H₃⁺ molecule stretches slightly out of its even triangular shape, and in that stretched form it carries a small electrical imbalance that lets it emit light as it rotates, according to Neufeld et al. Pan and Oka saw their result as a new way to find the molecule between the stars, not as a point of theory to file away. From then on, astronomers had a specific kind of light to search for, even though they had no means yet to catch it.

The stretching does more than make the light possible. It also lets the molecule give off that light readily by the standards of forbidden light, which made the prediction worth pursuing. The forbidden H₃⁺ lines reach emission rates of up to about 0.5 per second, according to Neufeld et al., while the faint quadrupole lines of ordinary hydrogen molecules reach only about 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁷ per second. That rate measures how readily a molecule gives off a given kind of light. By that measure, a spinning H₃⁺ molecule emits its forbidden light far more readily than ordinary hydrogen emits its own faint lines. The 1986 loophole therefore points to a real, usable signal rather than a technicality that yields almost nothing.

Strong for its kind still did not mean easy to see, and a prediction that was sound on paper left astronomers waiting.

Most predictions in physics meet a test within a few years, but this one went four decades with no answer. Nobody recorded the light in the 40 years between the 1986 prediction and its detection by the James Webb Space Telescope, according to Clarín. Scientists missed the signal in laboratories as well as in space, so the gap was not only a matter of waiting for a better telescope. With no lab measurement to lean on, researchers had no way to check the prediction on Earth while they waited for a sighting in space. For all those years, the claim stood on nothing but a worked-out calculation, with no observation anywhere to support it or count against it.

A calculation that no one can check carries only the weight of the people who did it. In this case, those people were Fu-Shih Pan and Takeshi Oka, astrophysicists at the University of Chicago. That made the prediction a specialists' result rather than a speculative guess. The failure in the labs also shapes how to read the long wait. Experiments on Earth came up empty too, so the decades without a sighting reflect how hard the signal is to catch. They do not show that anyone found a fault in the calculation itself.

The wait ended with Webb, and with a careful choice of where to point it.

The forbidden light first turned up in the turbulent gas around a newborn star, not in the calm space between stars. Astronomers spotted the faint H₃⁺ lines in James Webb Space Telescope data from Herbig-Haro 168, or HH 168, according to Clarín. Even there, with the most sensitive infrared instrument available trained on the object, the lines were faint. Astronomers needed a source that gave off enough of this light for Webb to register it. A quieter patch of gas would have given them even less to record.

Webb's sensitivity alone did not make the detection possible. Seeing the light took an extremely sensitive infrared telescope together with the hot, chaotic conditions that a newborn star creates, and HH 168 had those conditions. That reverses the usual picture of a discovery, in which a better instrument simply lets astronomers see more of the sky. Here, the conditions in HH 168 counted toward the discovery as much as the engineering behind the telescope did. The light is bright enough to record only in such violent surroundings. Astronomers needed the telescope and the turbulent gas at the same time, and neither would have been enough without the other.

For light this faint, what astronomers observed mattered as much as what they observed it with.

The forbidden light counts as proof because the people who found it in the sky were not the people who predicted it. ScienceAlert reports that researchers led by David Neufeld of Johns Hopkins University studied the James Webb Space Telescope's observations of Herbig-Haro 168. The lines they found match the ones that Fu-Shih Pan and Takeshi Oka of the University of Chicago had calculated, according to Clarín. Pan and Oka finished their work long before Webb recorded anything, so nothing the telescope later saw could have shaped their prediction. One group at one university made the prediction, and a separate group at another university found the light. That separation gives the agreement its weight. When a telescope's readings match numbers that another team worked out on paper long before the telescope existed, coincidence becomes the explanation that is hard to defend.

The match settles one question: H₃⁺ gives off forbidden rotational light, and the lines appear where the theory placed them. How certain the finding is remains a separate question, and its answer comes from statistics rather than from the match itself. The physics also stays open, because lines in the predicted places do not explain how strong those lines are. Each of those questions needs its own evidence, and agreement with an old calculation supplies neither.

The light was where the arithmetic said it would be, and the remaining work is to explain why it shines with such strength.

A faint signal that scientists already hoped to find invites a fair suspicion that they read it into random noise in the data. The brightest of the forbidden H₃⁺ lines answers that suspicion, because it reaches as high as 72 sigma, according to Clarín. Sigma is a measure of how far a signal sits above the random noise in a set of observations. A result that high means this line is not a fluke of messy data. The measurement belongs to the group at Johns Hopkins University that David Neufeld led, the same researchers who checked Webb's lines against the 1986 calculation.

The 72 sigma figure describes the strongest line, not every line the researchers found. That line is one of 26 lines the team identified in total, and the others are fainter. A single sharp line could be a coincidence. A full set of identified lines is far harder to explain away when the strongest of them stands so far above the noise and the rest fit the same pattern. The fainter lines also gain weight from the strong one, because all of them come from the same molecule in the same observations.

Scientists no longer have to accept H₃⁺'s forbidden light on trust in a calculation, because they now have a measurement of it.

A detection from a single object leaves room to suspect that something odd about that one place produced it. Clarín reports that the H₃⁺ discovery also involves a second target, Herbig-Haro 211, or HH 211, another Herbig-Haro object of the same kind as HH 168. With two sites in the picture, the result no longer rests on one patch of sky. The more useful question becomes what the two places have in common, and that shared trait is where any explanation should begin.

What the two objects share is violence. In both, shock waves have struck the gas instead of leaving it at rest, and that shocked gas reaches thousands of degrees. That heat ties the forbidden light to the most energetic parts of these outflows. It also gives astronomers a physical reason to expect the light in shocked regions and not in calmer gas.

Shock heating explains where the light appears, but current models of shocked gas cannot yet fully reproduce how bright the light is. The gap concerns brightness alone, and the detection itself is not in question. The prediction made on paper decades ago has passed its test, and the light is real. What sets that light at its observed strength is not yet fully understood.

The next task for astronomers is to build models of shocked gas that match the brightness Webb recorded.
