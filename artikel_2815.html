F1 bosses agree V8 engines deal from 2030

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Formula 1 bosses have reached a deal that means V8 turbo hybrid engines will replace the current controversial units in 2030.

A statement from governing body the FIA on Friday said that all F1's power-unit manufacturers had "committed" to a proposal for a 2.1-litre V8 turbo engine with a smaller electrical element than this year.

Plans for this specific engine, and its associated turbo and hybrid architecture were first revealed by BBC Sport last month.

The FIA said the use of "advanced sustainable fuels", which were first used this year, would continue and the engine would be "designed to be lighter, simpler, and more cost-effective than ever".

Some details remain to be resolved but the core of the rules has been agreed.

The electrical element will be for a maximum power output of 200kW - compared with 350kW this year.

But only half of that will be available at all times, with the remaining half used for an overtake mode.

A key element of the rules will be to remove the issue of energy starvation that has been criticised by the drivers this year for taking away "pure" racing.

The overall power output of the engines will be similar to now.

The combustion engine will have about 780bhp, while 200kW is 270bhp.

That means a total power output of 1,050bhp in overtake mode or just over 900bhp in standard running.

FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem said: "This agreement marks a significant milestone in shaping the future of Formula 1. From the outset I have been clear in my ambition to deliver a sport that is more competitive and more exciting for our fans, while remaining at the forefront of innovation and sustainability.

"The return of V8 engines represents an opportunity to reconnect with the DNA of this championship, bringing back the sound, emotion and pure racing that is loved worldwide.

"Our vision is clear, lighter, simpler and more affordable cars that put driver skill at the heart of the competition.

"I want to thank all involved for their collaboration. This is an important step forward, and demonstrates what we can achieve when we work together to deliver the best possible future for our sport."

McLaren Racing chief executive officer Zak Brown said: "Everyone is very, very excited. I think everyone will be, [for] the power units. But there's a lot more also going on to continue to evolve the sport. So, between the FIA and Formula 1, they're doing a great job."

The ruleset amounts to a compromise reached by manufacturers after Ben Sulayem initially pushed for a naturally aspirated engine without turbo or electrical components.

He first wanted a naturally aspirated V10, a proposal that was rejected by manufacturers in March last year.

He switched to a V8, but the manufacturers were keen to retain both turbochargers and a hybrid element, and have spent much of this year working towards an agreement.

This coalesced into a proposal document that was presented to manufacturers before last month's US Grand Prix, and was discussed and fundamentally agreed in meetings last week.

Having a turbo was important to reduce overall fuel consumption, and therefore weight at the start of races.

In the wider context, that along with the electrical element ensured the engines would remain relevant to road cars.

The plan means Ben Sulayem's desire for a return to the loud volumes of the previous V8 era from 2006 to 2013 will likely not be achieved. The engines are expected to sound not dissimilar to now.

This was important because a significant increase in engine noise would have put at risk some races in city centres commercially important to F1, such as Miami and Las Vegas.

However, the move marks an acceptance that the engine rules introduced this year, with their near 50/50 split between internal combustion and electrical power, has been a mistake.

The split, combined with other choices to do with car architecture, has meant the engines are short of energy and power often tails off before the end of straights as the batteries run of out charge and the electrical motors reverse to recharge them.

Drivers have labelled this "anti-racing", a phrase that also incorporates the new phenomenon of cars regularly swapping positions as a result of differing charge levels.

The 2.1-litre capacity has been arrived at because it amounts to adding to two cylinders to the current 1.6-litre V6 blocks, with the aim of reducing development costs.

Cars are expected to start races with about 110-120kg of fuel, up from about 100kg. A weight limit of 150kg for the power units will be imposed, down by about 30kg from the current engines.

There will be a fuel energy flow limit to keep the power of the combustion engine under control, and a budget cap figure for engines set at $190m (£145m) per year.

The target for overall car weight is to reduce it to less than 700kg from the current 768kg, with driver but without fuel.

As part of negotiations, General Motors, backers of the Cadillac team, will delay the production of its new engine until 2030. It had been scheduled for 2029.

In return, GM will lose its voting rights on current engine rules and be classified as a new manufacturer from 2030.

The team will continue to use Ferrari customer engines through 2029.

Cadillac F1 chief executive officer Dan Towriss said: "For a period of time, we were working on both. So, they've already got a V6 base to build off of, and working towards the V8.

"There are a number of designs that are in motion now, and so there's a lot of good foundation for what we're trying to build and the direction we're headed in."
