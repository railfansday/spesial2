Your Deal Didn’t Stall. You Were Pitching the Wrong Person.

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Bottom-up adoption breaks down wherever a purchase needs more than one signature, and founders who rely on a single enthusiastic user keep losing deals to buying committees they never mapped.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

Every founder knows this call. The demo went perfectly, the person who would use your product is thrilled and she promises to push it through. Then the deal goes quiet for three months. Founders blame inertia or a weak champion.

More often, the person who loved the product was never the person who could buy it, and nobody had mapped the people who could.

This is the limit of standard startup advice: find the user in pain, win their advocacy

and let them pull the product inside. That bottom-up motion works where users control the budget. It fails wherever a purchase has to clear finance, IT, legal and operations. I learned this early in my career selling orthopedic medical devices, where a surgeon could love an implant and the hospital still would not buy it. Since then, I have watched the same mistake stall more good products, in more industries, than competitors ever did.

Most startups spend their time with the User Buyer and assume she’s also their Coach. The way to tell is access. A Coach gets you in front of the CFO, tells you when procurement reviews the contract and warns you about last quarter’s objection from finance. Someone who loves your product but won’t do those things is a fan, and fans don’t close deals.

The room is bigger than most founders assume. A Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers, published in May 2025, found buying groups ranging from five to 16 people across as many as four functions and 74% of those groups showed unhealthy conflict: members with conflicting objectives, disagreement on the best course, or decisions overruled by outsiders. Forrester’s 2026 research puts the typical buying decision at 13 internal stakeholders and nine external influencers.

Healthcare is an extreme case, not an exception. In a 2025 HIMSS survey of 211 health IT buyers, 57% said five or more people take part in technology purchases, and 23% said ten or more. Hospitals add value analysis committees on top. IQVIA reported in 2023, based on a survey of committee members, that even after approval only about one third of products are cleared for broad use. The rest must prove themselves in a trial, and Forrester found that more than 60% of business buyers across industries now use a trial before they buy.

The Challenger Sale by Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson is the best playbook I know for this. Across more than 6,000 reps, those who challenged customer thinking made up nearly 40% of star performers and 54% in complex sales. Relationship builders made up 7%. Being liked won’t get you through a committee.

Almost nobody does this work. Our 2026 State of B2B Go-to-Market report, which surveyed 511 B2B professionals, found that roughly 24% of go-to-market spending goes to initiatives with no traceable commercial outcome. Selling medical devices taught me that the market isn’t irrational. The map most teams carry into it is incomplete. You can keep building for the person who loves your product and wonder why the deal stalls in a room you never entered, or you can find out who holds the budget and what they are measured on. Most teams don’t have a pipeline problem. They have a buyer map problem.
