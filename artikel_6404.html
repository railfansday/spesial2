Nearly one million hospital stays reveal a surprising pattern in patient survival

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Patients treated by female doctors had lower death rates in a study of nearly one million hospital stays, with the greatest difference among female patients.

When you need hospital care, you usually don’t get to choose your doctor. Who looks after you often depends on the hospital’s rotating schedule.

A study of nearly a million hospital stays in Ontario found that patients were less likely to die within 30 days of admission when a woman led their care.

That finding shows a link, but it doesn’t prove that the doctor’s sex caused the difference.

Angela Jerath, a scientist at ICES, an independent health research institute in Ontario, led the study. She is also an associate professor of anesthesiology at the University of Toronto’s Temerty Faculty of Medicine.

“Past research has largely focused on surgical patients and the admitting physician only,” Jerath said.

“Our findings add to the growing body of research examining the relationship between physician sex and patient outcomes, broadening the evidence beyond surgical care to a wider range of medical conditions and other physicians.”

The researchers studied health records for 958,150 adults admitted to Ontario hospitals between 2008 and 2022. Each patient had one of seven common conditions.

These included heart attacks, heart failure, strokes, and pneumonia.

The team also included patients with bleeding in the digestive tract, a long-term lung disease that makes breathing difficult, or an irregular heartbeat called atrial fibrillation.

When patients or referring doctors choose a physician, that doctor’s patients may differ in ways medical records don’t capture.

Researchers call this referral bias, and Jerath said assigning hospital doctors through rotating schedules helps limit it.

Those schedules also mean that several doctors may care for one patient. A typical hospital stay for a medical illness lasts about five days, so a patient might see a male doctor one day and a female doctor the next.

The researchers accounted for these changes by measuring care in two ways. First, they identified each patient’s main doctor, meaning the physician responsible for the most days of their stay.

They also calculated how much of the hospital stay female doctors managed. This let them look beyond the doctor who first admitted the patient.

Of the 15,165 doctors in the study, about 36% were women. A woman served as the main doctor for 259,759 patients, a little more than a quarter of the total.

Before comparing deaths, the team adjusted for patients’ age, income, frailty, and other illnesses. They also accounted for doctors’ age, experience, and workload.

After those adjustments, an estimated 12.3% of patients whose main doctor was female died within 30 days. Among patients whose main doctor was male, the figure was 13.7%.

Each additional 10% of a hospital stay managed by female doctors was associated with about 7% lower odds of dying within 30 days.

Both measures pointed to the same result, which the authors listed as a strength of the study.

The lower death rate appeared in six of the seven illnesses, in teaching and community hospitals, and among patients of both family doctors and specialists. Patients of female doctors were also slightly less likely to return to the hospital within 30 days.

Earlier US research involving older Medicare patients found a similar pattern. That included a 2017 study of patients treated by hospital internists, doctors who specialize in medical care for adults.

The difference was larger among female patients. Their 30-day death rate was about 1.3 percentage points lower when a woman was their main doctor, compared with about 0.75 percentage points among male patients.

Female patients treated by female doctors had the lowest 30-day death rate of any patient-doctor pairing in the study.

A 2024 US study of Medicare patients also found a larger benefit for women treated by female doctors. The Ontario researchers said their finding raises a question for future research, rather than explaining why this happens.

Patients with female main doctors continued to have lower odds of dying over longer periods, but the gap narrowed. Their odds of death were about 12% lower at 30 days, 8% lower at 90 days, and 4% lower at one year.

The pattern for hospital readmissions changed too. By 90 days, patients of male and female main doctors had returned to the hospital at similar rates.

By one year, patients of female doctors were slightly more likely to have been readmitted. Their initial hospital stays also lasted about 5% longer.

Atrial fibrillation was the exception to the early survival finding. For patients with that condition, neither measure showed a link between the doctor’s sex and deaths within 30 days.

The study couldn’t tell the researchers why patients of female doctors had better survival rates. Medical records don’t reveal how much time a doctor spent at the bedside or what the doctor and patient discussed.

Earlier research cited by the authors offers some possible explanations. Female doctors may spend more time with patients, involve them more in decisions, and show more empathy.

They may also order appropriate tests and prescribe treatments that follow clinical guidelines more often.

Some studies suggest that female doctors take a more cautious approach to treatment and seek advice from colleagues more frequently.

“The goal is not to favor one group of physicians over another but to identify practices and behaviors that may help improve care across the health system,” Jerath said.

The researchers used the sex doctors reported when registering to practice, which doesn’t capture gender.

They had no information about ethnicity or disability, or about how trainees and other team members contributed to care.

The female doctors were generally younger, had less experience, and treated fewer patients each year than the male doctors. Their patients were slightly older and frailer.

The team adjusted for those differences, but medical records can only tell part of the story. Factors the researchers couldn’t measure might still explain some of the gap.

They also couldn’t account for handoffs between doctors, differences in how doctors practiced, or expectations about workload and pay.

The study covered people admitted to hospitals, so its findings don’t apply to patients treated in clinics.

The researchers called for closer examination of how doctors deliver care, including their decisions about tests, treatments, and communication with patients.

They also recommended studying hospitals in other countries to see whether the same pattern holds.

Understanding what drives these differences could help improve hospital care for everyone.

The study is published in the journal JAMA Network Open.
