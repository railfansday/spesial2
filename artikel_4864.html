Pregnant, but Think You Might Already Have Postpartum?

With heightened attention on postpartum depression, anxiety, and psychosis, a critical truth has been missed: A significant percentage of “postpartum” mental health disorders actually begin in pregnancy.

Often termed PMADs, Perinatal Mood and Anxiety Disorders encompass depressive, anxiety, and bipolar disorders. (The latter is often linked to postpartum psychosis—a rare and poorly treated variant.) The perinatal or peripartum period means “surrounding birth,” and includes the entire pregnancy and year after.

Research suggests that a third of perinatal depression cases begin during pregnancy. Anxiety disorder symptoms during early pregnancy are actually more prevalent than postpartum, with a quarter of pregnant women experiencing anxiety in early pregnancy, dropping to 20 percent during the remaining peripartum. Perinatal obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is experienced by about 8 percent of women during pregnancy, and about twice that number experience it postpartum. New-onset bipolar is rare in the peripartum period, but over 50 percent of women who have bipolar before pregnancy experience at least one episode peri-partally.

Women and their loved ones need to be aware of the risk of early-onset PMADs. Frequently, women have been struggling with anxiety, depression, OCD, or bipolar symptoms for weeks or months before birth, but no one has inquired and women may feel anxious or embarrassed to mention them.

Often, mild PMADs can be treated with individual or group psychotherapy and lifestyle changes like sleep improvement, stress reduction, and social support. But a large proportion of women experiencing PMADs have moderate-to-severe symptoms requiring medication, which many fear or avoid, but shouldn’t because medication saves lives. In the United States alone, the largest causes of maternal death—20 percent—are suicide or overdose linked to PMADs.

Here’s what can help you or a woman you love get the treatment needed:

If you’re pregnant or even considering pregnancy and are also experiencing anxiety, depression, or bipolar symptoms, talk to your provider and find the help you need.

Learn more about psychiatric disorders during pregnancy, and medications during pregnancy, so you’re primed with knowledge. You can even help women following in your footsteps by signing up for the national pregnancy medication registry and contributing to improved medication knowledge for all women, long into the future.
