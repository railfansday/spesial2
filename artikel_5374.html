4 CDN benefits and the shared network risk behind each one

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A CDN can speed up, protect and cut the cost of a website. Here are 4 benefits and what owners give up by relying on a shared network.

Most people who open a website picture their request going straight to that site's own computer, wherever it sits. In practice, many sites hand their traffic to outside companies that serve pages for many other sites at the same time. That arrangement puts a company the reader never chose between the reader and the page. Sites that rely on the same company also rely on that company's network staying up, so a problem inside that network does not belong to one site alone.

A content delivery network, or CDN, adds a dependency without removing one. It cannot replace web hosting, because the original files still sit on the site's own server. The hosting layer is concentrated as well. Two companies, Amazon $AMZN Web Services and Microsoft $MSFT Azure, receive roughly 60% to 70% of U.K. cloud spending, according to Ofcom estimates reported by CNBC. Many sites therefore depend on two layers run by outside companies: the delivery network that serves readers and the host that stores the files. Sharing is part of how a CDN works. Providers pool capacity so each customer can use far more than it could build alone, and the same pooling ties many sites to the same network. Every benefit a site owner buys from a CDN comes with that trade between what the site gains and how much more it relies on a network someone else runs.

Cloudflare set out to identify the main benefits website owners get from using a CDN, from blocking attacks to cutting hosting bills to keeping pages fast. It says CDNs deliver the majority of web traffic. The company also sells CDN services, so its case is a seller's account of what its own product does. That is a reason to test each benefit, not to dismiss it. Cloudflare also protects roughly 20% of the web, according to BleepingComputer, so the benefits it describes and the risk of many sites leaning on one company come from the same network. Each slide that follows takes one benefit and asks two questions: what it really does for a site, and what the site gives up by getting it through a network it shares with much of the web.

Readers get a website's pages faster when copies of the site sit on servers close to them. Cloudflare says that a CDN places servers near the people visiting a site, which shortens the distance between each visitor and the page. Visitors then get pages faster, and fewer of them give up and leave. That is why load time counts as a core benefit. Those nearby servers belong to the CDN provider, not to the site owner.

A CDN also makes a site's files smaller before readers receive them. It compresses those files, so it has less data to send across the network to each visitor. That makes the CDN more than a place to keep nearby copies, because it also changes the site's own files before they reach readers. The more of a site's speed that comes from this work, the more of the site's performance belongs to a system the owner does not run.

For a site owner, speed matters because it holds readers' attention. Visitors who get faster pages spend more time on the site, and that extra time is the return the owner is paying for. To get it, the owner places the CDN between the site and the people who visit it.

The owner rents that speed rather than owning it, and keeps it only as long as the CDN's network keeps working.

For most websites, the expensive part is delivering pages to everyone who asks for them. Cloudflare describes bandwidth as a primary expense for websites. Every time a visitor asks for a page and the site's own server answers, the site draws on its bandwidth, so a growing readership raises the cost. A CDN provider can lower that cost by answering many requests from copies stored on its own servers. The origin server then sends less data, and the site owner can pay a smaller hosting bill. The more readers who receive those stored copies instead of pages from the site's own server, the more of the delivery cost the owner avoids. Of everything a CDN offers, this saving is the easiest for an owner to check, because it appears directly on the hosting bill.

The cost of moving data can also make it harder for a business to leave the company that holds that data. Cloud providers charge egress fees when data leaves their systems, and in the U.K. cloud market those fees are linked to the difficulty of switching providers or using more than one at once, according to CNBC. Ofcom, Britain's communications regulator, has framed the issue around a customer's freedom to leave. "I think that's all about making sure that the business customers who rely on the cloud can switch and use multiple providers easily," said Fergal Farragher, Ofcom's director responsible for the market study. By that test, a lower data bill and the freedom to leave are separate gains, and a site can pay less while finding it harder to change providers.

It is not established whether caching through a CDN also lowers the egress fees a cloud host charges. A site owner should check the host's own bill rather than assume the two savings add up. Whatever the CDN saves on hosting, it saves by sending readers' requests through another company's network. That arrangement makes the CDN provider one more company whose terms, prices and outages the site depends on. An owner weighing the deal should ask whether the site could still switch, or spread its traffic across more than one provider, if it needed to.

An owner who pays less but can no longer easily move the site has made only part of a good deal.

A website can go offline because of its own success. Cloudflare lists sudden spikes in traffic from a site's popularity among the threats to keeping a site online. When many readers arrive at once to read a popular story, a single server may be unable to answer all of them, and readers get an error page at the moment the most people want the site. A CDN provider guards against this by placing a much larger network in front of the site, with room for more traffic than many sites' own servers have. For a small site, this means borrowing spare capacity from a network built for loads far larger than its own.

CDN providers keep their networks running by moving traffic away from any part that breaks. When a single server fails, the provider uses failover to shift its traffic to other servers. When a whole data center fails, the provider uses a routing method called Anycast to send visitors elsewhere. Both fixes need a working part of the same network to take the traffic. That makes them defenses against local failures. A fault in the shared layer itself leaves no working part to move traffic to, and every site that relies on that layer goes down together.

The goal behind Anycast routing is broad. Cloudflare said its purpose when an entire data center has trouble is "ensuring that no users lose access to the website." That goal can hold when the trouble stays in one data center and other parts of the network are still working to take its visitors. In return, the site owner accepts a rarer kind of breakdown, one that does not stay with a single site but reaches every site that shares the network.

With a CDN, a site stays online only as long as the network does, which gives it more spare capacity than many sites have on their own and no protection on the day the network itself fails.

A CDN provider guards a website from the same position it uses to speed the site up: between the site and everyone who visits it. Cloudflare says a CDN can blunt distributed denial-of-service, or DDoS, attacks and keep a site's security certificates current. Both are protections the owner would otherwise have to arrange alone. The provider's servers sort through incoming traffic and stop many kinds of harmful requests, so much of that traffic never gets to the machine that stores the site. Attackers therefore hit the shared network instead of the site, and the site stays safe only as long as that network holds.

The value of that protection depends on how large an attack the provider can absorb, and the largest attacks on Cloudflare's network were far more common in the second quarter of 2026 than in the first. Cloudflare mitigated over 800 DDoS attacks above 1 terabit per second in the second quarter, up from 130 in the first, according to Cloudflare figures reported by BleepingComputer. If attacks of that size keep growing, the provider will need more capacity to stop them. A site owner who depends on a CDN leaves that work to the provider.

For most sites, the protection that matters from day to day is the provider's routine filtering of harmful requests. The attacks above 1 terabit per second matter to those sites in a different way, because they test the capacity of the network that all of them rely on.

A site owner also needs to know what the protection covers, not only whether the site has it. The company selling the service describes its benefit with care: "A properly configured CDN may also help protect websites against some common malicious attacks," Cloudflare said. By the seller's own account, the protection depends on setup and covers only some attacks.

A site's security behind a CDN reaches only as far as the owner's configuration and only as high as the capacity the provider keeps adding.
