Milburn’s absurdly ambitious report offers blueprint for fixing everything and everyone

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

He lays out sweeping aims and finer details for how to fix the Neets crisis – and is obsessive about welfare

It is not clear if too many people were expecting Everything Everywhere All at Once, but that is what Alan Milburn has delivered. From a Welfare State to a Working State is almost absurdly ambitious. The terms of reference said something about the drivers of youth labour markets. We got a blueprint for how to fix everything and everyone.

What Keir Starmer, who commissioned it, had in mind, we’ll never know, but Milburn has taken up with gusto Andy Burnham’s general invocation to think boldly and rewire the state. Yes, it’s about training and jobs, but it’s also about pulling apart the NHS, giving schools and colleges an MOT, and once again, blowing up old-fashioned social security.

It is nothing less, Milburn suggests boldly, than rescuing an abandoned generation and giving their lives “shape and meaning”. It is about strategy, planning – making sure all kids are school ready by the age of five, as well as job ready by 18 – but it is also across the finer detail: he asks bosses at one point, for example, if they might at least bother to reply to young people’s job applications.

It is not dull, as employment policy goes: this is Milburn possibly at the peak of his persuasive powers, throwing it all at one last big legacy-defining job, joined by an advisory committee packed with old Blair-era policy comrades. This is not your modest, average independent policy review, he suggests at one stage, but “a broad-based manifesto for change” on a generational scale. It’s not a plan, he says, so much as a 278-page settlement, a recasting of the state, laws, and institutions.

For the most part Milburn is well tuned to Labour voter ears. There are promises of full employment for the young and promises to change the way the labour market operates. There are calls to double the number of youth apprenticeships and roll out a dynamic new cohort of specialist career advisers. There is no shyness about the central role of the state in fixing markets that have let down young people so terribly.

Nor are the 1 million 16- to 24-year-olds who are not in education, employment or training (Neet) to blame for their predicament, he concludes. This is not a snowflake generation, lazy and unambitious. Milburn acknowledges the central negative presence in their lives of poverty, inequality, intergenerational unfairness, the scourge of mental ill health and disability discrimination.

Which is why Milburn’s proposed welfare changes lurk like a dark heart. We have been told for months now that this is a government that, unlike its predecessor, does not do “crude cuts”. But this is a plan that proposes a young, out of work disability claimant on a £2,000 benefit income has that replaced with £339 a month. This, it concludes, is better for their longer-term life chances.

For Milburn it is all about the guarantee, the promise of work and training and “the gift of a future”. It’s short-term pain for long-term gain. Not that they have much choice: young people will be expected to work or train. If they don’t engage they will have their benefits sanctioned. The report picks at welfare reform like an itch, obsessively; given half a chance it says it would love to overhaul everyone else’s benefits too. At one point it describes disability benefits as the “weakest link in the chain”. About half of Neets do not claim any benefits at all.

Within 10 years, the plan would strip £3.6bn from young people’s net disability benefits. Past experience, says the poverty charity the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, shows disability benefit cuts deepen poverty, worsen mental health and do not increase the likelihood of finding a job. It is unclear if Labour backbenchers will revolt on benefits as they did last year, but they too may ask themselves why Milburn offers jobs and skills support at the expense of financial security. Why cannot it be both?
