Remembering Dolores Huerta: Artists Barbara Carrasco, Judith F. Baca, Yreina D. Cervántez, and Vincent Valdez Reflect on the Iconic Activist’s Legacy

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

“It’s hard to believe this amazing icon is gone from our world,” artist Judith F. Baca said of Dolores Huerta, the labor activist who died last week at 96.

A cofounder of the United Farm Workers of America, Huerta is best-known for her decades-long dedication to fighting for the labor rights of farmworkers in the fields of California and more broadly for helping to galvanize the Chicano civil rights movement. She recruited members to the union’s cause, led strikes and ensured that companies felt the impact of the absence of the farmworkers whom they exploited, and negotiated the contracts that provided material change to the lives and working conditions for campesinos and campesinas. “She turned the phrase ‘Sí, se puede!’ into a chant that animated rallies and marches well into a new century,” as the Los Angeles Times put it.

“She was always conscious of having women be at the table,” artist Barbara Carrasco told ARTnews, noting that as a UFW negotiator Huerta, one of the few women in a leadership position, was labeled “the dragon lady” for her fierceness. Carrasco said she will always remember Huerta as a nurturing figure who mentored countless women over the decades.

After leaving UFW in the 1980s, she would continue to advocate for labor rights and women rights. In 2002, Huerta received the Puffin Prize for Creative Citizenship, which came with a $100,000 grant. She decided to use the funds start her an eponymous foundation, which aims to “cultivate empowered volunteer organizations actively pursuing social justice,” according to its website.

Huerta’s status as an icon has also translated into the art world, with numerous artists depicting her image over the years, including Baca, Carrasco, Yreina D. Cervántez, and Vincent Valdez, all of whom share their memories of Huerta below.

b. 1955, El Paso, Texas; lives and works in Los Angeles

I first met Dolores when I was around 19. My boyfriend at the time was UCLA student, and I heard Cesar Chavez speak on campus, and I was really impressed. I met Dolores right after, and never in my life, I have met a more charismatic lady. She was just so down to earth. That’s what always struck me about her: Here is this woman with all these accolades are given to her, but she still is such a great human being. She’d meet people—it didn’t matter if they were rich or poor—and she’d treat them all the same. I think that’s what struck a lot of us; we would just say, “Oh, this lady’s amazing.” I went to an UFW convention once, and so many farmworkers came up to her, and they just loved her. That doesn’t happen overnight. That takes years and years of dedication and of being out there and representing them. There’s a quote by her: “Every moment is an organizing opportunity, every person a potential activist, every minute a chance to change the world.” I think she really did take advantage of her time here on Earth.

I’m one of many, many women whom she nurtured and helped. There are so many women, from women in public office to women working in the fields. She always had such pride in the women she mentored. She was also very supportive of visual artists. I remember the night before an event for the Department of Labor, when Hilda Solis was secretary, Dolores asked me to write a list of all the artists who had contributed to the UFW over the years. During the event, she mentioned everybody. I thought, Who does that? Not too many people will pay tribute to the artists. The artist are often the last individuals to be thanked [in a political movement].

In the ’90s, Self-Help Graphics did a women’s atelier [series of prints]. I invited 10 women to join, and I remember saying we should do a print showing women as nurturers. I wanted to pay tribute to Dolores—that was who came to mind immediately. When you do something and you really truly believe in it, it shows. I truly loved her, and I wanted to show that. The colors were specifically chosen: the yellow ocher reflects the good energy coming from her, the blue-green background represents the fields and the farmworkers she was defending in those fields, the rose represents her strength and beauty, both inward and outward. I always thought she was so beautiful and a beautiful soul, too. It was a work I didn’t rush. I was so happy when the Girl Scouts of America selected that portrait to be on a merit patch.

Dolores had a good sense of humor. That was one of our big connections. She and I used to laugh about really silly stuff—if I told you, you wouldn’t believe what we were laughing about it. She always said to keep your inner child alive and not to ignore it. I think that’s why she was able to laugh with so many people and get along with everybody. Her energy was always high, even until the end. We partied together like crazy. At my 70th birthday last year, Dolores was dancing—she loved to dance. She said if she didn’t do what she did, she would have been a dancer.

The last time I talked to her on the phone was about two weeks ago. I got a chance to tell her that I loved her. A lot of us who have been friends with her were lucky enough to see her right before she started treatment. It took her faster than everybody thought. I know firsthand what that kind of treatment is like; I’ve been cancer-free now for some 30 years, and I remember how brutal that kind of treatment is, and I was young, and she was 96. It’s got to be really difficult. She told me she was in a lot of pain. Even though we knew the day was coming soon, it was still a sad day for all of us. She was one of those people who was extremely energetic. When everybody would be tired, she would say, “Come on, let’s do this, and let’s do that.” It was inspiring to be around her.

b. 1946, Los Angeles; lives and works in Los Angeles

Early on in the movement, Dolores and I would have been present at similar things, marches and organizing events in support of the United Farm Workers. I was also at La Paz working at one point [around 1974] to produce a big mural that Carlos Almaraz had designed. I remember painting a section that said, “No More Sweetheart Contacts,” and that piece was used for a march to Sacramento. The making of that giant canvas was the first time I went to La Paz. I might have met her then, but I knew her from a distance for a while. I can’t quite remember when I started to have more personal contact with her.

At a certain point, I had a chance to speak with Dolores at length, and she told me that she was not given the credit that she was due as a cofounder of the United Farm Workers and for the work that she had done. Of course, I understood that because I had struggled myself with the dominance of men within the mural movement and the Chicano movement, and in the art world as well. So, when I started working on Arch of Dignity, Equality and Justice (2005–08), which is a 25-foot arch on the campus of San Jose State University, I decided when I was going to put Dolores in the front of the monument—that she would be the dominant figure, not Chavez. I depicted her in a thoughtful way: One of her hands is outstretched, saying, “Join me,” and is welcoming, and the other hand is a fist. In other words, she was both those things simultaneously. It is a depiction of her in leadership role, coming up out of the fields. From that point on, when I was making something historical, I looked to Dolores as a way to represent the power of a Chicana organizer and a model for us in social activism. In a sense, she was a hero for me over and over again.

She had the most amazing energy I ever experienced—never tiring. Not only that, but you could end up dancing all night with her. I remember staying up until three in the morning with her listening to music at Burning Man in the tent. I have a fabulous image of her in a dust storm there. I couldn’t even get out of the car; it was so horrible. I put my head out and I was choking, and there she was standing there laughing. I thought, How did she get that tenacity, that resilience? It must have been from being out in the Central Valley and having dealt with bad conditions like that in the field. So that to me was a real image of her resilience and her capacity to withstand any storm.

In Gente del Maíz (2012) for the Miguel Contreras Learning Center, you see Dolores in the fields [on the left] with a bullhorn. That comes from a story that Maria Elena Durazo [then executive vice president of the national AFL-CIO Executive Council] told me about how Dolores had called Miguel Contreras to join the United Farm Workers. Later, of course, he joined the United Farm Workers and led the Delano Grape Boycott in Canada. I wanted to indicate that here was this young man, Miguel, who didn’t have a plan to become an activist. It was through Dolores’s encouragement that he joined the movement, and he became a critical figure in it. The central image is an ear of corn with three figures: a young Miguel drops soil, another drops kernels in the soil, and the other is watering the soil. Out of these planted corn kernels come community organizers. I was talking about how there’s the planting of people, the nurturing of souls that become engaged in in a passionate commitment to social justice. I think that’s what Dolores models for us repeatedly: a lifelong commitment to social justice. That’s something important for young people to learn, because they often don’t have an understanding of what it takes to commit a life to creating change. I think more young people need to learn activism is something that you put your body on the line. Dolores was arrested around 25 times, and she was beaten almost to death in San Francisco by the police and they ruptured her spleen. Dolores put her body, her heart, and her spirit into the movement.

In Seeing Through Others’ Eyes (2010), she is there between Chavez and Robert F. Kennedy leading the crowd, while these two men break bread. It’s important to say that we can’t let the discussion of Dolores Huerta focus on her being victimized by Cesar Chavez. It cannot be about that because that’s a diminishment of her character. She was much larger and much more powerful than simply a rape victim.

Dolores was a model, not only training young people to become activists but also she’s a model for standing up against the most powerful forces. I don’t think I can think of a person who can replace her or that can equal her contributions. She’s the Chicana icon, and she will be sorely missed. She was one of the greatest leaders we’ve had.

b. 1952, Garden City, Kansas; lives and works in Los Angeles

It has been my honor and privilege to know Dolores Huerta, whose influence on me and so many others have been tremendous. Dolores has been a warrior in the best sense of the word: powerful, resilient, compassionate, harnessing her formidable intelligence to fight for the rights of those most vulnerable. Dolores was a feminista, a great humanitarian, resolute, and committed for the long term in the struggle for justice and a better world.

Dolores Huerta has had an impact on many generations of women and men, but she had a very special significance for young Chicanas and Latinas of my generation. Before now there were virtually no positive images of women like us in film or media. She was such an inspiration as one of the first prominent mujeres of our time that was a public figure and activista. Dolores showed us courage and dedication, and what it meant to fight for our dignity, equal rights, and how to show solidarity with diverse peoples and struggles.

I met Dolores in Santa Cruz in the early ’70s when I was student, but I did not get to know her until the ’80s. One of my first encounters with her was in East LA. I was asked to help out for a UFW function organized by Fred Ross Jr. and Dolores was there. We had an opportunity to talk at length. I am grateful for that experience and other times spent in her presence; she was very generous and approachable. I last spoke with Dolores at the birthday party for artist Barbara Carrasco, her dear friend. Dolores looked radiant, peaceful, as always composed. I cherish the memory of her dancing—joyful, celebrating, mighty.

Dolores was a strong advocate for the arts and artists. I am very grateful for the support she showed me and many other artists, especially the mujeres artistas. My heartfelt dedication to Dolores Huerta came in the form of the mural La Ofrenda. Located in the Echo Park community, it was created as part of my graduate thesis at UCLA (sponsored by SPARC and the City of Los Angeles Cultural Affairs Department). The mural was executed with the assistance of talented mural crew(s) with contributions from Salvadoran poet Rossana Perez, Chicana/o poets Gloria Enedina Alvarez, Victor Carrillo, and graffiti artist Eric “Duke” Montenegro. Originally painted in 1989, it is meant as an offering to my community, a prayer to the ancestors for courage, strength, and sustenance. It is about the value of labor, the farmworkers, immigration, political exile, and very importantly Chicanx solidarity with the people of Central and Latin America and their wars for liberation. La Ofrenda highlights the vital role of women in creating transformative change. I thought it most fitting to honor Dolores as the central figure in the mural, a unifying force across many cultures, struggles, and advocate for many just causes.

Unfortunately, many of the issues La Ofrenda depicted almost 40 years ago, including the victimization and persecution of immigrants in the US and worldwide persists with unimaginable cruelty. Let us follow the example of Dolores and make “good trouble” as she did in her lifetime and continue to fight for the rights of immigrants, farmworkers, women, and all people. It is my hope that La Ofrenda be a reminder and inspiration to those that knew Dolores Huerta and those that will learn of her enduring legacy, and immeasurable contributions to humanity. So much love and gratitude to you Dolores, beloved mujer and hermana. You are siempre presente.

b. 1977, San Antonio, Texas; lives and works in Houston and San Antonio

There was no better way to begin my ACLU project, The New Americans (2026), than with Dolores Huerta. Dolores’s legacy remains a powerful testament and reminder—our current struggles are nothing new. Generations before us have long endured and remained steadfast in their commitment to confront and challenge the distorted social realities within the United States. Although my portrait of Dolores intends to pay respect to the elders, it is strategic in its conceptual pose. Despite her age, Dolores’s resilient spirit still burns bright. Her head holds high. She is caught gazing optimistically toward the right side of the page. As the first image in a series of 25 drawings, she leads us forward, eager to hand off the torch to those willing to come next. Dolores Huerta will forever be that reminder to us all—be the stubborn pulse in a dying heart.

The first time Dolores and I met in 2015 was completely random and somewhat surreal. It was a very late night after party in Berlin, after the Berlin Film Festival. It was already 2 a.m. And I left before she did. We met up again in 2023 at the Rothko Chapel in Houston. We sat on a stage together with Cheech Marin and shared stories and ideas together about the intertwining of art and democracy. By the end of the discussion, Dolores had managed to convince the entire audience to stand on their feet. She had several hundred people and a mostly white audience, shouting in unison from the tops of their lungs, “SI SE PUEDE!!” Cheech and I stared and smirked at each other in complete awe.

I last saw Dolores in 2024. We met at LACMA to view my exhibition, “El Chavez Ravine.” We spent a memorable afternoon together. I snuck Dolores a razor blade out of my pocket and asked her to etch her name into the back of the lowrider ice cream truck that was the centerpiece of the exhibition. Her spirit of rebellion was always ready, it seemed. At the tail end of the day, I expressed my idea to create a portrait of her in hopes of using it as a catalyst one day, for a bigger idea. I photographed her hands and face before we parted ways.

And finally, just [last Tuesday], I mailed Dolores a personal letter and package containing her portrait along with the completed series of drawings, The New Americans. [On Wednesday] morning, I heard the news. There is something bittersweet about the thought of this envelope never being opened and read by her. Her spirit was a powerful, magnetic force in life and, now, in memory.
