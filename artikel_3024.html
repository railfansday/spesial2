Sports Betting and the Way We Make Money

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The podcast takes a hard look at America’s booming sports-betting industry, revisiting the math behind the wagers and offering betting listeners an alternative: investing.

By Motley Fool Staff – Nov 27, 2026 at 2:24PM EST

In this episode of Motley Fool Rule Breaker Investing, Motley Fool co-founder David Gardner explores the world of sports betting, discussing the money made, the influence wielded by celebrities, and the legacy left behind.

This week, I want to ask three questions. First, what does the math tell us? Second, what distinguishes sports betting from stock market investing? Third, a question that matters very much to me. I hope you, too, does it matter how we make our money? Let's start with a little math. I've often said that investing in the stock market is a little like sailing. Imagine you and me sitting together in a sailboat, the wind pushing us forward. Well, that would be the stock market’s compounding positive returns. That wind is at our backs. When you're a stock market investor, the broad stock market consistently rises over time, even with, of course, the storm, and there will always be storms. Sometimes the wind blows right in our faces. Sometimes our boat gets tossed around. Over decades, though, investors have benefited from owning pieces of productive enterprises, companies that create goods and services, generate profits, and grow. Now, sports betting has a fundamentally different mathematical structure. Here's a simple example. Suppose you and I bet on opposite sides of a football game. We each have a 50% chance of winning. If we're just betting against each other, one of us wins, one of us loses. Between us, no wealth has been created.

Now introduce a sportsbook. A common arrangement requires you to risk $110 to win $100. So if you win, you make $100; if you lose, you're out $110. OK, so suppose you make 100 such bets, each one a genuine 50-50 proposition. You win 50 and lose 50. Well, on your 50 winners, you're going to make $5,000, and on your 50 losers, you're going to lose $5,500. Congratulations, you've gotten half your predictions right, and you've lost $500. You're a loser. That's not even including the time and the attention that you devoted to making those bets in the first place, which, by the way, might be just as expensive or even more so. Now, the rare skilled bettor may find favorable odds, and the precise mathematics are going to vary from wager to wager. The point is that the sportsbook’s margin — that middleman is creating a headwind that the typical bettor has to overcome in order just to break even.

And this is where I want to pull a page from my book Rule Breaker Investing. Last September, my book Rule Breaker Investing was published. Part 1 is the six habits of the Rule Breaker Investor. Part 2 is the six traits that we look for in Rule Breaker stocks. Then in Part 3, I discussed the six principles of Rule Breaker portfolio management. One of them, Principle number five, is that you get to invest the whole race, by which I mean that unlike horse race bettors who have to place their bets before the race, we stock market investors can pay attention to our investments over time while we're holding them as part owners and make better decisions as we learn. Here's what I wrote about sports betting, and I quote, "The key to principle number five is simple. Use what's happening in the race to make every additional investment you make during the race smarter.

This is in contrast to a raging new area of speculation in this era, sports betting, to which I briefly want to speak. Sports betting, of course, is all or nothing, and the bet has to be put down before the game is played. Sports bettors have numerous disadvantages compared to stock market investors. They routinely have to take all-or-nothing propositions. They pay a middleman, the house, up to 10% commission and have to put down all their money before the race or the game starts. It's regrettable how easy and attractive sports betting is made to sound when it's such a losing play, day in and day out. I'm hoping this book reaches many and helps clarify for them why their money is so much better invested in the stock market. The market tends to rise over time, you and me in the sailboat, the wind at our backs. Sports betting, by its nature, is zero-sum, and your expected return is always negative. The handicaps are set very effectively, whether we’re talking about a 6.5-point favorite in basketball or seven-to-five odds at a horse track. One bettor will win, the other will lose, and the house will always take its cut. Over time, almost all consistent sports bettors lose money. What happens to those who consistently invest over time? The difference is immense.

Though sports betting is now widely legal in the U.S., that doesn't make it a smart bet. I've made friendly wagers with friends for decades, which can make watching games more fun and stressful. But I look askance at big-volume betting and especially its influence on the media coverage of sports I love. A booming gambling industry does not reflect my best vision for our future. Sports betting, as has been said of state lotteries, functions broadly as yet another tax on imbeciles. While some bettors may see it differently, the reality is that over time, most will lose their money." Well, there you have it, my words in print from my book Rule Breaker Investing published a year ago.

Two quick clarifications. First, sports betting today does include live betting where you can place wagers while a game is underway. That particular distinction I drew about betting before the game isn't universally true. Second, when I speak about the house taking up to 10%, that's a shorthand for a common betting convention, rather than a universal commission on every dollar wagered. Though earlier, when I presented the $167 billion of gambled money, do you remember how much money the house did make? Yeah, its take was just about $17 billion, just about exactly 10% of what was wagered; it's simple math. On a side note, I also want to say something about that last phrase, tax on imbeciles. That's strong language. I certainly don't think everyone who places a sports bet is an imbecile. The phrase is intended to make us think about a system in which the average participant faces a mathematical disadvantage and keeps betting over and over. I especially want to add what I consider the biggest distinction between owning stock and making a bet. Even though you often hear people saying, both are just gambling.

The stock market, sports, they are fundamentally different when you own stock, you literally own an asset, part ownership of a domestic or foreign business, you are an owner. You own a piece of an enterprise that may create value for years or, even better, decades. When you make a sports bet, you have a wager on a short-term outcome. You're not an owner of the teams or the underlying economic activity. You're a speculator. You're typically paying a price to an intermediary of the house to make your bet. Over time, the typical bettor, as I said earlier, is fighting math. Now, I suspect many of those who are fighting math don't fully understand the math that they're fighting. If you add up all the winnings and losses across all the customers and the money that flows to the house, well, it means that collectively, their customers come out behind. Earlier, I mentioned making 100 $100 bets. Let's say you win half of them, so you win $100, 50 times; you lose the other 50. It sounds like breaking even, right? Except you don't. As we pointed out, you've paid the house $500 for the so-called entertainment of breaking even. That's a 5% loss on your $10,000 wager.

Now imagine if you’d simply invested that $10,000 instead: a 50-stock portfolio, $200 in each position, or just simply $10,000 plunked into an index fund. Imagine what happens to that money. It grows over the years, in fits and starts through ups and downs, but with the possibility of compounding into something much bigger. That is the real opportunity cost of sports betting, and it is massive. It's not just the money you lost. It's the wealth you never give yourself the chance to build. The house doesn't win every bet. It doesn't need to.

Its business model is built to win over time. Let me put that larger distinction as simply as I can. In the stock market, your success doesn't require my failure. If you and I both buy shares of a wonderful business and that business grows and prospers for 20 years, we both win, and the employees can win. The customers can win. Society can benefit from what that business creates. That's one of the beautiful things about capitalism at its best. Sports betting, again, a very different enterprise. The bookmaker makes its money by operating a market in wagers, and its customers collectively bear the cost of that business, collectively bear the cost of that business. We've talked about the math, and I hope we've talked about the money, and I hope especially to people who are new to the stock market and don't realize that it goes up over time and don't realize the opportunity cost they're paying by playing a losing game, putting their money down repeatedly in an expected negative return context.
