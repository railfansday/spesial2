Colorado to Vermont: the 8 states that still tax Social Security benefits

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Eight states tax Social Security benefits in 2026, each with its own rules on who pays, plus how federal taxation works and how West Virginia left the list

Social Security benefits face two separate tax systems, and they rarely line up. At the federal level, up to 85 percent of a retiree's benefits can count as taxable income, depending on a figure called combined income. The income thresholds that trigger the tax date to 1983 and 1993. They have never been indexed to inflation. The same dollar lines that applied decades ago still apply today.

Most states take the opposite approach and leave benefits untaxed. The states that tax Social Security benefits in 2026 are Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah and Vermont. Living in one of them does not mean a retiree automatically owes state tax on benefits. Most of these states shield lower- and middle-income households through exemptions, subtractions or credits. In several of them, those breaks shrink or disappear as income rises.

The details vary widely from state to state. Connecticut fully exempts benefits for single filers with adjusted gross income under $75,000 and joint filers under $100,000. New Mexico sets its exemption lines at $100,000 for single filers and $150,000 for joint filers. Colorado lets residents 65 and older subtract all of their federally taxable benefits. Rhode Island ties its exemption to reaching full retirement age and staying under an income limit. Utah offers a nonrefundable credit rather than an exemption.

The list has shrunk in recent years. Kansas, Missouri and Nebraska each stopped taxing Social Security benefits in 2024. West Virginia ran a three-year phaseout through 2026 and is generally listed as no longer taxing benefits this year. For retirees comparing states, how benefits are treated is only one part of the picture. Taxes on pensions, retirement account withdrawals, property and purchases also shape what a household owes.

This guide covers 10 subjects. The first explains how federal tax on benefits works. The federally taxable portion of benefits is the starting point for some state calculations. The next eight cover each state that still taxes benefits, without ranking them by tax burden. The last covers West Virginia's exit from the list. Thresholds and credit amounts can change from year to year. Each state's tax agency is the place to confirm the current figures for the tax year a retiree is filing.

Up to 85 percent of Social Security benefits can be subject to federal income tax, depending on a figure the IRS calls combined income. No one pays federal tax on all of their benefits. Retirees with little income beyond Social Security often owe no federal tax on their benefits at all.

Combined income has three parts. It starts with adjusted gross income. It then adds any nontaxable interest, such as interest from municipal bonds. Finally, it adds half of the year's Social Security benefits. The total is compared with fixed dollar thresholds that depend on filing status.

For single filers, benefits begin to become partly taxable once combined income passes $25,000. Between that level and $34,000, up to 50 percent of benefits can be taxed. Above $34,000, the taxable share can rise to as much as 85 percent.

Joint filers face the same two-step structure at higher levels. Their benefits become partly taxable above $32,000 in combined income. Up to 85 percent becomes taxable above $44,000.

Those percentages describe how much of the benefit counts as taxable income. They are not tax rates. The taxable portion is added to a filer's other income and taxed at ordinary federal income tax rates.

The two tiers came from separate laws a decade apart. Congress created the 50 percent tier in 1983 and added the 85 percent tier in 1993.

The money raised by each tier goes to a different place. Tax collected under the original tier is credited to the Social Security trust funds. Revenue from the higher tier goes to Medicare's Hospital Insurance trust fund.

The dollar thresholds have stayed at the levels Congress set. Annual cost-of-living adjustments, meanwhile, push benefit payments higher. That means more beneficiaries cross the thresholds over time.

The paperwork starts with Form SSA-1099, which the Social Security Administration sends each January. It shows the total benefits paid during the previous year. IRS Publication 915 includes a worksheet for working out the taxable portion.

Beneficiaries who expect to owe tax can have it withheld from their monthly payments. They do this by filing Form W-4V and choosing a withholding rate of seven, 10, 12 or 22 percent. Quarterly estimated tax payments are the other option.

The federal figures also carry over to state returns. Many state income tax returns begin with federal adjusted gross income, which already includes any taxable share of benefits. A state that taxes Social Security then applies its own exemptions, subtractions or credits to that amount.

Colorado residents 65 and older can subtract all of their federally taxable Social Security income on their state return. For filers in that age group, the subtraction removes benefits from Colorado taxable income entirely.

Colorado remains among the states that tax benefits because its break depends on age rather than income. Younger beneficiaries do not get the full subtraction.

The mechanism is a subtraction, not a blanket exemption. Colorado calculates its income tax starting from federal taxable income. Any Social Security that is taxable on a federal return therefore flows into the Colorado calculation by default. The subtraction is the line that takes it back out. A retiree has to claim it on the state return to receive the benefit.

The subtraction applies only to the federally taxable portion of benefits. That means the federal formula still determines the amount in play. A retiree whose benefits are not taxable at the federal level has nothing to subtract in Colorado.

For filers 65 and older, the Social Security subtraction has no dollar ceiling. A retiree in that age group can remove the entire federally taxable amount, whether it is small or large.

Residents aged 55 to 64 receive a more limited version. Their subtraction is capped at $20,000. That cap covers pension and annuity income, and Social Security counts toward it. A 60-year-old who collects both a pension and Social Security draws on the same $20,000 limit for both sources.

Federally taxable retirement income above that limit stays in Colorado taxable income for this age group. The cap is a fixed dollar amount. It does not grow with the size of a person's benefit.

Residents under 55 generally fall outside the age-based subtraction. Social Security Disability Insurance pays benefits to some people well before retirement age. Federally taxable benefits of that kind remain part of Colorado income for those filers.

Colorado taxes income at a single flat rate rather than through graduated brackets. Any Social Security left after the subtraction is taxed at the same rate as wages or other income. The Colorado Department of Revenue publishes the current rules and subtraction forms for each tax year.

Connecticut fully exempts Social Security benefits for single filers with adjusted gross income under $75,000 and joint filers under $100,000. Retirees above those lines still get a partial break. For them, the state can tax no more than 25 percent of their benefits.

The income test uses adjusted gross income. That is a different measure from the combined income figure the IRS uses to decide how much of a person's benefits are federally taxable. Federal adjusted gross income includes the share of benefits that is federally taxable. A retiree with large benefits can therefore move closer to the Connecticut limit through the benefits themselves.

The thresholds depend on filing status. A single filer compares adjusted gross income with the $75,000 line. A married couple filing jointly compares its joint adjusted gross income with the $100,000 line. The joint threshold is $25,000 higher than the single threshold, not twice as high.

Connecticut's 25 percent cap is far below the federal ceiling. Federal rules allow up to 85 percent of benefits to be taxed for higher-income recipients. Connecticut's limit sits 60 percentage points lower. The 25 percent figure is a maximum share of benefits, not a tax rate.

A dollar example shows the gap. Take a retiree above the state limit who collects $30,000 in Social Security benefits in a year. Up to $25,500 of that amount could be taxable on the federal return. Connecticut could tax no more than $7,500 of the same benefits. That leaves $18,000 of benefits within federal reach but outside Connecticut's.

Retirees below the limits owe no Connecticut tax on their benefits. Households with pensions, investment income or wages on top of benefits are the ones most likely to cross the thresholds. Connecticut's Department of Revenue Services administers the exemption and publishes the forms and instructions for each tax year. That agency is the place to confirm the current rules before filing.

Minnesota taxes Social Security benefits, but it lets many retirees subtract some or all of those benefits from their state taxable income. The size of that subtraction depends on income and filing status, and it shrinks as income rises.

A subtraction works differently from a tax credit. It lowers the amount of income Minnesota taxes rather than cutting the tax bill directly. The dollar value of the break therefore depends on the state tax rate that would otherwise apply to the subtracted income.

Minnesota uses a graduated income tax with four brackets. The same subtraction can be worth a different amount to two retirees, depending on which bracket the subtracted income would have fallen into.

The subtraction only reaches benefits that are already taxable at the federal level. A retiree whose benefits are not federally taxable has nothing to subtract on the state return. For that person, the Minnesota subtraction never comes into play.

Income is the main variable. Retirees with lower incomes can generally subtract more of their benefits. As income climbs, the allowable subtraction gets smaller. Past a certain income level, a retiree may receive only a partial subtraction or none at all.

The income that counts is not limited to Social Security. Pension payments, retirement account withdrawals and other taxable income all feed into the test. A larger withdrawal in one year can shrink the subtraction for that year.

Filing status also changes the calculation. Single filers, married couples filing jointly and married people filing separately face different limits. Two households with identical benefits can end up with different Minnesota tax results because of how they file.

The Minnesota Department of Revenue publishes the current income limits and the instructions used to figure the subtraction. Its figures for the 2026 tax year are the ones that apply to a 2026 return. Limits published for earlier tax years do not carry over automatically.

Montana taxes Social Security benefits in 2026, and retirees there can owe state income tax on part of what they receive. The state has also changed the method it uses to calculate the taxable amount.

That change affects anyone who files a Montana return each year. Rules from earlier tax years may not match the current ones. A filer who repeats a prior year's approach could misjudge what the 2026 state return requires.

The federal return does not settle the Montana question. A benefit that is partly taxable on the federal return still goes through Montana's own treatment on the state return. The two results are calculated separately. One does not automatically equal the other.

Older materials may be out of date. Saved worksheets, carried-over tax-software settings and guidance written under the previous method may not reflect how Montana handles benefits now. Anyone relying on those materials should confirm they apply to the 2026 tax year.

The Montana Department of Revenue publishes the state's current income tax forms, instructions and rules. It is the authoritative place to check whether any portion of a benefit is taxable in 2026. Its instructions also explain how a filer's circumstances affect the result.

Residency also shapes the outcome. Montana's treatment of benefits applies to people who are Montana residents for the tax year. A retiree who moves into or out of the state should check which state's rules govern their Social Security income for that year.

New Mexico exempts Social Security benefits from state income tax for single filers with income under $100,000 and joint filers with income under $150,000. The exemption has applied since the 2022 tax year.

The rule works as a threshold test. A retiree whose income stays below the line for their filing status leaves Social Security benefits out of New Mexico taxable income. Filing status decides which of the two lines applies.

The test looks at income, not at the size of the benefit check. Pension payments, retirement account withdrawals and wages can all count toward the limit. A retiree with modest benefits can still lose the exemption if other income pushes the total over the line.

Married couples filing jointly keep the exemption as long as their income stays under $150,000. Single filers must stay under $100,000. The joint line sits $50,000 above the single line, but it is less than double. Two retirees get less combined room as a married couple than they would as two single filers.

Filing status can also change late in life. A surviving spouse who eventually moves from a joint return to a single return is measured against the $100,000 line instead of the $150,000 line. An income that qualified on a joint return can fall outside the exemption on a single return.

These limits sit far above the points where federal tax on benefits begins. A New Mexico retiree can owe federal income tax on part of their Social Security while owing nothing to the state on the same benefits.

Retirees with income at or above those limits are the group New Mexico still taxes on Social Security. For them, the state return generally begins with federal adjusted gross income. That figure already includes the federally taxable share of benefits.

The New Mexico Taxation and Revenue Department publishes the current forms and instructions. Retirees close to either line can check them before filing.

Rhode Island exempts Social Security benefits from state income tax only for residents who have reached full retirement age and whose adjusted gross income falls below a state limit. Both conditions must be met. Missing either one leaves the benefits subject to Rhode Island income tax.

The age test ties the exemption to a federal benchmark rather than to a fixed age written into state law. Rhode Island does not set its own retirement age for this purpose. It borrows the one Social Security already uses to calculate benefits.

The Social Security Administration sets full retirement age by birth year. It is 66 for people born from 1943 through 1954. It rises in two-month steps for those born from 1955 through 1959. For anyone born in 1960 or later, it is 67.

That rule matters most for early claimants. Social Security benefits can start as early as age 62, at a permanently reduced amount. For a resident born in 1960 or later, that leaves a five-year stretch between the earliest claiming age and full retirement age. Benefits received during that stretch do not qualify for the state exemption, even when household income is modest.

The second test is income. Rhode Island compares a filer's federal adjusted gross income with a threshold that varies by filing status. The state indexes that threshold to inflation. The dollar limit therefore changes from one tax year to the next. A figure that applied in an earlier year is not a reliable guide to the current one.

Rhode Island's income tax calculation begins with federal adjusted gross income. For residents who miss either test, the federally taxable share of their benefits is the portion that flows into the state return.

The Rhode Island Division of Taxation publishes the current income threshold, along with the instructions for claiming the exemption. Retirees approaching full retirement age, or with income near the limit, can confirm both details there before filing.

Utah includes Social Security benefits in state taxable income, then offsets that tax for many retirees with a nonrefundable Social Security benefit credit. The credit shrinks as income rises and reaches zero for higher-income households.

This works differently from the exemptions and subtractions that several other states use. An exemption or subtraction removes benefits from the income a state taxes. Utah's credit acts on the tax bill instead. The portion of benefits that is taxable at the federal level stays in the income Utah taxes. The credit then lowers the tax that results.

That distinction changes how the relief shows up on a return. A credit reduces the amount of tax owed directly, dollar for dollar. An exemption reduces income instead, and its value depends on the tax rate applied to that income.

Nonrefundable means the credit can bring a person's Utah income tax down to zero but no further. If the credit is larger than the tax owed, the unused portion is not paid out as a refund. A retiree with little or no Utah tax liability therefore gets limited value from the credit. There is little tax for it to offset.

The phaseout ties the credit to income. Households below the point where the phaseout begins can claim the full credit. Above that point, the credit gets smaller as income climbs. At the top of the range it reaches zero, and benefits are taxed without any offset from the credit.

As a result, Utah's treatment of benefits depends on a household's total income, not on the size of its benefits alone. Pension payments, withdrawals from retirement accounts, wages and investment income can all move a household further into the phaseout.

The Utah State Tax Commission publishes the current phaseout thresholds and the instructions for claiming the credit. Retirees can check those figures for the 2026 tax year before estimating what they owe.

Vermont taxes Social Security benefits only for residents whose income rises above a set level. Below that level, benefits are fully exempt from state income tax. Across a middle band of income, known as the phaseout range, the exemption shrinks. Above that band, no exemption applies.

Under this design, total income determines whether Vermont taxes benefits. The benefit amount alone does not. Two retirees who receive identical Social Security payments can land in different parts of the system because of their other income. That income might come from pensions, wages or investments.

The phaseout range works as a sliding scale rather than a single cutoff. A retiree whose income sits near the bottom of the range keeps a larger share of the exemption. A retiree near the top keeps a smaller share. Once income clears the upper end of the range, no exemption remains. Vermont then treats the benefits like other taxable income.

A sliding scale avoids a cliff. With a single cutoff, one extra dollar of income could expose a retiree's entire benefit to state tax. A phaseout spreads that change across a band of income instead.

The design also means a retiree's decisions about other income can affect the state tax on benefits. A larger taxable withdrawal from a retirement account or a return to part-time work raises income. For a retiree inside the phaseout range, that extra income also shrinks the exemption. More of the benefit then becomes taxable in Vermont.

The exemption also works differently from a tax credit. An exemption removes benefit income from the amount the state taxes. A credit reduces the tax bill after it has been calculated.

The Vermont Department of Taxes publishes the current income level for the full exemption and the boundaries of the phaseout range. Those amounts can change from one tax year to the next. The department is the place to confirm the limits for the 2026 tax year before filing.

West Virginia is generally listed as no longer taxing Social Security benefits in 2026. The state taxed benefits in earlier years, then ended that tax through a three-year phaseout that ran through 2026.

That history explains why West Virginia still turns up on some lists of states that tax benefits. Guides written before the phaseout ended included it. A retiree comparing guides from different years may find the state described both ways. The 2026 status is the one that applies to current planning.

A phaseout differs from a one-time repeal. West Virginia did not switch off the tax in a single year. It reduced the tax in stages. The change reached retirees gradually rather than all at once.

The staged design matters for anyone handling a return from an earlier tax year. Each year's return follows the rules in force for that year. A West Virginia resident filing late or amending a return from the phaseout period would apply that year's treatment, not the 2026 one.

The phrase "generally listed" also carries meaning. It reflects how published tax summaries describe the state. Retirees who want certainty about a specific year can confirm the state's treatment with the West Virginia State Tax Department.

The end of the state tax does not change the federal picture. West Virginia residents can still owe federal income tax on part of their benefits. Whether they do depends on their combined income. The repeal removes only the state-level layer of tax on benefits.

The phaseout also covers Social Security benefits specifically. It does not by itself settle how West Virginia treats pensions, retirement account withdrawals or other income. Those follow separate provisions of state tax law.
