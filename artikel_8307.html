Trans-Catalina Trail Day 1: Surprisingly Green

Tanggal: 2026-11-03

In Spring 2026, I had the great experience of hiking the Trans Catalina Trail in Southern California. As the date nears to make reservations for 2027 on Catalina Island, I thought it might be helpful to finally write about my experience. My blog posts here are obviously a little delayed. I have included pro tips from my experience to help future TCT hikers have the best experience possible.  The TCT is a wonderful shoulder-season hike and I cannot recommend it enough. I hope you consider it for 2027!

I wake in Irvine, CA, at my husband’s friend’s house. They so kindly took us in and fed us incredible Indian food (truly, the best I’ve ever had). Unfortunately, what I thought yesterday was allergies appears to be a full-blown cold today. My throat is sore, my nose is running, and my lungs ache. Despite the symptoms, I am not easily deterred. I flew all the way down here from Seattle after a whirlwind week of performing (I’m a musician in my non-hiking life), and I WILL complete what I came here to do: hike the Trans-Catalina Trail.

I’ve had my eye on it for a while now. An off-season trail in such close proximity to close family? Constant ocean views? Something I can complete in 3-4 days? Sounds ideal. I haven’t planned it until now because of the logistical difficulties of traveling on a plane for such a short hike, but this year is different. I am excited to get a jumpstart on the hiking season. All of January and February I spent doing the training I would normally start in the spring. It’s nice to have a reason to get started, and my reason is just how difficult the TCT can be.

Elevation profile of the TCT courtesy of Catalina Island Conservancy.

The TCT is a roughly 40 mile trail across Catalina Island, one of the Channel Islands off of the coast of Southern California. The trail itself is a combination of single-track and dirt roads, leading sometimes to unforgivingly steep climbs and descents. It’s not easy, but established campgrounds with vault toilets and running water, unique animal species that live only on the island, and perpetual ocean views make it worth tackling.

The logistics can be tricky and expensive, but clear: take the ferry to Catalina Island (~$90 round trip), reserve campsites through the Catalina Island Conservancy ($44 per person per night), and reserve a free hiking permit for each day. I also needed to get myself to Southern California from Seattle, where I live.

I flew into the Orange County airport a day before my hike, the closest airport to Long Beach, CA. Ferries to Avalon, the traditional starting point of the TCT, depart from Long Beach, San Pedro, and Dana Point (near San Diego). The trail ends in Two Harbors, though, which only offers ferry service to San Pedro.

The trail is known for steep climbs, minimal tree cover, the potential for high temperatures and extreme weather, and little natural water. What I’m most excited about, though, is the animal species endemic to Catalina. There are a few species that exist only on Catalina or the Channel Islands, like the Catalina Fox and the Catalina California Quail. The fox can be abnormally friendly with humans. There are bear boxes at every campsite not for bears (there aren’t any on island), but for the foxes. Unique wildlife sightings are one of my favorite parts of hiking, so I can’t wait to get started.

The ideal time of the year for the Trans-Catalina Trail is spring or fall. Summer gets prohibitively warm and winter can get prohibitively wet. I’m aiming for early in the hiking season. Living in the beautiful Pacific Northwest has made me extremely averse to heat. Early-to-mid-March seems safest, although there is always a risk of rain this early. The trails can be impossible to hike when wet, due to sticky “peanut butter” mud. I just hope to avoid the worst of rain or heat.

My husband, Alex, joins me in line for the 9:50 am Catalina Express. The boat is called the “Jet Cat Express” which, as a cat lover, I take to be a good sign. Alex was supposed to join me on this hike, but badly sprained his ankle two months ago. He couldn’t walk normally for a few weeks and still wears a brace, so this trip became a solo endeavor for me.

The Jet Cat Express ferry to Avalon on Catalina Island.

As I board the ferry, someone directs me to a luggage room, where they ask me to leave my backpack. I make sure everything is fully secured and wedge it into a corner. It feels weird to leave it out of sight. I go to find a seat, a harder prospect than anticipated. The boat is large but crowded, nearly every seat filled.

I grab snacks and marvel at just how bouncy such a large boat can be. Sitting and watching the waves, I feel a flutter of excitement for the first time. The stress of traveling for the past week prevented me from thinking much about this trek, but now I feel eager to start. Unfortunately, I also feel a constant tickle in my throat. The timing couldn’t be worse. I hope this doesn’t become something more.

The first view of Avalon as I leave the ferry.

After about an hour on the ferry, we disembark in Avalon, the largest and most touristy city on the island. Most people are just here for a day trip, lunching and shopping in Avalon before taking the afternoon ferry back. Up ahead of me, though, I see two people with large packs.

I catch up and introduce myself, and meet who I will refer to as the Isabellas – Isabel and Bella, friends hiking the trail together. We try to stop at the Island Conservancy, which manages all the trails and permits, but it already closed for the day. Instead, we make our way to Chet’s Hardware to buy fuel. Fuel canisters are not allowed aboard the ferries, so the hardware store has a monopoly on fuel sales. The sizes available are larger and largest, unfortunate since the trail is relatively short. I buy the smallest one I can find and stuff it into my pack.

I stop at the local grocery store on the way through Avalon for a snack before making my way to the official trailhead at the end of town.

Pre-hike lunch of champions? Yes, I also snuck in Nerds Clusters.

Every step from the ferry to the trailhead is uphill, and then the trail winds ever more steeply to the top of the first large climb. There is a campground on the edge of town, right by the trailhead — Hermit Gulch.

In the middle of the day this early in the season, the campground is completely empty. I stop to use one of the bathrooms, but they are locked. That is, the row of bathrooms facing the road are locked. There is another row on the opposite side, completely open. Jackpot!

As I wash my hands, I notice a nearby trash can completely, well, trashed. I wonder if this is the first evidence of the ever-hungry Catalina fox.

1.7 mi from the ferry, I reach the official trailhead for the Trans Catalina Trail. From here, the climbing truly begins, and I get my first real taste of the ups and downs of this hike.

The grade steepens immediately. This first ascent climbs almost 1300 ft in just over 1.5 miles. Not the steepest climb I’ve faced on a long trail, but nothing to sneeze at. Thankfully, the climb is accompanied by beautiful wildflowers on both sides of the trail.

I am barely 2 miles into this hike, but already so surprised by how lush and green this island is. I was expecting it to be desert — dry, barren, and yellow. Instead, Catalina teems with life around every bend. I was not expecting to like it as much I already do.

Unfortunately, I keep getting distracted by deep aching in my chest. The cold that started yesterday is still obnoxiously present. My lungs absolutely do not want to hike today, and they are pointedly letting me know. Thankfully, the weather today is surprisingly cool and cloudy. Not cool by Seattle standards, but certainly pleasant enough to help ease the climb.

Water finds a way. Ruts and erosion are a particular theme on the TCT.

Near the top, the views truly open up. I see Avalon below, the sparkling blue of the Pacific Ocean, and even Long Beach way off on the mainland. It’s hard to imagine getting sick of this.

A lovely semi-shaded bench awaits at the top, as well as informative signage warning visitors never to feed the foxes. Another sign warns about the bison that roam the island. Almost a century ago, a crew brought a herd of bison to Catalina to film a Western. When they finished, they simply left the herd on the island. The bison remain to this day, though their population is managed. I hope to see one, but from a distance — bison are notoriously unpredictable and prone to anger. I don’t want to end up on the wrong side of that.

Looking ahead, I can already tell it will be easy to see bison coming. From this high point, I can see that tree cover only minimally covers the island. The foliage is mostly brushy and low, allowing for incredible views and negligible shade.

The trail joins onto a dirt road for a few miles, circling a crest before dropping down to another single-track trail. I’m grateful the weather is cool today. This road walk would be miserable in full sun.

Another view of Avalon before I hike too far to see it anymore.

Near the halfway point of the day, I encounter a small pond and parking area with picnic tables, vault toilets, and shade. I stop and have a small break with some Babybel cheese and Honey Stingers chews. At the next table, a group of young gentlemen wave hello. They also plan to hike to Black Jack Campground tonight, so I know I will see them later. As I hike out, I see the Isabellas again, who also have permits to camp there. I’m glad not to be alone.

The remaining five miles of the hike show clearly what to expect from the TCT: steep up, steep down, join the road, leave the road. This repeats over and over. The trail is anything but rolling, but still unquestionably lovely. I can’t believe just how green it is. I was not expecting anything in Southern California to be so lush, even an island, but the winter rains clearly were abundant.

In the midst of ups and downs and admiring the green, I come across the most surprising trail find yet: a water fountain in the middle of nowhere.

A water fountain? In the desert? Yes, please.

The TCT is a notably dry hike. All the campgrounds (except Parsons Landing) have water taps, but otherwise the only reliable way to get water is in the two towns or at the small airport. So, to see a water fountain is incredible. I take a sip and grin as I hike off.

Some of the few trees I’ve seen today. I love this little walkway.

By 6 PM, I’m on the final stretch of trail up to Black Jack Campground. Unfortunately, this last climb is brutally steep, and all I can feel is the continued aching in my lungs. I overdid it today. Obviously, I should have rested my sick lungs, but I had the permits and traveled all the way here. I had to hike. In fact, it never occurred to me not to hike today.

I stop to breathe at an overlook, trying to forget the aching warmth in my lungs. Almost there.

Finally, I crest a hill and through the trees ahead, I see a small building. A vault toilet! I made it to camp!

Black Jack Campground is nestled within these trees.

I reserved Campsite #1 back on January 1 when permits opened, and it is the first one I pass as I hike into camp. My lungs are grateful to drop my pack. Campsite 1 is a little removed from the others, and a bit of a hike to the restroom, but I love the beautiful tree that serves as the campsite’s focal point.

Happy little camp, complete with fox box.

The water tap is in Campsite 2, next to my camp. The group of college-aged fellows in that campsite are a little confused by the “potable water” sign on the tap, thinking it’s meant only to wash pots with. (Ah, to be young and learning the world again.) The Bellas get them set right and we all gather our water.

Water and toilets are already a major bonus of the TCT compared to some backpacking I have done. Another is the picnic table. The ability to lay out my things and sit comfortably while I eat? Absolute luxury.

I stash everything I can into the bear box (which I will call a “fox” box from now on). No signs of foxes yet, but I know how common it is to see them. I crawl into my tent feeling hopeful for both a future sighting and a great hike. Despite my aching lungs, hiking alone today made me feel like myself. Backpacking alone has done that for me these past few years. No one to answer to but me, no one to blame but me — just freedom.

Today was a perfect first day on the Trans Catalina Trail, despite my chest congestion. As I lay in my tent, writing in my trail journal, I can’t help thinking that I’ve never hiked anywhere like Catalina. The landscape is already one-of-a-kind, the views are nonstop, and the campsites are luxurious by backcountry standards. Today was a hard day, but tomorrow should be lighter and even more beautiful — and I cannot wait.

Miles Hiked – 11 Elevation Gain/Loss –  3200 feet/1850 feet Start/End – Started in Avalon, ended at Black Jack Campground Lowlight – Hiking with a cold Highlight – The water fountain in the middle of nowhere, and all the green! Bison count – 0 Fox count – 0 View count – Countless (already!)
