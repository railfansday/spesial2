A 457(b) plan is an employer-sponsored retirement savings account available to state and local government employees and certain nonprofit (tax-exempt) organizations. It operates similarly to a 401(k) or 403(b) — you defer a portion of your salary before taxes, your money grows tax-deferred, and you pay income tax when you withdraw.

But here is where the 457(b) separates itself from every other retirement account: there is no 10% early withdrawal penalty. None. Zero. Once you leave your employer — whether you retire at 35 or 65 — you can access your 457(b) funds immediately and pay only ordinary income tax. No penalty, no waiting until age 59 and a half, no complicated Roth conversion ladders.

This single feature makes the 457(b) the most powerful account for anyone pursuing financial independence through government employment. Teachers, firefighters, police officers, state university employees, city workers, and county staff all potentially have access to this account. If you have a 457(b) available and you are not maxing it out, you are leaving the best early-retirement tool on the table.

The plan comes in two flavors: governmental 457(b) plans and non-governmental (tax-exempt) 457(b) plans. The differences are significant and affect rollover options, creditor protection, and how your money is held.

Every other retirement account — 401(k), 403(b), Traditional IRA — charges a 10% penalty if you withdraw before age 59 and a half (with limited exceptions). The 457(b) has no such penalty. The moment you separate from your employer, your entire balance becomes accessible. For a government employee pursuing FIRE at age 40, this means immediate access to potentially hundreds of thousands of dollars without the penalty, without a Roth conversion ladder, and without SEPP/72(t) calculations. No other account offers this.

The 2026 457(b) contribution limit is $24,500 — the same as 401(k) and 403(b) limits. If you are age 50 or older, you can contribute an additional $7,500 catch-up, bringing your total to $31,000.

But the real power move is the double-dip strategy. The 457(b) has its own separate contribution limit from the 403(b). If your employer offers both plans — and many government and university employers do — you can max out both accounts in the same year. That means $24,500 into your 457(b) plus $24,500 into your 403(b) equals $47,000 per year in tax-deferred savings, not counting employer matches or catch-up contributions.

For a dual-income household where both partners have access to 457(b) and 403(b) plans, the combined tax-deferred savings potential exceeds $94,000 per year. Add HSA contributions, Roth IRAs, and any employer matches, and a government-employed couple can shelter well over $100,000 annually from taxes.

The 457(b) offers a unique catch-up rule that no other retirement plan has. In the three years before your plan's "normal retirement age" (typically 65 or 70, defined by your employer), you can contribute up to double the regular limit — that is $47,000 in 2026. This provision lets late starters make up for years of lower contributions. You cannot use both the age-50 catch-up and the 3-year catch-up in the same year — you must choose whichever is larger.

This provision requires careful planning because the normal retirement age varies by employer and plan document. Review your specific plan to confirm your eligible catch-up window.

This distinction matters enormously. Governmental 457(b) balances can be rolled into a Traditional IRA, 401(k), 403(b), or another governmental 457(b) after you separate from service. This gives you full flexibility to consolidate accounts, access better investment options, and implement tax-efficient withdrawal strategies in retirement.

Non-governmental 457(b) plans — those offered by tax-exempt nonprofits like hospitals, charities, and private universities — cannot be rolled into an IRA or any other retirement plan type. The balance can only be rolled to another non-governmental 457(b). This limitation is critical to understand before relying on a non-governmental 457(b) as a cornerstone of your retirement strategy.

Even more concerning: non-governmental 457(b) assets are held as general assets of the employer, meaning they are technically subject to the employer's creditors. If your nonprofit employer faces financial trouble, your deferred compensation could be at risk. Governmental plans hold assets in trust for the benefit of employees, providing the same creditor protection as a 401(k).

Most governmental 457(b) plans offer a menu of mutual funds, target-date funds, and sometimes a self-directed brokerage window. The quality of investment options varies dramatically between plans. Look for low-cost index fund options — total stock market, S&P 500, total international, and total bond market funds with expense ratios below 0.10% are the gold standard.

If your plan's investment options are poor — loaded with high-fee actively managed funds — contribute enough to capture any match, then prioritize your 403(b) or IRA if those have better options. After separation, roll the 457(b) balance to a low-cost IRA provider like Vanguard or Fidelity.

A step-by-step plan for government employees pursuing FIRE.

Check with your HR department whether your 457(b) is a governmental plan (state/local government employer) or a non-governmental plan (tax-exempt nonprofit). This determines your rollover options and creditor protection.

Pro tip: If you have a non-governmental 457(b), consider limiting contributions to what you can access penalty-free and prioritizing other accounts for the rest.

Many government and university employers offer both a 457(b) and a 403(b). If both are available, you can contribute the maximum to each — doubling your tax-deferred savings to $47,000+ per year.

Contribute the full $24,500 for 2026 ($31,000 if 50+). Set up automatic payroll deductions and increase them annually until you reach the maximum.

Pro tip: Front-load contributions early in the year if your employer allows it — more time in the market beats dollar-cost averaging on average.

Choose the lowest-cost index funds available in your plan. A simple two-fund or three-fund portfolio (total US stock market + total international + total bond) keeps fees low and diversification high.

When you reach your FI number, your 457(b) balance is immediately accessible after separation from service with no penalty. Plan your withdrawal rate alongside any other income sources (Roth IRA, taxable brokerage, rental income) to minimize your tax bracket.

Pro tip: Consider partial withdrawals from the 457(b) to fill lower tax brackets while doing Roth conversions on your 403(b) or Traditional IRA balances.
