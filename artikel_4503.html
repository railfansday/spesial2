Partners in a romantic relationship rely on each other to be truthful. It can be both unnerving and stressful to wonder if your partner isn’t holding to this standard. Kim and Chris are in a stable long-term monogamous relationship, but lately Kim has begun to wonder if Chris means what he says. In the latest incident, an important one, Chris promised that they would take their relationship one step further toward commitment. But so far, nothing has happened. What makes this particularly odd is that Chris has seemed to be honest and authentic, not just in the relationship, but in general. Maybe it’s all been a fraud, though.

Because couples in committed relationships share so many aspects not only of their lives but of their vulnerabilities, hopes, and aspirations, it is particularly important for the relationship’s health that they don’t lie to each other. As noted in new research by the University of Copenhagen’s Rachele Mazzini and colleagues (2026), although honesty is a fundamental “relationship ‘rule,’” “dishonesty occurs regularly in romantic relationships.”

Dishonesty can take the form of telling falsehoods, but it can also show up as keeping secrets. Once the dishonesty starts, in whatever form, it can spiral out of control. But all of this begs the question of why someone would lie in the first place. After all, if you love someone, what would lead you to disguise the truth? Mazzini et al. believe that the motives behind dishonesty are as important as the behavior itself. Or, the person may just be an inveterate liar who does so for no particular reason at all.

Previous studies have examined different types of lies, motives for lying, and personality traits associated with lying, but none have addressed directly the question of how to measure lying in relationships. The U. Copenhagen team decided to address this very problem.

Mazzini and colleagues began their quest by asking preliminary samples of online participants to complete a 47-item version of what became, as tested in the final sample, a 10-item Dishonesty in Intimate Relationships Scale (DIRS). They administered the DIRS two times, one week apart, in this final sample of nearly 900 participants. This allowed them to analyze its value in predicting relationship outcomes above and beyond other measures of deception. The relationship outcomes included how well a couple was functioning in terms of trust, ambivalence, closeness, and authenticity.

Here are the 10 items on the DIRS; each should be rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree); an (R) indicates the item is reverse-scored:

Items 1 to 6 fall into the Deception factor and 7 to 10 into the Infidelity factor.

Based on mean scores the authors report in supplemental materials, their final online sample tended toward the honesty end of both subscales. The least likely to be endorsed was item 7, and the highest were items 2 and 3. Most items ranged between 2 and just about 2.5.

Correlation between DIRS subscales and the other relationship measures showed that all high DIRS scores bode poorly for relationship quality. Furthermore, based on the one-week separation between the two times of testing, it appeared that the DIRS also went above and beyond other deception measures in predictive value.

There were some connections between DIRS scores and personality measures of dishonesty, but this makes sense. A liar will be a liar, no matter what, which will spill over into a close relationship. As stated more formally by the authors, “domain-specific dishonesty in relational contexts is not fully separable from broader dispositions.”

So far, you may have learned about how much you tend to lie in your relationship compared to those in the online sample, but what about measuring your partner? If, like Kim, you suspect something is going on, you probably won’t want to hand this scale over to your partner and demand completion.

Instead, you can use the DIRS as an inner check as you navigate the ins and outs of your daily experiences with your partner. Do you notice that details are sometimes eliminated from stories your partner tells you? Like Kim, does it seem that your partner says things just to please you when it comes to taking your relationship to the next level? A promise-based white lie can be a true danger signal, especially when you think the lie is meant to protect your feelings.

You might also notice something off in the Infidelity domain. People in the Mazzini et al. study were unlikely to admit to cheating, and they also tended not to say they would become emotionally involved with someone else. But you may notice your partner making excessively complimentary remarks about someone else if not directly flirting at social gatherings (while ignoring you). In-jokes, winking, and more than a few "friendly" lunch dates can be another clue.

To sum up, it can be helpful to have a short measurement guide in your back pocket to check whether your partner truly has your best interests at heart. If something seems “off” from time to time, the DIRS can provide a good reality check. Relationship fulfillment requires honesty, and when it’s not there, it may be time to figure out how to get back on track.
