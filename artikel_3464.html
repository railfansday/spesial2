The 10 Worst Horror Sequels in Film History

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Some horror sequels can instill dread within the hearts of audiences; other times they're just dreadful to have to sit through. Horror has a tremendous history with sequels that far exceeded expectations. Unfortunately, that's not the case for most of them. Sequels haven't had the best reputation throughout the years, as many of them have been considered inferior to their predecessors. While, yes, there have been plenty of notable exceptions, it seems horror has a track record for the most egregious continuations in cinema history.

As horror fans, we all know there are some notable stinkers out there. They're unavoidable for anyone who is a true enthusiast who enjoys everything about this genre. One thing is for sure, the worst of the worst really seem to be sequels of popular franchises. Whether from slashers, zombies, or the supernatural, no matter the subgenre, we have a plethora of atrocious horror sequels that most of us would like to forget. But let's dig up the most iconic from the trash bin and address the ten worst sequels in horror movie history. Get ready for the most horrifyingly mediocre experience you'll ever have when watching a movie.

Even when Scream 3 was a bit divisive, most of us could agree for a while at least that the Scream franchise was without flaws. Despite not always being the most polished series, each installment was at least entertaining, if not better, than most of the horror sludge that comes out these days. That was until, sadly, Scream 7 in early 2026, which became arguably the worst, most tiring sequel in recent horror history. Despite seeing the return of franchise co-creator Kevin Williamson and leading star Neve Campbell after her controversial departure in the previous film, Scream 7 was an utter mess of poorly executed ideas, flat characters, tired clichés, abysmal killer reveals, and a lack of an identity similar to its predecessors.

Though Campbell gives it her all, she's mixed in with a bunch of bland characters who are mostly just redshirts, being there to get killed and leave no impact whatsoever. The final reveal of the killers, Jessica (Anna Camp) and Marco (Ethan Embry), is not only incredibly underwhelming, since we barely even knew who they were, but it was added as a lame "gotcha" moment for audiences who still believed Stu (Mathew Lillard) was returning. Along with it being considered the rushed seventh film in a franchise already running out of steam, and the result of the controversial firing of the series' leading lady, Melissa Barrera, Scream 7 became a calamity that has sunk this once beloved franchise towards an unknown future.

Director Dan O'Bannon's 1985 zombie masterpiece The Return of the Living Dead was a kick-ass thrill ride that defined '80s horror-comedy dominance. A beloved classic that was darkly hilarious, bone-chillingly suspenseful, and unquestionably a cheesy, fun product of its time, The Return of the Living Dead was the zombie film to watch in the 1980s. So, of course, its smashing box office success led to the creation of a franchise, but boy, it's seriously shocking how some horror franchises can immediately tank so quickly.

To put it simply, 1988's Return of the Living Dead II was an utter train wreck that flopped immediately upon release. It's an unamusing, dull, and painfully unfocused sequel that limps its way by the second half. The jokes are flat and uninspired, the characters aren't as fun as the original, and the practical effects and makeup look cheap and lazy, despite the fact that this had a larger budget than the first film. It lacked the creative passion and energy of its predecessor, and it has since become a symbol for how to quickly lose a franchise's momentum. After this wreck, The Return of the Living Dead franchise, if we can even call it that at this point, never came close to honor the original.

By the time the mid 1990s rolled around, the slasher subgenre was well and truly in a massive decline. Before 1996's Scream saved it from the brink, franchises that were once unstoppable juggernauts like Nightmare on Elm Street and Halloween were quickly running out of steam, and the whole horror genre itself was just becoming embarrassed by one lousy slasher sequel after the next. No film can truly symbolize the lowest of this nosedive more than Jason Goes to Hell: The Final Friday, the most insulting installment in the once great Friday the 13th franchise.

This legendary misfire struck the final nail in the coffin for this beloved horror franchise. It's a bizarre, nonsensical, incoherent mess that doesn't even feel like a Friday the 13th film. Jason Voorhees (Kane Hodder) was marketed as having one last big hurrah, but he was killed in an explosive opener, then spent the rest of the film possessing people to target his previously unmentioned bloodline. In other words, it was a painful head-scratcher that felt like it was written by screenwriters who had never watched this series before. It was a disappointment to fans who already felt beat after the disappointing release of Jason Takes Manhattan. Overall, it symbolized slashers at their lowest point, and a franchise that never truly recovered afterward.

Horror sequels just didn't work in the 1990s. Aside from Jason Goes To Hell, another case in point is 1995's Texas Chainsaw Massacre: The Next Generation, arguably the weirdest, most nonsensical horror sequel ever made. The fourth installment in The Texas Chainsaw Massacre franchise, this wacky head-scratcher is anything but scary. In fact, it's a downright bonkers, unintentionally hilarious laugh fest that's full of incoherent dialogue, unapologetically over-the-top performances, and a plot so off its rocker that it includes conspiracy theories and the Illuminati.

The franchise really went downhill after Tobe Hooper's legendary 1974 original The Texas Chainsaw Massacre redefined the slasher subgenre, and this film certainly represents its lowest point. Gone is the suspense and gritty violence of the original; instead, this has nothing but bad pacing, no atmosphere, and barely any real scares. The Next Generation seems like a fever dream rather than a proper sequel. It's a sequel so out of place and different than the others in the franchise that it might as well be its own separate film. While some audiences can get a big laugh out of how terrible and insane this story and its characters are, The Next Generation is definitely not for fans looking for a proper Texas Chainsaw Massacre sequel.

If there's one horror film that seriously did not need a sequel, it's American Psycho. Mary Harron's 2000 psychological dark comedy was an instant horror masterpiece, a near-perfect film that struck the right mix of twisted, mind-bending tension, slasher terror, and humorous satire. It didn't really leave much room for any spinoff or continuation, but sadly, this didn't stop the studio from releasing the absolute travesty that is 2002's American Psycho 2.

This forgettable mess was a dud upon arrival. It is hardly ever mentioned these days, even when discussing the worst of the worst when it comes to horror sequels. The plot is flimsy and barely connects to the first film. The characters are boring and nowhere near as quotable as the ones from the first movie. And, while Mila Kunis did her best with what little she was given here, there was no surpassing Christian Bale's career-defining powerhouse performance as the titular psycho Patrick Bateman. This isn't even a proper sequel and does nothing to honor the greatness of Harron's original. It just feels like a massive cash grab, except no one bought it.

Four franchises, four completely different ways of getting underneath your skin. This is the one built out of your particular fear, why it works so well on you, and what it would leave behind.

Your nightmare does not wait outside the door. It waits for you to close your eyes, which you are going to have to do eventually, because nobody has ever won that particular argument.

Your nightmare is not really the doll. It is the face of the person you tell, and the way they decide, very kindly, that you have imagined all of it.

Your nightmare has your number, knows your name, and has almost certainly been inside your house before. The worst part is that you already know them.

Your nightmare is not really about you. It is about the people asleep down the hall, and the feeling that whatever is here is not interested in leaving.

The Halloween franchise is the roller-coaster ride of the slasher subgenre. It's got the most extreme highs, but also the most depressing lows, and sometimes its lows were too hard to get out of. This series has seen plenty of notable stinkers, like Halloween 5: The Revenge of Michael Myers or even the most recent disappointment, Halloween Ends. But none come close to the embarrassing legacy of 2002's Halloween: Resurrection.

Halloween: Resurrection is by all means one of the worst horror movies, period. It's not scary, barely makes any sense, is full of cheap jump scares, and littered with weak characters, if we can even call some of them that. Honestly, if you don't feel your own intelligence getting insulted by the halfway mark, then rapper Busta Rhymes' character's awkward karate fight scene with Michael in the climax of the film certainly will. Overall, this is just a nightmare, and not in a thrilling way. While the Halloween franchise has bounced back and forth over the years, Halloween Resurrection will always be a reminder of its dark history.

No horror series has taken a more immediate nosedive after the first film than the unnecessary Jaws franchise. Seriously, no one really wanted this to be a franchise in the first place. Steven Spielberg's 1975 smash hit was the classic that practically built the summer blockbuster, becoming a box office juggernaut and timelessly praised by fans. None of that success or fame carried over to the sequels, nor especially to the franchise's killer: Jaws: The Revenge.

Jaws: The Revenge is perhaps the biggest head-scratching, frustrated mess of the horror genre of the 20th century. It's quite remarkable how nothing goes right throughout this story. The spark of the original film is gone, along with compelling characters and thrilling suspense. In its place is incoherent nonsense, bland characters, bad casting decisions, and a ridiculous plot that tries to have us think a great white shark can harbor grudges against an entire family. Truly, this is a spectacular mess that immediately sank to the bottom of the ocean.

A sequel made solely to cash in on the monumental success of a beloved classic never works out. Sometimes they can be downright insulting to the original. That's the best way to describe Exorcist II: The Heretic, the egregious, unnecessary sequel to the much-revered 1973 classic The Exorcist. How do you mess up continuing one of the scariest and best horror movies of the 20th century? Well, take away the suspense, the tone, and literally anything resembling a coherent story.

The Heretic is pure nonsense, a rambling bore-fest that's nothing like its predecessor. It's not scary, lacks tension, and wastes the talents of its acclaimed cast. Even with Linda Blair and Max von Sydow returning from the first movie with more screen time, they can't save their characters from being somehow less complex as before. The Heretic has everything going wrong from beginning to end, and it started The Exorcist franchise on a rocky start that still hasn't fully recovered much from.

As a whole, the I Know What You Did Last Summer franchise isn't exactly the best horror franchise. It's not to everyone's liking. Even the first film is only enjoyable so long as you look past its unoriginal Scream-inspired premise and obnoxious characters. For a series that doesn't really have any high standards, you'd think it couldn't sink so low, but it did. In 2006, the direct-to-video third film, I Always Know What You Did Last Summer, was released to instant criticism and fan backlash.

Frankly, I Always Know What You Did Last Summer is a crime against slashers. It's an insulting bore of a film, lacking stakes, likable characters, and decent writing. It has absolutely nothing to do with the previous two films, outside featuring the fisherman killer villain, and even that didn't make any sense, as he appeared here in a small town in colorado slaying teens who never really wronged him. It's an obnoxious cash grab that failed spectacularly as a sequel. It's honestly one of the worst slasher movies of all time.

We all knew this was coming. The one and only Troll 2 has earned its place in cinema for all the hilariously wrong reasons. It truly is one of the worst movies ever made, a complete dumpster fire that is poorly written, sloppily edited, incompetently shot, and terribly acted. And yet, despite all of its terribleness, it's considered one of the most enjoyable so-bad-it's-good movies of all time.

Most fans of this film know by now that it's not an actual sequel to 1986's Troll. It was running under the title Goblins, but the studio slapped Troll II on it for marketing reasons, which only further sparked its infamy. There aren't even any trolls in it! But that's the least of the issues this has, as it's full of the most unintentionally hilarious comedy and quotable bad dialogue you'll ever hear. It's a terrible mess, but a lovable one at that, and proudly stands here as the worst horror sequel in film history.
