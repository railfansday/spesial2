Lawyer’s book tells story of her work representing serial killer John Wayne Gacy

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

It was 1993, and serial killer John Wayne Gacy was on death row. He wanted to hire a First Amendment lawyer to help him fight to keep the profits from the artwork he’d been making while in Illinois’ Menard Correctional Center.

Chicago-based lawyer Karen Conti and Greg Adamski, her husband and law partner, agreed to visit Gacy and talk about representing him. Conti admits she was curious about the man who killed at least 33 men and boys and buried most of them under his house in suburban Chicago.

“People think of these kinds of killers as being monsters, but they don’t always present themselves that way,” Conti says. “He was a bragger and a know-it-all, but he seemed very normal until you thought about what he was saying. He was affable, and he could be charming.”

Conti and Adamski, who died in 2011, ended up resolving the First Amendment issues with a “quick negotiation” and then agreed to join Gacy’s death penalty defense team. Conti long had opposed the death penalty.

Her book on the experience, “Killing Time with John Wayne Gacy: Defending America’s Most Evil Serious Killer on Death Row,” was published in 2024, 30 years after Gacy was executed by lethal injection on May 10, 1994.

“We were never trying to get him out of prison,” Conti says. “Our only thought was to have the execution stayed or stopped.”

In the book, she describes what it’s like to handle the negative publicity that comes with a high-profile case. In addition, she recounts what she learned from Gacy during her conversations with him.

“My book is really just about a young lawyer getting involved in something way over your head and how you have to find humanity in something inhumane,” she says.

Because Gacy was a “horrible sexual predator,” some people were angry Conti represented him, thinking she condoned his behavior, she says.

Gacy was known as being a friendly person who hosted summer parties and dressed as a clown for charity fundraisers and local parties. At the same time, he lured male victims to his house, then raped and murdered them, usually by strangulation or asphyxiation.

In 1978 he was arrested, and a jury convicted him of 33 murders in 1980.

During the eight months or so in which she helped file appeals on his behalf, Conti visited Gacy in prison and talked to him almost every day on the phone.

Gacy, Conti says, had a sense of humor and was “very engaging.” He asked a lot of personal questions and later would draw upon them to manipulate the other person.

“He wanted to make you feel intimidated or guilty to get what he wanted,” she adds.

During one visit, Conti noticed that Gacy had a scrapbook about the victims that he’d compiled. He explained to Conti that he’d wanted to know who “the young men were” who were under his house.

Gacy treated Conti differently than his other lawyers, she says, because she was the only woman on his team.

“He really liked women,” Conti says. “He was oppositional with men, but with me, he was softer.”

Because he was more cooperative with Conti she was tasked with the “hard communications,” including telling him that the U.S. Supreme Court had denied his last-ditch appeal.

“He didn’t seem that upset,” she says. “I think John Gacy was in denial his whole life about who he was and what he did. He was able to compartmentalize. That helped him deny what he knew was coming.”

When asked why it took so long for her to write the book, Conti says that for years “she was very tired of the whole thing,” as representing Gacy had drained her of energy and money. But, she says, she always knew she had a story to tell.

During the Covid pandemic, she had a little more time on her hands, and she started writing about Gacy.

“It came pretty easily to me,” Conti says. By waiting to write the book, “I could look at the experience in hindsight.”
