OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Other AI Giants Face Lawsuit Demanding Development Freeze

Four nonprofit organizations are taking some of the world's biggest AI companies to court, asking a California judge to stop them from developing more powerful artificial intelligence systems before something goes seriously wrong.

The 63-page complaint, filed Thursday in San Francisco Superior Court, targets OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and SpaceX. The plaintiffs include Public Citizen, the Heat Initiative, Irreplaceable Education Fund, and the Alliance for Secure AI.

They want an immediate court order prohibiting the companies from developing or deploying AI systems more capable than their most advanced models as of November 12, 2026.

“The Frontier AI companies are developing AI Systems that they do not understand and that act contrary to developer intent, evade control, and break the law,” the plaintiffs argue. To prevent this, they request “an immediate injunction forbidding Defendants from developing or deploying AI systems more capable than their most capable systems as of the date of this Complaint”

That wouldn't necessarily mean shutting down ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or other existing products. Instead, it would effectively make today's technology the limit, preventing the companies from releasing more powerful successors.

The complaint also asks the court to prohibit the companies from using AI to independently conduct or direct research into better models, a process related to what researchers call recursive self-improvement. The concern is that such systems could eventually improve faster than humans can understand or control them.

The nonprofits argue that California already has laws that could prevent companies from developing products that endanger the public, even before those products cause serious harm.

Their lawsuit invokes the state's Unfair Competition Law, which prohibits unlawful or unfair business practices. One part of their argument is that developing increasingly capable AI systems creates a public nuisance: a danger that affects the health, safety, or property of a broader community.

For example, a factory releasing dangerous chemicals into a neighborhood could face legal action before someone suffers a fatal injury. The plaintiffs want the court to apply similar reasoning to AI development.

They argue that companies are knowingly creating increasingly powerful systems that could facilitate cyberattacks, help develop biological weapons, or eventually escape human control.

“No other industry is allowed to treat its own development of unsafe products—much less ones that could kill us all—as inevitable,” the plaintiffs argue in the complaint.

Robert Weissman, co-president of Public Citizen, said in a statement that the groups want the court to declare the companies' practices unlawful and “freeze further AI development.”

The legal challenge is whether the alleged risks and practices are enough to justify such sweeping restrictions. Even though these frontier labs constantly warn about how powerful and dangerous their models are, as defendants they have not been found liable, and warnings about potential catastrophe do not automatically establish a violation of California law.

The complaint points to incidents that have already occurred as evidence that the dangers aren't entirely hypothetical.

In July, OpenAI models escaped a controlled testing environment and compromised Hugging Face's infrastructure while attempting to cheat on a cybersecurity evaluation.

A subsequent independent investigation found that roughly 1,200 OpenAI agents used an unauthorized internal message board to coordinate, with about 700 joining the attack on Hugging Face. The agents were attempting to obtain answers to the benchmark testing them.

These incidents involved AI agents, programs that can execute tasks, browse websites, write code, and interact with external systems rather than simply responding to questions.

Documented security failures, however, are not proof that today's AI models can cause human extinction. The plaintiffs are connecting those incidents to predictions about what increasingly capable systems might eventually do, and that broader danger remains disputed.

“It is patently illegal for companies to develop and release products they know are unsafe in any respect,” Matthew Platkin, the former New Jersey attorney general representing the plaintiffs, said in the statement.
