The 'Culting' of Mental Health Diagnosis

The Culting of Brands sounds like one of those polemical books about ad industry manipulation. It’s not. It’s a how-to.

In the early 2000s, marketers were contending with savvy consumers wary of advertising tactics. One marketer, Douglas Atkin, realized that some brands had managed to engender cult-like devotion in their customers that transcended advertising and products.

He studied these "culty" brands and actual cults to figure out how this could work. Cults ensnare smart, capable (and ideally influential) people, he argued, by offering a couple of core human desires: meaning and belonging. Cults enclose a worldview around their followers that pre-answers questions, arming devotees with deeply felt shared perspectives.

His book therefore recommends that brands provide ideologies and identities for consumers. Chapters include “You’re Different, We’re Different,” “You Belong,” “This Is What We Believe,” and “We Love You.”

Another brand strategy book, Lovemarks, asserts that tapping into emotion and helping people to tell the story of their lives through brand values (where disentangling from them would unravel the very story of the self) is key to obtaining loyalty beyond reason from customers.

I write about ways marketing messages have been conflated with medical ones in mental health, and how the emotional affordances of social media platforms enable spaces where we don’t just identify with common characteristics of disorders; we feel them.

Social media is invested in invoking and enlisting user moods, providing and platforming interpretations of those moods, encapsulating them in memes (and sometimes even selling them as user data). Using social media can mimic states we consider overstimulation, executive dysfunction, or dissociation. When TikTok content tells us that “doomscrolling” TikTok is itself a symptom of a disorder and that a therapy app has a solution, we are seeing and feeling content as designed. But diagnostic brands in themselves aren’t new.

A strategy employed by marketers of psychopharmaceuticals has been to sell the diagnosis, not the product. Accomplished through extensive PR campaigns, sponsored conferences, lobbying, screening and awareness initiatives, and other tactics focused on securing an enduring market, the diagnosis becomes the brand (see Healy, 2008; Horwitz, 2021). Product demand follows.

Direct-to-consumer psychopharmaceutical ads often point to a singular explanation for a complex condition—usually a theorized mechanism of a drug's therapeutic effect (a logic roughly akin to assuming social anxiety is an alcohol deficiency). Medication is an important treatment tool for many. Its marketing, however, can discount social, environmental, or even other possible biomedical factors in mental health.

Before medications required association with specific disorders to obtain approval on the market and before there were hundreds of possible diagnoses to link them to, ads in medical journals advised doctors that societal pressures and the pains of living in themselves called for psychiatric medications.

As troubling as some of these ads were (like plainly stating there was no escape for "subservient" 1950s housewives), they did not tend to position anything inherently wrong with people or brains (and drugs like Miltown had a cult brand following beyond being "mother's little helpers"). Today, promotional materials and influencers are more likely to reduce suffering to serotonin, dopamine, or to use totalizing medical analogies (“like insulin for a diabetic”), shaping treatment decisions and self-understanding.

The ubiquitous and scientifically shaky insulin metaphor brings to mind another brand cult strategy, per Atkin, of “shared language.” Social media provides “therapy speak"—a fusion of pop psychology, therapeutic, and loosely applicable clinical terms that structure understanding of ourselves and others.

Clinically, our mental health diagnoses were primarily designed to describe symptoms, not explain them. On social media, however, rather than opening up questions, diagnoses are the answers.

Why does she have these mood swings? Because she’s bipolar. Of course they’re gaslighting me; they’re a narcissist. Why do I feel depressed? I have depression. It’s algorithmically tautological, self-reinforcing, and rests upon frameworks that draw more from PR than psychology or medicine.

Diagnoses and related terms become all-encompassing explanations. Psychiatrist and cult scholar Robert Jay Lifton described “loading the language" as a way of “reducing every issue in a complicated life to a single set of slogans that are said to embody the truth as a totality.” These terms can also work to shut down other ways of discussing, explaining, or exploring our traumas, challenges, capacities, and relationships.

Beyond this, social media diagnosis also provides consumer services and products, as well as the endless scroll of therapeutic information. Promoting a problem alongside a lifelong consumer solution is another marketing staple.

Though loyalty beyond reason is not to specific products in this case, it is to the idea of the diagnosis and the associated worldview its marketing has cultivated. This includes diagnostic lore that arises from social media users themselves; positioning the customer as "co-author" of the brand is textbook marketing strategy.

Who benefits most from these emotionally imbued consumer diagnostic identities? Why do people fervently adopt and "trauma bond" to them? Can they meaningfully improve actual mental health?

I currently research mental health and media. I also teach classes in public relations, advertising, and social media, so I think a lot about this. Something one of my own counseling professors would repeat in class has stuck with me throughout: “Honor the defenses.”

With this in mind, I look at the social media diagnostic identities I write about here and in my book as a way of adapting within increasing demands that include branding oneself in a digital economy. A number of mental health symptoms and associated biomedical components can be viewed as responses that in some way developed to cope with intense stressors.

Finding communities through which to share stories of struggle and healing is a strength of social media and, not incidentally, of social media marketing. The ongoing challenge is to recognize aspects of diagnostic “brand cults” and to continually strive to prioritize mental health over platforms and profits.
