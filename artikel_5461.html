Stocks after midterms: 10 findings on the S&P 500's record since 1950

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The stock market after midterm elections has a consistent record, with the S&P 500 rising in the 12 months after all 19 midterms since 1950

Midterm election years have a reputation on Wall Street for nervous, uneven trading. The year that follows has told a different story. The S&P 500 has finished higher in the 12 months after every U.S. midterm election since 1950, a run of 19 gains in 19 tries. That streak is the main subject of this slideshow, which looks at the stock market after midterm elections through a set of findings rather than a year-by-year table.

Midterms arrive in the second year of a presidential term. That stretch has often been one of the rougher periods of the four-year presidential cycle. Campaign season builds, and pre-election anxiety tends to rise with it. Choppy trading in the run-up to Election Day is common.

After the votes are counted, the picture has tended to change. The figures in the slides that follow measure what the index did over the 12 months after each election. They also measure the first six months of that window. Both averages are positive. Much of the 12-month gain has tended to arrive in the first half.

The record also has a range. The best post-midterm year produced a gain well above the average. The weakest finished only slightly above flat. Both ends of that range are covered, along with the years in which they occurred.

The slideshow also lays out three explanations for the pattern. One is relief: when an election ends, a layer of political and policy uncertainty lifts. Another is the market's historical preference for divided government, in which different parties hold the White House and Congress. Under that arrangement, sweeping legislative or tax overhauls become less likely. The third is the presidential cycle itself. Policy makers have tended to shift toward supporting the economy as a term heads into its third year.

None of this amounts to a forecast. A pattern that held across 19 elections describes what happened, not what will happen next. Past performance does not guarantee future returns, and an unbroken streak can end in any given year. The figures here record how the index behaved in the year after each midterm vote.

The S&P 500 finished higher in the 12 months after every U.S. midterm election held since 1950, according to GNG Research. That works out to a 100% hit rate across 19 elections.

The record measures direction, not size. None of the 12-month stretches that followed a midterm in that period closed with a loss.

That difference shapes how the streak should be read. One exceptional year can pull an average upward. It cannot add to a win rate. Each of the 19 elections had to produce its own gain for the record to hold.

The count also gives every midterm equal weight. A year with a large gain and a year that barely finished in positive territory each count as one win. On its own, the streak says nothing about how big any single gain was.

The measure is tied to a fixed window. A year counts as a gain only if the index ended the 12-month period above where it began. A decline partway through the window does not break the streak if the index recovered by the end of it.

An average can also be pulled up or down by one unusually large or small result. That effect is stronger when there are few observations. Midterms come only once every two years, so the record since 1950 is a limited sample.

The six months from November through April are historically one of the strongest stretches in the four-year presidential cycle, according to GNG Research. In a midterm year, that window opens in the same month voters go to the polls.

U.S. midterm elections are held on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November. That timing puts the start of this stretch close to the moment results become known. The window then runs through the winter and into the spring of the following year.

The window also spans two phases of the cycle. November and December are the last two months of year two, the midterm year. January through April are the first four months of year three. The handoff from one phase to the next happens inside the window itself.

The political calendar turns over during the same stretch. Members of Congress chosen in November are sworn in in early January. The legislature that takes office then is the one voters picked only weeks earlier.

Stocks have often risen after a midterm once the vote is over and the uncertainty around it is gone, according to GNG Research. The source identifies the removal of political and policy uncertainty as one reason the S&P 500 has tended to climb after results are in.

The pattern begins before the vote. GNG Research describes Wall Street as often anxious in the run-up to Election Day. Trading in that stretch can turn choppy.

A midterm decides which party controls each chamber of Congress. Until the results are known, investors have to weigh several possible outcomes at once. Each outcome carries different implications for policy. Once the count is complete, that open question closes.

GNG Research describes the buying that can follow as a relief rally. This explanation centers on the end of uncertainty itself rather than on any particular result. The source does not tie the relief effect to a specific election outcome.

The stock market has historically leaned toward divided government, in which one party holds the White House and the other party controls Congress. GNG Research ties that preference to a single outcome: split control makes sweeping legislation less likely to pass.

The reasoning centers on policy risk. A party that runs both the executive branch and Congress has a clearer path to large changes. Divided control raises the bar for that kind of bill.

The mechanics of lawmaking explain why. A bill must pass both the House and the Senate before it reaches the president. The president can then sign it or veto it. Congress can override a veto only with a supermajority in both chambers. When the parties split those institutions, the side out of power has more than one place to stop a proposal.

The source names two kinds of change in particular. One is a major legislative overhaul. The other is a tax overhaul. Under the Constitution, revenue bills must originate in the House. Under split government, both kinds of overhaul become harder to push through. For investors, fewer sweeping changes means fewer new rules and tax terms to build into business plans and stock prices.

Midterm elections decide the makeup of Congress for the second half of a presidential term. That makes each vote a direct test of whether the federal government ends up unified or split for the next two years. A midterm can turn a unified government into a divided one. It can also leave the existing balance in place.

The preference is a historical tendency, not a rule. GNG Research presents it as one of three explanations for post-midterm gains. It does not say how much of the post-midterm record divided government accounts for. It also does not tie the pattern to any specific election year or party, or compare returns under unified and split control.
