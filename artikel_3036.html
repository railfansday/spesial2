If a Bear Market Is Coming, History Says This Is the Safest Investing Move You Can Make

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Bear markets are a normal part of investing; your safest path through a downturn is sticking to your long-term plan.

The history of the stock market is very clear: if you invest long enough, you will eventually face a bear market. How you handle a deep and prolonged downturn can have a material impact on your long-term success as an investor. Don't wait for a bear market to determine your safest course of action; prepare in advance. What should you do? The safest move is to avoid panicking, so you can stick to your long-term plan. Here's how I do that, and you can too.

I am a dividend investor with a value focus and buy only when I'm willing to hold a stock for the long term. The truth is, I look at a lot of stocks but buy very few. My approach starts by considering companies with long histories of consistently increasing their dividends. I believe this highlights companies that are well-run and prioritize returning value to shareholders over time.

From there, I look for companies that have historically high dividend yields. This is my first indication of value, but I normally check traditional value metrics, including price-to-sales, price-to-earnings, and price-to-book ratios, to support this assessment. If a stock has passed these two tests, I then do a deep dive into the business. I'll pass if I don't like the business, preferring companies that I believe have resilient businesses and attractive long-term growth prospects.

Some of the stocks in my portfolio include Realty Income (O -0.46%), Hershey (HSY +1.19%), and Procter & Gamble (PG +1.90%). A company I've decided to pass on, multiple times, is Altria (MO +0.33%). The first three are slow-and-steady businesses that have continued to grow over the long term, while Altria's most important business, selling cigarettes, has been in decline for years. For example, it sold 29.4 billion cigarettes in the first half of 2026, down from 47.3 billion in the first half of 2021, a drop of nearly 38%.

The key point here is that owning Altria would leave me worried about the business's sustainability. I don't have that concern with Realty Income, P&G, or Hershey. That's not to suggest these companies won't face their own headwinds, but I believe they can be overcome. I'm not convinced that Altria can overcome the ongoing decline in cigarette volumes.

When a bear market arrives, and my nerves are on edge, I'm not going to suddenly wonder if Realty Income, P&G, or Hershey can survive. Owning Altria would mean entering that downturn already worried about the company, with the bear market compounding my fear. The chance that I would panic and sell would increase materially.

And by owning only dividend companies I am confident in over the long term, I can shift my focus from volatile stock prices to dividends. That, in turn, lets me key my emotions to a financial metric that tends to be highly consistent over time. So long as my dividend checks keep coming in, I can avoid getting caught up in the heightened emotions that will pervade Wall Street during a deep market downturn.

Here's the thing: I know the history of the S&P 500 index (^GSPC -1.92%). After every bear market in history, the stock market has gone on to hit new all-time highs. So even buying just before a bear market isn't terrible if you just avoid panic selling and hold for the long term. So the safest thing you can do is keep calm, or at least as calm as possible, and stick to your long-term investment plan.

You just have to make sure your long-term investment plan is constructed in such a way that it can allow you to sidestep, as much as possible, the fear that will come with the next bear market. That's what my dividend-focused approach allowed me to do through the dot-com crash and the Great Recession. You need to choose an approach that is right for you, but whatever investment path you decide to go down, make sure to mentally stress-test it, ahead of time, against a bear market.
